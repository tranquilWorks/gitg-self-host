# 22.09 — Culture and institutional design

Trace what a group's ordinary systems reward, then test one authorized change to early problem reporting. Allow twenty minutes to map the fictional system and fifteen to review the next cycle. The task concerns mechanisms that make conduct easier or harder. A slogan, new form or lively discussion does not show that an institution's culture has changed.

## Look beyond the announced value

A stated norm describes what the group says should happen. An enacted norm is suggested by repeated responses: who gets praised, interrupted, included or excused. One incident can identify a hypothesis, but it cannot establish the whole culture. Ask which system could repeatedly produce the observed behavior and what contrary evidence would weaken that explanation.

Incentives include more than pay. Visibility, relief from awkward questions, access to interesting work and the leader's approval can all matter. A team that says “raise risks early” but celebrates only green status may make silence attractive. The person who reports a problem carries a visible burden while the person who hides it looks successful until the deadline. Do not conclude that every late problem was deliberately concealed; inspect information, expectations and capacity.

Rituals organize attention. A brief pre-deadline check can invite a missing input before it becomes a failure, but only if someone can act on it. A form with no decision owner merely records blockage. Design an information route from the person who sees the issue to someone with authority to resolve it, then back to the reporter. State the response time and what to do if the owner cannot answer.

Selection and progression teach what the institution values. Who is chosen to lead a visible task? What evidence matters for the next role or promotion? If only uninterrupted success stories count, people who disclose uncertainty may be disadvantaged. Review these assumptions through the legitimate governance or personnel process. Do not use an informal experiment to change hiring, advancement, pay or access to protected opportunities.

Consequences matter after both candor and failure. Useful reporting should receive attention and an appropriate response, while avoidable concealment or harmful conduct may need an authorized process. Rewarding the number of reports can create another distortion: trivial or invented issues become a route to recognition. Prefer relevant information and resolution over raw volume, and preserve the distinction between a good-faith estimate and an unfulfilled commitment.

A small intervention should specify the mechanism, burden and expected observation. For example, adding “ready / missing input / decision needed” plus an owner may expose a missing approval earlier. Check whether the issue was actually acted on, how much effort the ritual cost and whether participants began producing reports merely to look engaged. Do not quietly add surveillance or personal rankings under the language of openness.

Review causality cautiously. A problem surfaced after the new ritual, but this could also reflect the task, participants or a source change. One cycle can show feasibility and a specific response; it cannot demonstrate a durable cultural shift. Use the observation to revise the mechanism, identify which other system would need authorized attention and decide whether further testing is worthwhile.

## Worked example

A group introduces a “risk champion” award for the most reports. The next review contains five copies of one missing-room question. Reporting increased, but useful information did not increase fivefold. The group can retain an early check while removing the volume contest, recognizing a precise warning and its responsible resolution instead. The revision follows the mechanism rather than assuming more activity means more integrity.

## Complete fictional system map

The Paper Almanac group is preparing three items due at **16:00 on 20 November 2026**: a session notice, a welcome script and a paper agenda. Coordinator Emi may change this cycle's check-in ritual with the group's approval. A separate three-member council owns role selection and progression rules; Emi cannot change them alone.

The supplied system facts are: the stated **norm** is “tell us early when an input is missing.” The **incentive** is public praise for every all-green status. The **ritual** is a deadline-day question, “All good?” The **information route** puts venue approvals only on Emi's paper board; writers sometimes see them late. **Selection** for the next visible coordinator task uses a tally of on-time deliverables only. **Progression** to regular facilitator is decided by the council, but its criteria are undocumented in this case. The **consequence** in one prior cycle was that a reporter was called negative, while a late surprise got no inquiry. These facts support a mechanism hypothesis, not a verdict about all members.

This cycle's original records are: notice owner Bea has event/date/time but lacks the venue approval; script owner Sol has all approved inputs; agenda owner Iri needs Emi to decide whether an optional five-minute item is included. Bea, Sol and Iri have accepted their tasks. Emi and venue steward Val are available at **12:30**. The group permits a **12:00** pre-deadline check using one line per task: status, missing fact or decision, accepted resolution owner and response time. No private performance ranking is authorized.

The proposed recognition rule says “one point for every risk report.” Keep it visible as a design choice to evaluate, not an endorsed reward. The intended useful result is early identification and timely disposition of actual dependencies. Measure report count separately from distinct issues, resolution and reporting burden.

## Map, test and revise the mechanism

1. Fill all seven system headings from the supplied facts, marking the unknown progression criteria honestly. Link one plausible incentive to one behavior. Output: a mechanism map without a whole-culture diagnosis.
2. Design the permitted pre-deadline check, route and ownership. State what would count as a useful warning and why a volume reward might distort it. Output: a bounded change, prediction and burden check.
3. Save before the [later packet](later-packet.md). Inspect distinct issues, time surfaced, ownership, response and repeat reporting. Output: a cycle result separate from the raw report count.
4. Revise one unintended incentive and propose a legitimate council review of selection/progression. Output: what changes now, what needs separate authority and what remains untested.

## Support and broader use

Use an accessible paper, spoken or shared-text check; a particular software tool is unnecessary. A harder variation makes the resolution owner unavailable: identify escalation or keep the dependency open rather than praising the report as solved. Real use requires permission, minimal data and a proportionate workload. Do not introduce covert monitoring or alter personnel consequences through a learning exercise.
