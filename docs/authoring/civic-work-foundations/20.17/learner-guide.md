# 20.17 — Social insurance and shared risk

Compare two fictional risk-pooling designs and stress-test their arithmetic without hiding who is excluded or who pays. Use two twenty-five-minute passes and a ten-minute explanation. All amounts are invented units. This is policy literacy, not benefit eligibility, actuarial analysis, tax advice or a conclusion about any actual system's solvency.

## What a pool can and cannot do

Pooling spreads some burdens across people or periods rather than leaving every loss with one household. A society may finance protection through contributions, taxes, reserves or a mixture. Social insurance and tax-funded assistance can overlap in purpose while using different entitlement and funding rules. Do not assume every social-protection mechanism is insurance or that poverty is simply an unpredictable insured event.

Distinguish the needs being addressed. Health costs may be uncertain and uneven. Unemployment can interrupt income. Disability may involve lasting access and support needs as well as earnings loss. Aging can change income and care needs over a long period. A disaster can affect many contributors simultaneously. Poverty can reflect persistent inadequate resources and structural exclusion. One small equal-contribution model cannot represent all these mechanisms or contexts.

A large enough pool for an ordinary period may still be short when needs correlate. A local shock may reduce contributions while increasing claims, although this case holds contributions fixed to isolate one change. Administrative costs, delayed payments, unequal ability to contribute, people outside eligibility and long-term obligations also matter. An arithmetic remainder before costs is not verified profit, a funded reserve or proof of adequate protection.

Evaluate six dimensions separately. Adequacy asks whether support meets the relevant need and arrives in time. Access asks whether entitled people can obtain it in practice. Dignity includes respectful treatment, privacy and challenge routes. Incentives concern possible effects on behavior and institutional effort, which need evidence rather than stereotypes. Cost includes administration and burdens shifted to applicants or helpers. Distribution asks who gains, pays, is wrongly excluded or is wrongly included.

Verification can protect a fund, but paperwork is not free and does not automatically distinguish accurate from inaccurate claims. Simpler access can reduce a barrier without meaning that accountability disappears. Later review can create uncertainty or recovery burdens; a complex process can delay people who qualify. Ask what data would estimate these tradeoffs, whose perspective is missing and how errors could be corrected without unnecessary disclosure.

A conditional judgment is stronger than a perfect score built from unknowns. State which design you currently favor for which purpose, what you cannot estimate and what evidence would reverse that preference. Missing fraud, cost or take-up data must stay missing. A numerical total cannot turn dignity, an unmet essential need or a serious exclusion into an irrelevant detail.

## Worked calculation

If one hundred members contribute ten units, the fund receives 1,000. Ten needs of eighty total 800, leaving 200 before administration. If twenty members need eighty instead, needs total 1,600 and exceed the unchanged fund by 600. This second case is an alternative stress scenario, not an additional period with an automatically accumulated 200-unit reserve.

## Complete fictional policies

The group has 100 adults, each contributing 10 units. In the ordinary scenario ten people each need 80 units. Policy A requires several proof documents and a review before any payment. Policy B permits a simpler initial declaration and later review under a stated challenge process. Both promise the same 80-unit support to eligible applicants; neither has measured processing time, take-up, fraud, erroneous denial, administration cost or later-recovery burden in the packet.

The case does not supply members' incomes, actual eligibility rules or a reserve. A voluntary fictional applicant reports that one document required by A is hard for them to obtain; this identifies a possible access barrier, not a population exclusion rate or proof that B is sufficient. Both designs can be evaluated, but no real person is advised to apply or contribute.

## Compare criteria and a correlated-need shock

1. Calculate contributions, promised ordinary support and the pre-cost remainder. Distinguish the six risk/need areas and name one the toy model represents poorly. Output: an arithmetic account with missing costs and distribution visible.
2. Compare A and B across all six dimensions. Identify both wrongful inclusion and exclusion and what evidence could estimate them. Output: a criterion-by-criterion comparison without invented behavioral rates or moral labels for applicants.
3. Examine the supplied document difficulty and the cost it might shift to a helper. Consider a usable alternative verification or appeal route, but keep feasibility unverified. Output: a specific access question rather than assuming all review is oppressive or all declarations reliable.
4. Save the ordinary model before the [later packet](later-packet.md). Recalculate for twenty needs and examine reserves, broader funding, higher contributions and smaller benefits. Output: consequences for each burden-bearer, with administration still unmodeled.
5. Make a conditional preference and identify a decision-reversing measurement. Explain what the calculation cannot show about a real policy. Output: a transparent judgment that remains open to evidence and affected people's experience.

## Support and transfer

Use counters, a calculator or an oral comparison. Start with one criterion at a time rather than inventing an elaborate score. A harder fictional case reduces contributions during the same shock; state the new assumption before recalculating and do not present it as a forecast.

Actual policy work requires current jurisdiction-specific evidence, appropriate expertise and participation from people affected. Personal benefit, appeal, contribution and tax questions need official local guidance and qualified help where appropriate. Keep income, health, disability and account details outside this exercise. A toy comparison can build understanding but cannot establish real eligibility, fairness or financial sustainability.
