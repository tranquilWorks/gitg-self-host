# 20.15 — Civil disobedience and principled lawbreaking

Compare two fictional claims of principle without planning a breach. Use two twenty-five-minute analyses and a ten-minute comparison. The task ends with a reasoned judgment and revising questions. It does not require public advocacy, violating a rule, risking arrest or giving up legal rights.

## Separate definition, justification and legal status

Civil disobedience commonly refers to a conscientious breach of law used to communicate opposition to a law or policy and seek change. The roles of publicity, nonviolence and accepting consequences are debated in political philosophy. A sympathetic cause alone does not settle whether conduct fits that description or is justified. This exercise examines the canonical dimensions of public reasons, proportionality, nonviolence, transparency and accountability without presenting one checklist as a universal legal test.

First ask what is actually being breached. A venue's custom, a contractual term and an enacted law are not interchangeable. If the case does not establish legal status, say so. A breach of an internal rule might express protest without being a breach of law. Moral judgment and legality are related questions but neither automatically answers the other. A law can be criticized without assuming that conviction creates immunity from its consequences.

A public reason should explain a wrong in terms that others can examine, rather than merely proclaiming loyalty to a favored group. Ask whose dignity or interests are affected and whether the principle would protect opponents under comparable conditions. A person may benefit from a principled change; private benefit does not automatically disqualify the reason. Conversely, adding moral language after a convenient act does not establish that principle guided it.

Consider alternatives seriously. A lawful appeal, public explanation or authorized request may be available, unavailable, ineffective or too slow for an urgent harm. “We did not try” and “it cannot work” are different claims. Neither endless delay nor skipping every alternative is automatically justified. Name evidence that would bear on necessity and acknowledge what the supplied case leaves uncertain.

Proportionality examines the relationship between aim, method and foreseeable burden, including effects on people who did not consent. A nonviolent intention does not guarantee a harmless result. Do not excuse intimidation, property damage, blocked essential services or exposing dependents to danger simply because the cause seems worthy. Your analysis should contain a real condition under which the proposal should not proceed, rather than a decorative assurance that all risk is acceptable.

Transparency concerns reasons and responsibility; it need not require broadcasting vulnerable people's identities. Accountability includes honest description of conduct, attention to harm and willingness to answer reasoned challenge. It does not mean waiving counsel, admitting an accusation you contest or accepting every punishment as just. Real decisions need current qualified local advice. This task does not prescribe how to execute, conceal or evade responsibility for an act.

## Worked comparison

An actor states an equal-access principle before a proposed breach and applies it to opponents. Another hides a convenience-based act and later borrows the same language. Their reasons differ, but the first proposal still needs facts about the rule, alternatives and third-party risk. The second actor could develop a sincere later conviction, yet that would not retroactively establish the purpose or proportionality of the earlier conduct.

## Complete fictional case packet

Case A: Cedar Hall has a written restriction reserving evening room requests to long-standing clubs. Newer clubs report exclusion. An association publicly proposes a nonviolent breach of the restriction to challenge it and says the same access principle should protect clubs it dislikes. One written request has received no answer. The association claims appeals are unavailable, but supplies no policy text or legal advice supporting that claim. No breach occurs in the case; no operational method is provided.

Case B: a person secretly bypasses a booking rule to obtain a convenient time for a private game, then claims equal access after discovery. The stipulated initial purpose was convenience. The person has not explained why the rule is unjust or why ordinary booking was inadequate. Their later claim deserves examination but is not evidence of their earlier motive.

For both cases, the rule's legal status, enforcement powers, immediate harm, available alternatives and effects on uninvolved users are incomplete. Do not invent an arrest, physical obstruction, real political group or jurisdiction.

## Produce a conditional comparative judgment

1. Identify the challenged rule, stated grievance, affected parties and supplied purpose. Keep law, policy and custom distinct. Output: known facts beside missing facts for each case.
2. Present the strongest public reason without endorsing the proposed act. Apply it to an opponent and examine mixed motives without assuming that all benefit implies selfishness. Output: a consistency test with a specific reason for each provisional judgment.
3. Compare lawful or less harmful alternatives and the evidence needed to assess delay or inadequacy. Assess nonconsenting people's burden and name a stop condition. Output: an alternatives-and-proportionality analysis, not instructions for carrying out a breach.
4. Save the judgment before the [later packet](later-packet.md). Update it when the assumed unavailability of review is challenged. Output: a changed necessity claim without erasing the grievance.
5. State what remains a moral question, what needs qualified legal interpretation and what cannot be inferred. Output: a bounded comparison with no universal approval or demand to surrender rights.

## Support and transfer

For support, compare the cases aloud using “reason, alternatives, burden, accountability.” A harder case includes a sincere moral grievance alongside a harmful method; evaluate both rather than granting a blanket exemption. No one needs to reveal their own political commitments.

Transfer remains a source-based or fictional analysis. Real civil-disobedience decisions are outside this exercise and require appropriate legal and safety guidance with attention to affected people. Historical examples can inform reasoning without making present circumstances equivalent or their participants infallible. A thoughtful case analysis is not participation, legal approval or proof of civic courage.
