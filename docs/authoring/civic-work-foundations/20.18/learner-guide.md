# 20.18 — Peacebuilding and reconciliation after collective harm

Compare three responses to a fictional legacy of serious institutional exclusion. Use two twenty-five-minute comparisons and a fifteen-minute synthesis. No survivor contact, testimony collection, mediation or personal disclosure is required. The task asks what different mechanisms can address, not whether a community should forgive or whether a polished plan guarantees healing.

## Peace is more than a quiet surface

Collective harm can affect people, institutions, livelihoods, memory and the credibility of shared rules. A public celebration or apology may acknowledge something important while leaving material loss or ongoing exclusion untouched. Absence of open conflict does not establish consent, equal safety or resolution; people may remain silent because participation is costly or unsafe.

Distinguish six questions. Truth-telling asks what happened, to whom, through which structures and with which evidential limits. Accountability asks how responsibility is established fairly and acted on by legitimate institutions. Memory preserves an honest public record without forcing every person to relive events publicly. Reparations concern meaningful redress, which may be material or symbolic and need to reach affected people. Institutional reform changes rules, incentives and procedures that permit recurrence. Coexistence concerns the conditions for living together with rights and safety despite remaining disagreement or grief.

These functions can complement one another but are not interchangeable. A testimony archive does not itself compensate a loss. Payment does not settle responsibility or guarantee nonrecurrence. A trial has procedures and standards a civic discussion cannot replace. An institutional reform announced without resources or enforcement may change a document while leaving the actual barrier in place.

Affected people need meaningful agency, including ways to participate privately, receive appropriate support, challenge errors or decline participation. They do not all want the same remedy. Do not equate a spokesperson, a public ceremony or a high attendance count with everyone's consent. Preserve absent voices and avoid making access to an ordinary service contingent on a public account of suffering.

Fair accountability includes reliable fact-finding and opportunities to contest claims. Protecting those procedures does not require forgetting harm or granting impunity. Reconciliation, where it is sought, is distinct from individual forgiveness. Coexistence can require enforceable protections and institutional change even when forgiveness never occurs. The learner has no authority to assign guilt, collect sensitive testimony or promise anyone a legal remedy.

Implementation exposes tensions. Speed may help meet an urgent need but can weaken careful inquiry if treated as a reason to bypass it. Open memory can conflict with privacy or safety; a useful public record need not expose every identity. Symbolic repair may be easier to fund than material redress, yet more components on a plan do not make it more effective. Identify resources, sequence, legitimate owners, independent oversight and a route to challenge mistakes.

## Worked example

A town proposes a new archive after discriminatory exclusion. One proposal requires people to tell their stories publicly before ordinary access is restored. The analyst removes that condition, separates support from testimony and asks how records can be checked through legitimate procedures. This improves the design on paper; it does not show that a remedy reached anyone or that the institution changed in practice.

## Complete fictional proposal packet

Cedar Town's official records establish that an institutional rule excluded a category of residents from training access for ten years. The category has no real-world identity in this exercise. The records do not identify every affected person, individual legal responsibility, ongoing risk or each person's loss. The rule has been withdrawn on paper; actual access and enforcement have not been checked.

Proposal A funds a celebration and asks everyone to move on. It closes the old files and creates no repair or challenge route. Proposal B creates a supported voluntary record process with privacy choices and a way to dispute factual errors, but has no budget for material repair or institutional reform. Proposal C proposes a truth process, fair accountability through proper authority, a protected memory record, material redress and reform with independent oversight. It estimates 40 units of work and resources, but only 25 units are identified; the estimate is fictional and none of the mechanisms has begun.

C also contains a problematic draft clause requiring a public personal account before ordinary training access resumes. Affected people have not accepted that condition. Your comparison must address that defect and the resource gap, rather than choosing C merely because it names more desirable mechanisms.

## Compare mechanisms and design conditions

1. List established harm separately from unknown responsibility, loss, actual access and continuing risk. Identify whose participation must be voluntary and which decisions require legitimate qualified authority. Output: a bounded account without invented testimony.
2. Evaluate each proposal across the six questions. Name what it offers, omits or risks, including privacy, retaliation and exclusion. Output: a comparison that does not award success for an intention or ceremony.
3. Examine C's resources and testimony condition. Preserve ordinary access and challenge the compulsory-disclosure clause; explain how a separate repair process would need appropriate eligibility and evidence without coercion. Output: a revised proposal and a real implementation constraint.
4. State a possible sequence with legitimate owners, support, independent oversight and an accessible error-review route. Keep one unresolved tension visible. Output: a conditional design, not a self-authorized investigation or legal decision.
5. Save your comparison before the [later packet](later-packet.md). Revise any claim that paper reform or a fuller plan establishes reconciliation. Output: the strongest limited conclusion and evidence still required.

## Support and transfer

A supported route can use six question cards and non-graphic summaries. A harder case has competing affected-person preferences about public memory; do not force a consensus or select the loudest voice as representative. Privacy and continued ordinary services should not depend on performing forgiveness.

Transfer should remain educational unless qualified, locally legitimate processes authorize actual work. Do not approach survivors, accused people or communities to complete a worksheet. Case analysis can compare models; it cannot establish recovery, justice, peace or reconciliation for real people.
