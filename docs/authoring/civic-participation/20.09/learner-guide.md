# 20.09 — Public discourse and information responsibility

Check one consequential public claim, make a proportionate sharing decision and revisit it once. Allow twenty minutes for the initial check and ten minutes for a later correction. Use the complete fictional closure story or a low-stakes public notice. Staying informed includes knowing when enough has been checked for the present decision; repeated outrage is not a measure of civic attention.

## Match the claim to its evidence and stakes

Begin with the exact proposition. “The center is closing next week” can mean one repair day, an indefinite closure or permanent loss of service. Identify the institution, place, date, duration and uncertainty that would change the meaning. Separate quoted text from a poster's interpretation and a prediction about what will happen. A reaction count does not supply missing context.

Pause before forwarding material that produces urgency or indignation. Ask who created it, what their connection is to the claim and what evidence they show. A source can be mistaken without being malicious. Cropping, an old date, missing conditions, copied language, misleading presentation and pressure to share immediately can all distort understanding. Do not diagnose a stranger's motives or infer a coordinated campaign from one screenshot.

Trace the material back to its original context and compare with a relevant current source. Five posts copying the same notice are five repetitions, not five independent confirmations. An official source may be well placed to state its own operational plan while still lacking evidence that the future plan will occur. A separate staff reply can confirm what is currently planned; it cannot make a conditional reopening certain. Independence, authority, freshness and direct observation answer different questions.

Use a bounded check appropriate to the consequence. A routine service notice may need a dated original and a relevant clarification. A high-stakes health, legal or emergency claim requires the proper authoritative route and should not be settled by a classroom checklist. Consider both kinds of mistake: spreading a false permanent-closure claim can alarm people, while confidently dismissing a real temporary closure can waste a trip. State what is known, what is planned and what remains uncertain.

Sharing is an action with a purpose and an audience. You can decline to amplify, send a concise contextual correction where a false claim already went, or point someone to an authoritative current update. Do not add inflammatory language to make a correction travel. Preserve the useful source, date and scope while minimizing needless repetition of the rumor. A correction should be easy to identify and reach the original audience where feasible.

Set a stopping point and a sensible revisit trigger. An unresolved detail is not always a reason to scroll indefinitely. Save the operational uncertainty, stop checking for now and consult the next relevant dated update. If facts change, distinguish an honest update to a previously qualified statement from correction of an earlier error. Sending a correction does not prove that everyone read or believed it.

## Worked example

A cropped image says a center is “closing next week.” The full notice limits closure to one repair day and makes reopening conditional on inspection. A careful message states those limits and links the dated notice. It does not announce permanent closure or promise next-day access. When the inspection is delayed, the message changes because the plan changed, not because every earlier source was necessarily deceptive.

## Supplied fictional case

A fictional learner has already forwarded a cropped closure message to three fictional friends. No real messages were sent. The crop reads, “Cedar Community Center closing next week,” with no visible date or duration. Five reposts repeat the same crop and express anger; none provides a separate observation.

The complete original notice, dated 8 October, states that the center will be closed on 12 October for repairs, with reopening planned for 13 October subject to inspection. A separate fictional operations-desk reply confirms this current plan and says the inspection result is not yet known. That reply is a separate contact about the same plan, not observation of a completed reopening. The later packet will supply one subsequent notice; do not invent the outcome now.

## Produce a responsible claim and correction

1. Copy the exact proposition you need to check and identify the crop's missing context. State the harm of an overclaim and of wrongly dismissing the closure. Output: a precise checkable claim and decision purpose.
2. Trace the crop, five copies, original notice and operations reply. Mark dependence, date, competence and whether each concerns a plan or an observed event. Output: a short evidence chain without a false vote count of sources.
3. Write a contextual correction to the three original fictional recipients. Include the one-day closure, conditional reopening and source date. Output: an explicit correction with no guaranteed reopening or unsupported permanent-closure allegation.
4. Set one bounded revisit point for the inspection update, then stop. Open the [later packet](later-packet.md) and revise the operational statement. Output: a dated update that distinguishes changed plans from the original cropping error.
5. Record the actual communication state. In this exercise every transmission is supplied fiction; in life, record only what you really sent. Output: audience, correction/update status and any unknown readership, without collecting contacts or screenshots as proof.

## Support and transfer

For support, lay out the four supplied source types on separate cards and explain the chain aloud. For a harder case, the official page has not been updated but a screenshot claims it has. Preserve uncertainty, seek a relevant clarification once and avoid an unsupported categorical share.

Real transfer can use an ordinary public service notice. Prefer direct links over reposting sensitive content. Do not contact people repeatedly, harass a source or infer anyone's politics from sharing behavior. The goal is an accurate bounded decision, not winning a dispute or maximizing attention. Fiction practices verification and correction; it cannot demonstrate an actual audience's understanding.
