# 20.07 — Helping without paternalism

Offer a small piece of help that the receiving person chooses and controls. Allow fifteen minutes to prepare, a brief consented exchange and one agreed low-risk action. Use an event-registration form or the supplied fiction. Useful help can include continuing assistance; independence is not a test someone must pass to deserve support.

## Start from the person's task

The helper's first interpretation can be wrong. Difficulty with a form might reflect unclear instructions, a poor layout, an unfamiliar term, an inaccessible format or something else. It does not establish low literacy, disability, inability to decide or lack of motivation. Ask what the person wants to accomplish and what kind of help, if any, would be useful. A refusal is information about permission, not a challenge to overcome.

Separate the outcome from the mode of assistance. Someone may want to understand a waiting list and prefer a term read aloud. That does not authorize choosing an event, supplying personal details or submitting an application. Options might include reading instructions, clarifying a word from the official text, locating a public contact or stepping back. Describe your own limits; a friendly helper is not automatically a benefits adviser, interpreter or legal representative.

Preserve control at the actual decision point. Let the person keep their device, credentials, documents, choices and final submission. An offer is not acceptance, and acceptance of one task does not expand silently. Ask before changing the mode or scope. Where more assistance is requested, confirm it rather than withholding it to manufacture an independence outcome. If a task exceeds your competence, offer an appropriate route without taking over or promising eligibility.

Paternalism can also hide in how a result is described. Gratitude might express politeness or relief; it does not prove that a form was understood or a goal met. Ask whether the agreed part was useful in the person's terms. A concrete observation, such as locating the intended public contact with a chosen accessible copy, can support a narrow capacity claim. Do not make the person demonstrate ability for your satisfaction, reveal a diagnosis, endorse your views or appear in a success story.

Consider what remains after you leave. Keeping the only usable instructions or contact information with the helper can create avoidable dependence. Offer a copy in a useful format, while respecting a preference for ongoing help. Dependence on reliable support is not humiliation or failure. The problem is unwanted control, blocked options or help made conditional on loyalty. Context matters: improving the form itself may require its owner, not more effort from the recipient.

## Worked example

A helper starts to say, “Give me the phone and I'll finish it.” The person replies that they only want the waiting-list term explained in large print. The helper checks the supplied instruction, produces the requested copy and leaves the decision and device with the person. The person can now find the public questions contact; whether they register is still their choice. The helper's smaller role is the agreed useful contribution.

## Supplied fictional case

Lee is looking at a fictional community-event registration form. Its instruction says: “A waiting-list request does not reserve a seat. The organizer sends a separate confirmation if a seat becomes available.” A second line identifies the public Event Questions contact, without a real address. No deadline, fee, eligibility rule or actual event is supplied.

Lee asks Pat for a large-print copy of those two lines and a spoken explanation of waiting list versus confirmed place. Lee keeps the fictional device and all choices. Pat is permitted to read the instruction and prepare the copy, but not to handle credentials, select an event or submit anything. Lee wants to retain the contact line for future questions and may ask for the same reading support again. Do not invent a personal history to explain the request.

## Carry out one chosen assistance unit

1. Ask what result and mode are wanted. In fiction, restate Lee's specific request and the ability to stop. Output: agreed task and permissions, with no assumed diagnosis or broad takeover.
2. Read the supplied instruction and distinguish the two statuses in ordinary words. Identify anything it does not answer. Output: a usable explanation that does not promise a seat, eligibility or a response date.
3. Make the requested large-print copy or rehearse that action accessibly. Leave the device, choices and submission with Lee. Output: the selected support actually offered or completed, accurately labeled.
4. Ask whether the agreed help was useful and whether anything else is wanted. Offer the public contact copy without demanding a performance test. Output: the recipient's chosen next step and the actual endpoint, including “no further help wanted.”
5. Compare your first assumption with the [later packet](later-packet.md). Retain the option of recurring chosen help and identify a form-design question only its owner can resolve. Output: one correction to the helper's approach and a bounded benefit claim.

## Support and transfer

A supported attempt can rehearse the request and response aloud with the fictional instruction. Large print, oral response, more time and continuing chosen assistance are legitimate, not lowered dignity. For a harder case, the helper could finish quickly but the person wants time to decide. Preserve the pause; urgency created by the helper is not permission.

Real transfer should use a willing person's low-risk task with explicit scope. Stop at private credentials, regulated advice, uncertain authority or unrequested intervention. Leave necessary information accessible to its owner and agree any follow-up rather than assuming continuing access. Fiction can demonstrate the distinction between support and control, but cannot establish that an actual person's capacity or safety increased.
