# 20.12 — Institutional and media-system literacy

Trace one public claim from its original evidence through the institutions that produce, select and distribute it. Allow two twenty-five-minute checks and a ten-minute explanation to a willing reader, or use the complete fiction. The goal is to identify where the claim changes and which accountability route fits the error, without assuming that prestige proves truth or financial support proves fraud.

## Follow both the information and the roles

Institutions can perform different functions in the same claim's journey. A university may produce research; an agency may commission it and administer the program studied. Journalism selects, checks and explains material for an audience. A party may use it to argue for a policy. A nonprofit may conduct research, provide services or advocate a position. A corporation may fund work, own a publication or operate a platform. A platform can select and amplify distribution through its rules and systems. A court may assess a dispute under legal procedures, which is a different role from routine scientific review.

These are roles to investigate, not fixed judgments about virtue. One organization can occupy several roles, and an institution type may have no part in the particular chain. Do not invent a court case or hidden funder to fill a diagram. Ask what each actor actually did: collected data, paid, summarized, edited, distributed, corrected or reviewed. Separate an institution's stated responsibilities from evidence that it performed them well in this instance.

Start with the original material. Identify author, sponsor, date, question, population, recruitment, response, method, limitations and accessible underlying evidence. A headline saying “a study proves” leaves all of that open. Count denominators: a percentage among respondents does not automatically describe all invited people, all users or every resident. Nonresponse creates uncertainty; it is not evidence that everyone who did not answer holds the opposite view.

Causal claims need more than positive reports. Satisfaction among a selected group can matter without showing what would have happened without a program. Ask whether there is a suitable comparison, what changed and what else could explain the result. Missing evidence does not establish that the program failed. It limits which conclusion the supplied design can support.

Then inspect the handoffs. Quote or paraphrase each actor's actual claim accurately, mark its source and note any change in population, certainty, timeframe or outcome. Several articles derived from one survey are not independent replications. Selection and incentives can explain what to examine, but do not determine whether an individual claim is false. A disclosed agency sponsor deserves scrutiny of method and independence, not an automatic accusation of corruption.

Match accountability to the problem. A methods question belongs with the research producer; an inaccurate headline can be corrected by its newsroom; an advocacy overclaim calls for clarification from its author. A platform may offer a route for a distribution or policy issue without adjudicating survey design. Courts are not a generic correction desk. Check an institution's actual route before real contact, and distinguish a draft inquiry, a submitted correction request, an acknowledgment and a published correction.

## Worked example

A survey reports that forty of fifty respondents express satisfaction. A headline changes that to “80% of residents benefit,” and an advocate calls it proof of effectiveness. The denominator has expanded without evidence and satisfaction has become causal benefit. A careful trace retains the survey's limited descriptive finding while identifying two separate overclaims and the people responsible for each statement.

## Supplied fictional case

The fictional Cedar agency funds a university survey about one of its programs. The report states that 200 program users were invited, 50 responded and 40 respondents selected the positive satisfaction option. Ten respondents did not select that option; no finer breakdown is supplied. The response rate is 25%, and 80% of respondents selected the positive option. The report describes this sample and does not claim a causal effect, a representative city estimate or comparison with a nonparticipant group.

The agency's summary preserves “40 of 50 respondents.” A fictional newspaper headline instead says, “80% of residents benefit.” A party post links that headline and says the program is “proven to work.” A nonprofit reposts the party post without adding data. A corporation-operated platform distributes those posts. Its ranking mechanism, the newspaper's ownership, and any funding beyond the disclosed agency sponsorship are unknown. No court has reviewed the claim.

All names, numbers and statements are original fiction. They make no accusation about an actual university, party, company, publication or program.

## Trace and explain the public claim

1. Reconstruct the original survey's population, response, question and design limits. Calculate the two stated percentages with the correct denominators. Output: a methods note that leaves nonrespondents' views and program impact unknown.
2. Map the actual producer, funder, summarizer, news selector, advocate, republisher and distributor. Identify where roles overlap and where a type, such as a court, is absent. Output: a role chain with explicit unknowns, not a hierarchy of trust by brand.
3. Compare the wording at each handoff. Locate the first denominator expansion and the later causal escalation. Output: separate error descriptions that preserve the valid descriptive result.
4. Draft a proportionate clarification or correction for the responsible role. No real contact is required. Output: a relevant route and precisely requested change, without allegations of hidden motives or guaranteed correction.
5. Explain your conclusion to the supplied fictional reader or a willing real reader, then consult the [later packet](later-packet.md). Output: a revised explanation after a fresh denominator challenge, with all communication statuses accurate.

## Support and transfer

For support, use a two-stage count drawing: 200 invited, then 50 respondents, then 40 positive responses within those 50. An oral explanation or accessible list can replace a diagram. For a harder case, the source supplies only a percentage without its denominator. Ask for the missing method and withhold the broader claim; do not invent sample size or a margin of error.

For real transfer, use public material and a low-stakes claim. Check current institutional roles and procedures without collecting private records or profiling staff. A friendly reader can test your explanation but cannot certify the study. The fictional chain supports reasoning about institutional filtering; it does not establish actual misconduct, effectiveness or a completed correction.
