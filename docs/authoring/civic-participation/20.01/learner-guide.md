# 20.01 — Social interdependence

Trace one ordinary benefit through the people and systems that make it possible, then complete a proportionate reciprocal action. Allow two twenty-minute sessions and one small action within a week. Use borrowing a library book or the supplied fiction. The aim is to recognize dependencies and responsibilities, not calculate your moral debt or require spending, unpaid work or public gratitude.

## See the system behind the encounter

The visible transaction is rarely the whole service. A book on a shelf depends on acquisition, cataloging, storage, a way to find it, rules for access and people maintaining that arrangement. Paying a fee, paying taxes or returning the book is a contribution, but it does not mean you personally produced every benefit. Earlier work can remain useful long after the people who performed it have left.

Trace six kinds of dependence. Infrastructure includes buildings, power, transport and digital systems. Labor includes visible assistance and less visible selection, repair, cleaning and administration. Law and rules establish authority, responsibilities and access conditions; a policy is not necessarily a statute. Knowledge includes authorship, cataloging methods and accumulated practical understanding. Environment includes materials, energy and the conditions supporting the service, although a precise footprint requires evidence. Inherited institutions include practices and assets that earlier generations built and maintained.

A map becomes informative when it distinguishes support from speculation. Seeing a volunteer badge does not tell you how every worker is paid. A free digital catalog still needs maintenance, but its cost cannot be invented from that observation. Mark the source of each entry and preserve unknowns. Public documents and ordinary use are enough; do not observe workers covertly, collect their names or ask them to justify their jobs.

Reciprocity is a reasoned response to shared benefit. Returning an item through the correct route can protect another person's access. Reporting damage can help a steward decide what to repair. An unsolicited donation may instead create sorting, storage or disposal work. Ask whether a contribution is wanted and whether its burden fits both your capacity and the receiving system.

Individual and institutional responsibilities differ. A user should handle an item responsibly; the institution still needs fair access, adequate staffing and sound maintenance. Gratitude does not excuse unsafe work or make an inaccessible service the user's fault. Criticism can be compatible with responsible participation. Neither receiving help nor having limited capacity reduces someone's dignity.

## Worked example

A reader thinks the service consists of a staff member handing over a book. Their trace adds the catalog record, purchasing decision, shared building and earlier preservation work. They offer a box of unwanted books, then learn that donations are not currently requested. They instead return the borrowed item properly and report a loose page. The second action fits an actual need without making the staff sort a surprise delivery.

## Supplied fictional case

Cedar Community Library lends Alex one book. The fictional service note says that the book was acquired from its materials budget, staff maintain the catalog, the building and digital system support access, and the local borrowing policy asks users to report damage at the desk. A preserved collection from earlier years is still used. The book's paper source, energy use and individual workers' pay are not supplied.

Alex notices a loose page during ordinary use. The due date is 10 October. The note asks that damaged items be brought to the desk rather than repaired by a borrower. It also says unrequested donations are not currently accepted. Alex can return the book on 9 October but has no repair role. No real institution or worker is described by these invented facts.

## Make a dependency-to-responsibility trace

1. Put the borrowed book at the center of a small map. Add the six branches and place a supplied fact or explicit unknown on each. Distinguish the paper's unknown origin from the observed material object. Output: a sourced trace, not a list of people you assume are responsible.
2. Separate Alex's own effort from the inherited resources and ongoing work received. Identify one benefit that would disappear if its support stopped. Output: a short explanation of dependence without implying that one worker or user carries the whole system.
3. Compare returning/reporting, attempting a repair and bringing an unsolicited donation. Consider actual permission, likely usefulness and receiving burden. Output: one bounded reciprocal action and one institutional obligation that remains with the library.
4. Rehearse or perform the permitted return and report through the proper route. Check what endpoint occurred: handed over, accepted, logged or repaired. These are different states. Output: the actual action and acknowledgment, with no employee photograph or private record required.
5. Save your map before the [later packet](later-packet.md). Revise one unsupported assumption and state what the action can reasonably show. Output: a changed understanding and a modest contribution claim, without claiming system-wide improvement.

## Support and transfer

For support, begin with the visible book and trace only one connection at a time until all six branches have either evidence or an unknown. An oral explanation or accessible diagram can replace writing. For a harder fictional case, the easiest personal action transfers extra work to already busy staff. Compare whether a smaller requested action is more useful, and identify a policy question that cannot be solved by individual helpfulness.

Real transfer can use transit, a shared utility or a public knowledge resource. Check the service's actual rules and your authority; do not touch infrastructure or edit records to create evidence. A fictional trace develops explanation but cannot establish an actual contribution. A completed return shows responsible use in that instance, not the fairness, environmental performance or resilience of the whole institution.
