# 20.05 — Engagement across difference

Represent another person's concern accurately, then cooperate on one limited shared good while keeping a genuine disagreement visible. Allow fifteen minutes to prepare, a willing twenty-minute exchange and a small task. Use the fictional notice case when there is no safe willing partner. This is not covert persuasion, mediation of abuse or a demand that someone defend their dignity.

## Understanding and agreement are different outcomes

A fair representation identifies the other person's actual reason, not the weakest version you can rebut. Let them correct it. “You dislike technology” may be a caricature of “I do not want people without smartphones excluded.” A summary can become more accurate even if you still prefer a different policy. Accuracy is not disloyalty to your own conviction.

Religion, politics, culture, class, age and experience can shape what a person notices and values. None makes them a spokesperson for everyone in a category. Ask about the concern relevant to this task rather than building a profile of their beliefs or resources. Do not infer a person's motives from age, accent, clothing or presumed affiliation. They may choose not to explain private experiences.

Cooperation needs an identifiable common good. Preparing a readable event notice is narrower than agreeing on a whole philosophy of public services. State what you can do together and what remains disputed. A useful shared task must not quietly commit either person to a broader ideology, organization or future role.

Boundaries make honest engagement possible. Each person can decline topics, refuse recording, choose a suitable communication format or stop an unsafe exchange. Respect for a person does not require accepting every claim or normalizing harassment. Exclude situations involving retaliation, coercion or pressure to debate basic dignity. An unwilling partner is not raw material for practice.

A courteous response is not necessarily consent or confirmation of understanding. Ask whether the summary is accurate and whether the proposed task is accepted. Listen for corrections rather than treating them as a delay before your argument. When a correction changes the task, make that change visible. Credit should reflect actual contributions, and a disagreement must not become an excuse to sabotage the shared result.

## Worked example

Jo wants important community notices available on paper because some users cannot reliably use a phone. Bea prefers online notices because they can be searched and updated. Bea first summarizes Jo as wanting to abandon digital access. Jo corrects that summary: the concern is exclusion, not banning technology. They may cooperate on one readable notice while still disagreeing about which format should usually lead.

## Supplied fictional case

Jo and Bea have permission from an event coordinator to prepare one public, non-sensitive notice in an agreed paper format and a matching digital text. The coordinator supplies the correct date, place and public contact route. No one is authorized to change the event, adopt a new organization-wide policy or publish personal details.

Use these exact invented public facts for both versions: **Reading Together; 18 October, 15:00–16:00; Cedar Hall, Room B; questions: Event Desk at Cedar Hall**. “Event Desk” is a fictional public contact label, not a real address. The agreed paper layout has the title, date/time, place and contact on separate lines in large readable type; the digital version preserves the same four facts in plain text. Keep both as drafts for the coordinator, with no printing or posting required. These are all the facts needed; if asked about cost or booking, mark it unknown.

Jo prefers paper access to have equal standing; Bea still prefers online-first updates. They are willing to discuss that ordinary disagreement and prepare the notice, with no recording or inquiry into private political, religious or financial circumstances. Jo can check the supplied event facts. Bea can prepare the readable layout and matching text. The roles remain offers until each accepts them.

## Represent, agree and deliver the bounded part

1. State the shared deliverable and your own conviction separately. Identify the topics or details that are outside the willing exchange. Prepare an open question about the other person's concern. Output: a task invitation that neither assumes agreement nor asks them to represent a group.
2. Summarize their reason and invite a correction. State what you now understand, where you still disagree and what is uncertain. Output: a checked representation without a rebuttal smuggled into it. The other person's right to decline remains intact.
3. Ask whether the limited notice task is worth doing together. Confirm roles, facts, owner permission and what completion means. Output: explicit acceptance rather than a polite nod interpreted as a commitment to a larger program.
4. Complete the accepted parts and hand the paired notice to the coordinator through the agreed route. Share credit and identify any unresolved output issue. Output: the actual deliverable status, not an assumed public benefit or policy compromise.
5. Save your account before the [later packet](later-packet.md). Ask whether each person could contribute honestly, and record one improved understanding alongside one retained conviction. Output: a cooperation review that does not claim the disagreement vanished.

## Support and transfer

For support, use asynchronous text and a one-sentence summary with a chance to correct it. An interpreter or accessible format can help when appropriate and wanted. For a harder fictional attempt, the other person corrects your account twice or declines the task. Accept that information without diagnosing bad faith. A responsible closure can be a valid outcome even when no joint output follows.

Real transfer can involve an ordinary shared good with a willing neighbor, colleague or community participant. Do not use assistance or access to a resource as leverage for a political or religious conversation. Preserve substantive disagreement when it remains; the aim is fair understanding and bounded cooperation, not conversion. One pleasant exchange says little about long-term trust, conflict resolution or whole-group coexistence.

## Source use and evidence

Its broader program and claimed effects are not evaluated here. The original two-person notice task is not presented as an official LRC session or validated intervention. A supplied fictional correction is not actual participant feedback; in real work, appropriate freely given correction and a completed task provide different evidence from self-report alone.
