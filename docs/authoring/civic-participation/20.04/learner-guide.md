# 20.04 — Justice, inequality, and social structure

Explain an observed opportunity gap through competing mechanisms, then propose a reversible response with limited claims. Use three fifteen-minute passes over the fictional workshop case. No demographic, income, health or identity data are needed. The exercise develops causal judgment and responsible design; it cannot establish the prevalence of a real inequality or the character of a group.

## A gap is a question, not its own explanation

An outcome describes what happened. A mechanism proposes how it happened. A value judgment explains why an outcome or process matters. Keep those different kinds of statement visible. “Twenty of forty interested people attended” is an observation. “They lacked motivation” is a proposed explanation requiring evidence. “People should have a usable opportunity to participate” is a normative commitment, not a statistical finding.

Individual choices and structural conditions can operate together. Someone may decide that another obligation matters more while facing limited transport, an inaccessible venue or a difficult signup route. Recognizing a constraint does not erase agency. Recognizing agency does not make every option equally available. A useful explanation asks which choices existed, at what cost and under whose rules.

History can shape present resources through inherited institutions, location, wealth and earlier access to education or services. Class can affect disposable time and transport options. Race and sex can be relevant to histories of exclusion, institutional treatment or expectations. Disability can interact with a building or communication design. Geography affects distance and available routes. These are questions about mechanisms and context, not permission to assign identity, experience or motive to a person from appearance.

Institutions and incentives deserve the same scrutiny as individual decisions. An online-only signup may simplify administration while excluding some interested users. Attendance targets may encourage organizers to recruit the easiest-to-reach people. A feedback form framed as “Why did you fail to attend?” may discourage honest answers. Neither an incentive nor a barrier proves deliberate discrimination; actual rights concerns still require the appropriate institutional or qualified legal process.

Justice analysis asks who receives an opportunity, who bears the cost, who can influence the design and who remains unheard. It also asks whether the proposed response creates new exclusion. Moving an event may improve physical access while lengthening someone's journey. A design can be worth improving for a clear access reason even before a study establishes its effect on attendance.

## Worked example

A free course has lower attendance than expressions of interest. Calling it free captures the fee but omits travel, time and access costs. A respectful analysis compares several mechanisms and asks what evidence could distinguish them. Changing both the venue and reminders might be practically sensible, but a later attendance increase would not isolate the effect of the venue. Better conditions and stronger causal evidence are different achievements.

## Supplied fictional data

Forty people express interest in Cedar's evening workshop; twenty attend. The advertised venue requires stairs, a typical transit route uses two bus changes, and registration is online only. No participant identities, demographic breakdown, reasons for nonattendance or costs are supplied. There is no control group and no evidence that all expressions of interest were commitments to attend.

The organizer can consider a step-free room or a second signup route, with permission and input from willing users. The exercise does not ask you to inspect a building without authority, contact nonattendees or request sensitive data. Start from the supplied facts and mark the rest as hypotheses.

## Compare mechanisms and a possible response

1. Calculate the attendance fraction and separate the gap from explanations. List plausible individual, logistical and institutional mechanisms. Include historical or demographic questions only as unknown context, not participant labels. Output: an observation column distinct from a hypothesis column.
2. Choose three mechanisms and an appropriate evidence route for each. A verified transit timetable can test travel feasibility, a permitted accessibility review can examine the venue, and a voluntary minimal question can explore signup difficulty. Output: what each route could and could not show, without claiming that it has already been used.
3. Identify incentives that could distort collection or interpretation. Ask whose experience might be missing and retain a competing explanation such as a schedule clash. Output: a privacy-conscious inquiry plan with no required demographic disclosure or blame-framed question.
4. Propose one reversible change, its permission and consultation needs, resource burden and a possible unintended exclusion. Define a modest usability or attendance comparison. Output: an actionable proposal that does not promise all forty people will attend or claim that the change proves a theory of inequality.
5. Save your analysis before the [later packet](later-packet.md). Recalculate the supplied later result and identify changed conditions. Output: a conditional conclusion, including what would still be needed for a causal or fairness claim.

## Support and transfer

For support, draw forty marks and distinguish the twenty attendance marks from unknown reasons. Use three mechanisms rather than an exhaustive social explanation. For a harder version, the preferred barrier is removed and attendance stays flat: explain why that need not prove the barrier was unreal or the effort worthless. It may matter to access while other constraints remain.

Real transfer requires an authorized organization, appropriate consultation and minimal consented information. Do not infer race, class, disability or other identity from a name or photograph. Serious discrimination concerns need the proper process, not an improvised exercise verdict. Repeated, well-designed evidence and affected people's perspectives are needed to understand broader opportunity and harm.
