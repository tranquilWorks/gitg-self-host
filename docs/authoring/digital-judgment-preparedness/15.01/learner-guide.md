# 15.01 — First aid, CPR, and emergency response

## Make the handoff usable and the training gap visible

Emergency response includes recognizing a possible emergency, summoning appropriate help, communicating useful observations and giving care within current guidance and your ability. These are related skills, but completing one does not demonstrate the others. A polished call script cannot establish CPR technique; enrolling in a course does not establish attendance or a successful skills assessment.

Allow 35 minutes for the supplied communication and course-selection exercises, plus the separate time required for appropriate qualified training. Use paper, an unconnected phone or an accessible communication aid. Never dial an emergency number for practice, have someone pretend to be unconscious, or perform compressions, choking maneuvers or other treatment on a healthy person. In a real emergency, seek local emergency help immediately and follow dispatcher/AED guidance; do not delay action while completing a worksheet or waiting to earn a certificate.

## Know what the training topics actually concern

The [American Red Cross first-aid resources](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid) distinguish topics that should not be collapsed into a generic “first aid complete” label. The concepts below orient your training questions; they are not a standalone procedure manual.

| Topic | Meaning and question for practical instruction |
| --- | --- |
| Severe bleeding | Ongoing major blood loss can be urgent. Learn recognition, protective measures and appropriate bleeding control on approved equipment; specialized methods need suitable instruction. |
| CPR | Supports circulation in cardiac arrest. An unresponsive adult who is not breathing normally or is only gasping needs immediate emergency response; learn the current sequence and technique for your role and age group. |
| AED | A defibrillator analyzes rhythm and gives prompts; learn safe pad placement, prompts and coordination with CPR using a trainer. Merely knowing where a cabinet is does not demonstrate use. |
| Choking | Airway obstruction differs from cardiac arrest, and response varies with the person's condition and age. Learn recognition and the appropriate supervised skills, including relevant adaptations. |
| Shock | A dangerous failure of adequate circulation/oxygen delivery, not simply emotional surprise. Learn recognition, emergency escalation and appropriate supportive care. |
| Burns | Heat, chemicals and electricity create different hazards. Learn scene safety, appropriate first aid and escalation; do not improvise a single treatment for every burn. |
| Poisoning | Harm can follow swallowing, breathing, skin or other exposure. Use emergency/poison-service guidance appropriate to location and severity; do not induce vomiting unless the poison specialist specifically instructs it. |
| Communication | Give location, access, observed condition and changes; listen and answer the dispatcher. Do not replace “not responding, only gasping” with an assumed diagnosis or “breathing fine.” |

Current professional topic links and limits are in [sources](../SOURCES.md). For local emergency and poison-service numbers, use the actual jurisdiction's official service information. No universal number or text-service availability is assumed here.

Worked example: “Someone is ill at the club” leaves responders without a usable location or condition. “Cedar Hall, 18 Practice Lane, east entrance, main room; one adult collapsed, is not responding and is only gasping” supplies concrete facts in the fictional case. The latter is still a rehearsal, not a real call or proof that care occurred.

## Complete fictional collapse case

Cedar Hall is at **18 Practice Lane, Cedar Town**. This address is fictional. The ordinary response entrance is the east door; the main room is directly inside. You observed one adult collapse. They do not respond and are only gasping. There is no visible fire, traffic or electrical hazard in the initial scene. A willing bystander, Jo, is beside you. An AED cabinet is marked at reception, but nobody has fetched or used it. Your callback information would come from the actual device during a real call; write “practice callback placeholder” here, not a made-up working number.

### Action 1 — Prepare and speak the handoff

Write a short card with location, entrance, callback placeholder, number of affected people and exact observed condition. Include “follow dispatcher questions; report changes.” Then speak the report aloud, or communicate through your chosen accessible practice method. Record whether you actually rehearsed and which facts you omitted on the first attempt.

Delegate a specific task in the rehearsal: “Jo, get the AED from reception and tell me when you have it,” while the caller follows emergency guidance. In a real event, task assignment depends on the situation and dispatcher; do not leave an unsafe scene or abandon necessary care to obey a rigid worksheet sequence. Do not invent that the AED is present merely because someone was asked to fetch it.

### Action 2 — Repeat with a changed access route

Open [the later packet](later-packet.md) after saving the first report. Repeat using its changed entrance facts. No physical treatment is acted out. If an observer helps, ask them to identify omitted location/access/condition facts, not to certify clinical competence. If alone, compare your written or spoken recall with the packet. Recording audio is optional and should contain only fiction.

For the packet's separate electrical-hazard branch, state that an unsafe approach ends the ordinary rehearsal and that real responders need the hazard information. Do not enter a hazardous area to collect better observations. “Unknown breathing” should remain unknown if it cannot safely be assessed.

### Action 3 — Select training and audit actual scope

These are complete **invented course descriptions**, not purchasable recommendations:

| Course | Format and stated assessment |
| --- | --- |
| A | Two-hour online knowledge module covering all eight listed topics; no instructor-observed physical skills |
| B | Three-hour supervised session on approved equipment: adult CPR, AED, adult choking and emergency communication; no burns, poisoning, shock or bleeding-control assessment |
| C | Four-hour first-aid session: observed bleeding-control practice plus instructor-assessed shock, burns, poisoning and communication scenarios; no CPR/AED/choking skills assessment |

Choose a route for the listed scope and identify any remaining age-group, role or accessibility gaps. B plus C covers the described adult course content on paper; it does not prove that either was attended or passed. [Red Cross's online-course explanation](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/first-aid/first-aid-training/first-aid-online) explicitly distinguishes online learning from demonstrating proficiency to an instructor.

Use the later attendance record to make eight topic rows: `not yet learned | knowledge studied | attended | assessed result | follow-up`. For actual training, verify the recognized provider, local/role requirements, equipment, accessible participation, instructor observation and renewal guidance. Record only what was actually demonstrated. A certificate's title is not a substitute for its assessed scope.

Attempt [fresh checks](check-prompts.md), then [the key](check-answers.md). The default completes communication practice and a training-gap audit; hands-on qualification remains pending until real instruction and assessment occur.

## Adapt and continue

Use a communication card, interpreter or locally verified accessible emergency route. Physical capacity can change which role you perform; useful communication and delegation still matter. Do not describe an unperformed skill as passed because an adaptation was planned. Harder route: choose training for a setting with children and show why adult-only assessment is insufficient for that requirement.

For transfer, verify your local emergency route without making a test call, prepare a private location/access card and arrange an appropriate recognized practical course or refresher if needed. Keep enrollment, attendance and assessed outcomes separate. In a real emergency, immediate help and current guidance take precedence over this learning sequence.

**Supportive:** Communication is rehearsed and actual assessed topics are recorded accurately. **Mixed:** The handoff improves but physical skills remain unverified. **Contradictory:** Forceful practice is performed on a healthy person or real help is delayed for the exercise. **Inconclusive:** A course is bookmarked without rehearsal or observed skills evidence.
