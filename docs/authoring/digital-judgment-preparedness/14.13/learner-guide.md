# 14.13 — Digital citizenship and platform governance

## A policy must work for the people who need to challenge it

Platform governance decides who can participate, what gets removed, how errors are corrected and what information is collected along the way. A rule can pursue a legitimate safety goal and still impose unnecessary privacy costs or silence people through mistakes. A policy evaluation should explain both the protection and its tradeoffs, then test whether the process is usable with the available staff and technology.

Allow 45 minutes for the fictional Harbor Forum policy below. Use paper or an accessible editor. You are proposing a rule, not moderating real users or giving a legal judgment. No abusive text, identity documents or private reports need to be collected. Your output is a short policy, three case decisions, a capacity calculation and a revision.

## Six lenses with different questions

Safety asks whether users can participate without targeted abuse. Expression asks what legitimate speech could be mistakenly restricted. Privacy and surveillance ask what data are collected, who can access them and how long they persist. Accessibility asks whether people can read notices and use controls. Incentives and competition ask what behavior the platform rewards and whether users can leave without losing essential access. Due process asks whether a person can understand and challenge a decision.

The [European Commission's DSA user-rights overview](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/factpages/user-rights-under-digital-services-act) describes transparency and challenge mechanisms in the EU framework. It is a jurisdiction-specific example, not proof that the same obligations apply to every small forum worldwide. Our invented policy deadlines below are design choices, not statutory periods. [W3C accessibility guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tips/developing/) supports keyboard-operable controls and usable forms; adding the word “accessible” to a policy does not establish that its interface works.

Worked example: a ban notice says only “rules broken” and provides an appeal button visible only after sign-in. A banned person cannot sign in. The appeal exists in the document but is unusable at the moment it matters. A repair needs an appropriate authenticated-by-alternative route, clear reasons and a responsible reviewer, with careful protection against someone else taking over the appeal.

## Complete platform case

Harbor Forum has 200 adult members discussing local hobbies. It earns advertising income when people keep reading. Pseudonyms currently help several members participate safely. Users can export their own posts in plain text, but the export excludes their saved topic organization; moving communities therefore has a real cost. No evidence shows whether legal names reduce abuse on this forum.

Proposed rule: “Every removed post produces an automatic permanent account ban. No explanation or appeal is provided. Every member must publish their legal name. All private messages are retained forever for future safety investigations.”

The stated purpose is to stop repeated harassment and reduce moderator workload. Consider at least these affected users: a person being targeted, a member whose quotation was mistaken for endorsement, a person needing pseudonymity for safety, and someone who uses keyboard navigation. Do not assume these needs are mutually exclusive.

Available capacity: two moderators each have 30 minutes per day for this review queue. An ordinary review requires ten minutes; an urgent safety case requires twenty minutes in the model. Today's expected workload is four ordinary reviews and one urgent review. There is no 24-hour coverage or guaranteed outside support. The current appeal form requires dragging an image slider and a logged-in account, both defects for some users or banned accounts.

### Action 1 — State consequences and uncertainties

Make six short rows, one per lens. For each, identify a concrete effect, affected person and uncertain factual assumption. Fairly state the proposed rule's intended benefit before evaluating costs. Separate a value choice (“we should preserve pseudonymity”) from a factual claim (“public legal names prevent repeat abuse”) that would need evidence.

Include the incentive conflict: a platform paid for engagement may benefit from heated threads while moderators bear the work. This is a plausible incentive in the stipulated model, not proof of any actual company's intent. Consider competition beyond “people can leave”: exports, community ties and switching effort can constrain that option.

### Action 2 — Draft a feasible replacement

Write a policy of roughly 200–300 words. Define prohibited conduct through behavior, such as repeated targeted contact after a clear stop request, rather than disagreement or identity. Distinguish removing harmful material, temporary protective restrictions and permanent sanctions after proportionate review. Permit urgent protective action while preserving later review; an imminent real-world danger needs appropriate emergency routes beyond an unstaffed hobby forum.

Specify a notice containing the affected item, rule, reason, action and review route while protecting reporters' private details. Provide an appeal route usable without the banned account's ordinary session and without the slider-only barrier; include an alternative contact method and identity verification proportionate to the appeal. Preserve pseudonymity unless a specific justified process requires private verification.

Choose who can see necessary evidence, a limited retention/review period and exceptions that require explicit justification. Do not adopt permanent collection of all private messages as the default. State the daily review window and capacity limit honestly. Pick a modest operational target rather than claiming immediate around-the-clock decisions. Plan how a person learns that a case is pending.

### Action 3 — Test cases and capacity

Apply your rule to Q: a quoted offensive claim clearly criticized in surrounding text; H: repeated targeted messages after a stop request; and T: an apparently credible imminent threat that needs urgent assessment. No actual wording is supplied or required. For each, state the initial protection, notice and review route without exposing a reporter or deciding legal guilt.

Calculate today's review minutes, then open [the later packet](later-packet.md) for a demand surge and accessibility failure. Revise one operational weakness. A triage scheme can prioritize harm; it cannot create staff time or turn an acknowledgment into a decision. Keep unresolved coverage and legal-review needs visible.

Complete [fresh checks](check-prompts.md), then [the key](check-answers.md). Compare policy intent with actual usability, not just how balanced the prose sounds.

## Adapt and transfer

Discuss through text or voice and use case labels rather than reproducing abuse. A supported route evaluates a small club's rule, one lens at a time. A harder route adds a reporter who fears retaliation and an appellant seeking evidence: explain what can be disclosed enough to challenge the decision without exposing the reporter unnecessarily. Mark unresolved conflicts rather than promising perfect transparency and perfect secrecy at once.

For real transfer, inspect one platform's published rule, notice and actual appeal entry point without submitting a fake report. Identify a concrete barrier and propose a correction through an authorized feedback route if desired. Actual adoption requires affected-user input, applicable legal/institutional review and practical testing.

**Supportive:** A proportionate rule survives cases, access checks and realistic capacity. **Mixed:** Rights improve but backlog or off-hours coverage remains unresolved. **Contradictory:** Safety becomes unlimited surveillance or expression becomes dismissal of targeted harm. **Inconclusive:** A preferred slogan has no implementable notice, review or staffing process.
