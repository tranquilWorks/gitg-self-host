# 15.03 — Situational awareness

## Notice what changes a useful action

Situational awareness means noticing relevant conditions, understanding their practical implications and updating when they change. It does not require constant scanning, predicting a stranger's motives or turning an ordinary visit into surveillance. A short orientation that helps you move, find assistance and return to your purpose is more useful here than an elaborate story about hidden threats.

Allow 25 minutes for the fictional library route exercise. Use the written layout below and an accessible editor or paper. No public visit, photograph, following of people or confrontation is required. The optional real route uses a place you already visit safely and ends if it increases distress or interferes with ordinary functioning.

## Observation, interpretation and response

“A queue extends into the passage” describes an observable condition. “The people in the queue are dangerous” adds an unsupported interpretation. You can ask staff for a clear route without deciding why anyone is there. Appearance, language, disability or unfamiliarity is not a threat category. Relevant behavior and environmental conditions should carry the decision.

[HSE's slips-and-trips overview](https://www.hse.gov.uk/slips/preventing-overview.htm) discusses obstructions, walkway suitability, lighting and maintenance. That supports noticing concrete movement hazards; it does not validate this exercise as a violence-prediction method. Emergency exits and responses in a real building depend on its actual signs, staff instructions and emergency plan.

Worked example: you notice a wet patch near the usual door and a staff member directing people to another entrance. The useful response is to avoid the wet area, confirm an appropriate alternative if needed, and report the hazard to staff if they are unaware. Investigating who spilled it adds little to getting safely to your destination. An alternate route must also fit your access needs; “there is another door” does not settle that.

## Complete fictional layout

Nia visits Alder Reading Center to collect a reserved book. Nia needs a level route without stairs. All route information below is invented and complete for the initial case; it is not an actual building safety assessment.

| Landmark / route | Initial visit facts |
| --- | --- |
| South entrance | Level entrance from the public pavement to the lobby |
| Help desk | Staffed point in the lobby, visible from the entrance |
| East corridor | Level connection from lobby to reading room; usual route |
| West corridor | Connection from lobby to reading room with six steps; no level bypass on this corridor |
| Garden corridor | Another connection from lobby to reading room; level status and current opening are not yet known |
| Signed south exit | Leads from lobby to the pavement |
| Signed north exit | Leads from reading room to outside; its suitability for Nia's emergency access needs is not yet verified |
| Ordinary condition | A short queue near the desk leaves the east corridor clear |
| People | One visitor looks around at signs; no threatening action or blocked route is observed |

No timer determines whether Nia is “aware enough.” The route goal is to reach the reading room through a confirmed suitable path, find a help point and then collect the book. An emergency-route question should be checked with staff; it must not be resolved by trying doors or entering restricted areas.

### Action 1 — Orient once and separate facts

Write a compact orientation: entrance, usual path, help point, signed exits and one relevant condition. Mark north-exit suitability and garden-route status unknown. Record the visitor's observed behavior separately from any interpretation; there is no evidence here for treating the person as a threat.

Choose the initial ordinary route using the supplied facts. East is confirmed level and open. West has steps and does not meet Nia's requirement. Garden is unknown. Then explicitly return attention to the purpose: collecting the book. You are not required to watch people throughout the visit.

### Action 2 — Compare the changed condition

At the second fictional visit, a posted sign says, “East corridor closed for maintenance. Use west corridor.” No other route detail has yet been confirmed. The desk remains staffed. The west route's steps remain unchanged. A second visitor is unfamiliar to Nia but does nothing that blocks access or threatens anyone.

Write what changed and what did not. The sign provides a general diversion, but it does not remove the steps or establish suitability. Ask the desk: “I need a level route to the reading room. The east corridor is closed; is the garden route open and level today, or is there another way to collect my book?” Do not enter the closed corridor to check whether the warning really applies.

Set proportionate response thresholds: a minor access ambiguity prompts a staff question; no suitable route prompts another service option or leaving the visit; an actual immediate hazard ends the exercise and calls for the building's emergency response or local help as appropriate. Discomfort can justify choosing another place without requiring an accusation about a stranger.

### Action 3 — Check whether the chosen response works

Save your question and route decision before opening [the later packet](later-packet.md). Use each branch separately. A staff suggestion is evidence about a possible route; the packet's supplied traversal outcome establishes whether it worked for Nia in that fictional event. Keep your own actual performance separate from the supplied story.

Record `observed change | question/action | confirmed outcome | unknowns | return to ordinary activity`. If the route fails or the person cannot continue comfortably, revise the plan rather than declaring success because a direction was given. A safe alternative such as desk pickup may meet the visit's goal without entering the reading room.

Attempt [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) before [the key](check-answers.md). Your output is a two-visit comparison, suitable-route reasoning and a proportionate action. It should contain no dossiers, photos or identity judgments about other visitors.

## Adapt the observation burden

Use a companion, spoken directions, a tactile or accessible building map, or a quieter familiar setting. Asking staff is a legitimate strategy, not a failure of independence. If repeated scanning increases anxiety or distracts from life, reduce the task to one planned orientation or use the paper case; this exercise is not treatment for anxiety or hypervigilance.

Harder route: garden access is unconfirmed and the desk cannot establish a level route today. Do not turn “unknown” into “probably fine.” Ask for a reachable pickup alternative or postpone. A successful visit may mean choosing not to enter a place whose access requirements cannot currently be met.

For real transfer, during two ordinary safe visits notice the same functional landmarks and one relevant condition. Record a change or honestly record none. Ask a staff question if needed, take a small proportionate action and check whether it helped. Do not force a second visit, create a hazard or look for suspicious people to make the exercise eventful.

**Supportive:** A meaningful change is noticed, a suitable response checked and attention returns to the visit. **Mixed:** Access is resolved but repeated scanning disrupts the activity. **Contradictory:** Strangers are followed, photographed or confronted based on appearance. **Inconclusive:** Imagined threats replace observations or a suggested route is called tested without a result.
