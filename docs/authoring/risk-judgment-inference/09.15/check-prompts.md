## Check A — Same observations, opposite causal worlds

Four fictional learners have these observed binary outcomes. T = 1 means attendance; T = 0 means no attendance. Y1 is the potential outcome with attendance and Y0 without it.

| Learner | T | Observed outcome | Unobserved value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | 1 | Y1 = 1 | Y0 unknown |
| b | 1 | Y1 = 0 | Y0 unknown |
| c | 0 | Y0 = 1 | Y1 unknown |
| d | 0 | Y0 = 0 | Y1 unknown |

Calculate the observed completion proportions for attenders and nonattenders. Fill the four unknown binary values to create one possible world with a positive average of Y1−Y0 across all four learners and another with a negative average. Keep every observed value fixed. Can the observed difference alone determine the sign of the average causal effect? What assumptions or design information would be needed to go further?

## Check B — An offer is not the same as attendance

In a fictional adequately governed design, a support offer is randomly assigned to 40 people, with 40 assigned no offer. All 80 outcomes are measured using the same rule. In the offer group, 24 attend and 28 complete. In the no-offer group, 8 access similar support elsewhere and 24 complete. The packet gives only these group totals, not individual outcome-by-attendance records.

Compute the completion difference by original assignment. What intervention does that comparison concern? Can you calculate completion among attenders from these totals? Does the comparison establish that compelling every nonattender would help? Identify two possible unintended consequences of such a mandate and one appropriate next investigation. State remaining sampling and finite-study uncertainty even though assignment was randomized.
