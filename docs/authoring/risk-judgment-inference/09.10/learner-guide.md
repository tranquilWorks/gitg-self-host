# 09.10 — Risk assessment and precaution

## The skill and its boundary

Choose a usable safeguard for a specified hazard, explain the remaining uncertainty, and change or stop when the conditions no longer support proceeding. Precaution is neither a promise of zero risk nor a reason to accept avoidable harm because a score looks small.

**Canonical scope:** Estimate severity, likelihood, exposure, detectability, controllability, and mitigation without demanding zero risk.

**Deliverable:** A six-dimension risk record, a chosen method, an owner and verification step for each safeguard, and a three-event proceed/change/hold review. The default takes roughly 35 minutes and is entirely on paper. Do not stage a physical obstruction, lift a test load, or expose anyone to harm. Use words, cards, dictation, or a calculator. All required conditions and events are supplied.

The introductory identify–assess–control–review approach is consistent with the limited background in [R1](../SOURCES.md). That workplace reference does not turn this fictional exercise into a professional risk assessment, legal compliance finding, or permission to operate equipment.

## Six different questions

A **hazard** is something that could cause harm; the **harm** is the adverse consequence. An obstructed view is not itself an injury. Specify the causal route from the condition to the possible consequence and who would bear it.

**Severity** asks how bad a credible consequence could be, not just what happened last time. **Likelihood** asks how plausible that consequence is under the stated conditions. **Exposure** asks how often or how long a person encounters the condition. A low frequency of encounters is not the same as a low probability of harm per encounter.

**Detectability** asks whether there is a useful warning early enough to act. Seeing a dropped item afterward is detection, but not necessarily prevention. **Controllability** asks which conditions you can actually change, with what authority and capability. Another person's movement is not yours to command. **Mitigation** asks what measure changes the route to harm and what remains afterward. Sometimes a measure lowers probability, sometimes consequence, and sometimes only the time to recover.

Use reasoned descriptions: “occupancy not known; need a fresh check,” not a made-up probability. Multiplying ordinal labels such as 2 × 3 × 4 can create numerical precision without a defensible measurement scale. Keep the dimensions visible even when an organization has a legitimate specialized rating system. This exercise does not replace that system.

## A different worked example: keep paper away from a drink

At a personal desk, a closed drink container stands beside draft paper. Moving the container to a stable separate part of the desk changes the contact opportunity. A waterproof sleeve would limit damage if contact occurred. Looking at the desk before starting supplies detection. These are different controls, not three interchangeable labels.

Record the owner, permission, and check: “I may relocate my own closed container; verify that the new position is stable and does not obstruct anything.” A control that moves it onto an unstable edge creates another problem. One spill-free session shows that no spill was observed in that session; it does not establish a zero spill probability. This example is not an instruction to work near powered equipment or hazardous liquids.

## Complete fictional route packet

You are planning to move **12 paper tokens** from station S to station D. Simulate only; the room and tokens below are imagined. The purpose is to practice decisions, not manual handling.

There are two possible routes: **S–A–B–D** and **S–C–D**. Route A passes a narrow shared passage. Route B includes an opening through which another person might enter. Route C is a longer, designated alternative passage even though the map uses fewer named segments. Segment count is not physical distance.

| Record | Current supplied condition | What you may control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V | A tall imagined stack hides the route ahead; two smaller stacks preserve the view | Choose a lower stack or a different method; no prescribed weight limit is supplied |
| A | An assistive device is parked in the narrow passage; you have no permission to move it | Do not move it or block its user's access; choose another route or defer |
| B | Occupancy beyond the opening is not yet checked | Request or make an appropriate fresh check; do not treat unknown as empty |
| C | The alternative passage is currently confirmed clear and available to you | Use it only while that confirmation remains applicable; keep it available to other users |
| P | You may pause at S or D; no essential deadline is threatened by deferring | Pause, use an authorized alternative, or do not move the tokens now |

There is no instruction to move a real object, assess a person's medical capacity, or determine a generally safe load. In a real task, a comfortable amount depends on the actual person, environment, and established procedures. Unsupported capability is a reason to change the task, not to test tolerance.

### Action 1 — Make a record that does not hide unknowns

For V, A, and B, write:

`Condition → possible harm → affected people | severity with reason | likelihood with reason or unknown | exposure | warning and response time | authority/capability | existing and additional control | remaining concern`

Also consider the route-C method: the alternative removes some exposures but may introduce a longer journey or a conflict with another user's access. Do not claim it is risk-free just because the supplied initial check is favorable. Identify which concerns are established facts in the packet and which are possible consequences.

A useful entry explains a mechanism. “Danger, medium, be careful” does not tell anyone what to do. For unknown occupancy, distinguish information missing now from a situation known to be occupied. Both can prevent an immediate move, but the next steps differ.

### Action 2 — Choose a method with executable safeguards

Compare at least two methods: lower visible stacks on an authorized clear route; keep the tokens at S and arrange a later permitted transfer; or another method justified by the supplied facts. Do not choose the narrow route by assuming permission to relocate the assistive device.

For the selected method, fill:

`Safeguard | specific exposure or consequence changed | responsible role | permission | check before proceeding | change/hold condition | possible new burden`

Example stop language must be observable: “Hold if the route becomes occupied, permission is missing, or a needed check is unavailable.” Avoid “stop when unsafe” without explaining what the person can notice. A control must actually be in place, not merely on a list. Record which action needs another person's authority and therefore cannot be completed by your plan alone.

Use **proceed** only for the next bounded simulated move under satisfied conditions, **hold** when you must wait or obtain a missing condition, and **change** when a different authorized method is available. None of these labels is permission for a real hazardous task.

### Action 3 — Test the decision rule, not the harm

Save the method and thresholds, then apply them to these three events in order. No one needs to enact them physically.

| Event | New information |
| --- | --- |
| E1 | For this tabletop event the chosen method’s stipulated capability check is satisfied, its lower stack preserves view, and C is freshly confirmed clear and authorized |
| E2 | Before the next move, another user occupies C; there is a safe place to wait at S |
| E3 | C remains unavailable; the only alternative would require moving the assistive device without permission |

Record your decision and the specific condition responsible. Explain what would have to change before you could reconsider E2 or E3. A method that says proceed after any previous successful move fails to respond to changing exposure. A decision to hold is a successful safety decision when the condition requires it; it is not completed transfer work.

Complete the separate fresh checks before opening their answer file. Keep the record of the first method and any revision so a later reader can see why it changed.

## Exposure arithmetic without pretending to know real probabilities

This optional mathematical toy assumes independent crossings and an unchanged **1/10 chance of a harmless token mismatch per crossing**. It is not a probability of injury. With six crossings, the probability of no mismatch is (9/10)^6; at least one is 1 − (9/10)^6. The same assumptions with three crossings give 1 − (9/10)^3. Calculate both and state why doubling exposures can matter.

Do not transfer these invented probabilities to the room. Smaller stacks may preserve view but increase trips. That tradeoff needs information about both effects; the arithmetic alone cannot choose the safest carrying method. Correlated encounters or changing conditions invalidate this independence model.

## Accessibility, harder work, and transfer

A spoken route with labeled cards is equivalent to a drawn map. A helper may read conditions and record your chosen action without selecting it. No balance, carrying ability, or speed is being tested. Keep access supports rather than removing them to demonstrate independence.

For a harder analysis, identify a safeguard that depends on a person remembering a check and compare it with one that removes the exposure. List its failure condition and who could notice it. Do not assume a hierarchy automatically settles every real case; feasibility and new harms still matter.

For later transfer, select an ordinary low-consequence planning task you actually control and compare your proposed safeguard with what was usable. Stay outside electrical, machinery, fire, road, chemical, clinical, and other serious-risk activities. No real transfer is required to complete this fictional lesson, and a paper exercise cannot establish safe field performance.

## Interpret the result

**Supportive:** The record distinguishes the six dimensions, a specific safeguard is verified, and new occupancy changes the next decision. **Mixed:** The plan is sensible but relies on a check no one can perform or shifts burden onto an access route. **Contradictory:** Missing permission or warning is ignored, or no observed incident is called proof of safety. **Inconclusive:** There is a score but no mechanism, owner, verification, or usable threshold.

**Final review:** What changed the exposure, what remains unknown, and which new observation would invalidate proceeding? Completion is a bounded planning artifact, not a general safety qualification.
