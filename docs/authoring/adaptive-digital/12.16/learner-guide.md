# 12.16 — Pain literacy and safe response

## Describe the change before explaining it away

Pain is a real experience. A useful response considers onset, location, associated changes, ordinary function and any existing care plan. Intensity alone cannot tell you which tissue is involved or whether it is safe to continue. The purpose here is to communicate and choose an appropriate help route while preserving uncertainty, not to diagnose yourself from a checklist.

Allow 30 minutes with the supplied fictional records and an accessible notes tool. No exercise, pain provocation, medication change or personal health disclosure is required. Health and exercise content must be adapted to the person and does not replace licensed medical care. If you are experiencing a possible emergency, use local emergency care now rather than finishing a learning exercise.

## Six distinctions that improve a response

**Post-activity soreness** is a possible explanation for discomfort after unfamiliar effort, not an automatic verdict. **Acute injury** involves a new event or sudden change; severe pain or loss of function should not be tested repeatedly to prove toughness. **Chronic pain** persists or recurs over time and may need a continuing individualized plan. A familiar label never rules out a new problem. **Referred symptoms** are felt away from their source, so pointing to the sore area does not locate the cause with certainty. **Red flags** are concerning features that require an appropriate urgent response, not proof of a particular diagnosis. **Fear-driven avoidance** can restrict valued activity; the answer is a supported, appropriate plan rather than forcing a feared task or dismissing pain as imaginary.

The [NIAMS injury overview](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/sports-injuries) distinguishes sudden injury from gradually developing injury and lists functional warning signs. The [NHS back-pain page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/) distinguishes ordinary follow-up, urgent worsening symptoms and emergency combinations. Its emergency examples include back pain with new bladder/bowel changes, loss of feeling around the genitals/anus, symptoms in both legs, chest pain, or a serious accident. These examples are not an exhaustive screening tool. Absence of a listed item does not establish safety. Use the equivalent local service; UK service numbers in the source are not universal.

For a real concern, communicate the facts and ask what activity is appropriate now, which changes require urgent help, and when/how to review. Medication choices and a return-to-activity progression belong to the applicable care process. Do not impose complete indefinite inactivity from fear, but do not apply a generic “keep moving” slogan to a possible serious injury.

## Worked example: a familiar label loses its authority

A person with a known recurring back problem develops a new symptom absent from their usual pattern. “It is always my back” is an interpretation, not a comparison. A useful record says what changed, when, how function changed, and whether the current plan covers it. New concerning features can require a different route even when the previous plan worked well. This is a communication principle, not a diagnosis of the new symptom.

## Complete material: three first-contact cards

| Card | Supplied observations | Existing plan / missing information |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A — Imani | General thigh aching the morning after an unfamiliar outing; no sudden incident reported; can perform usual ordinary activities; symptoms described as mild | No assessment of cause; no other symptom information supplied; wants to repeat the unfamiliar outing immediately |
| B — Devon | Ankle twisted during an outing today; sudden severe pain; cannot bear weight normally | No examination or diagnosis; another person proposes walking on it repeatedly to see whether it is serious |
| C — Kit | Recurring pain for four months under an individual care plan; familiar discomfort has increased; concerned about giving up an enjoyable weekly activity | Existing plan says use its previously agreed lower-demand option for a familiar flare, contact the team for new/changed features, and review function the following day; whether any new features are present has not yet been checked |

The supplied facts deliberately do not support a diagnosis. Kit's lower-demand option is already known to Kit, not taught by this page and not a prescription for a reader.

### Action 1 — Make an observation-first note

For each card write `onset/event | location/description | function | associated changes known/unknown | existing plan | question needing assessment`. Use the actual words and mark unknowns. Then state a possible concept **and** why it remains uncertain. A could be post-activity soreness; the timing alone does not prove it. B has concerning acute functional loss. C is persistent pain with a possible familiar flare, pending a check for changes.

### Action 2 — Rehearse a proportionate response

For A, do not prescribe another unfamiliar effort as a diagnostic test. Describe a conservative choice within ordinary familiar activity, ask about concerning changes, and seek advice if symptoms worsen, persist or cause concern. For B, stop repeatedly loading the ankle and seek prompt medical evaluation; severity, transport and local urgent/emergency advice determine the practical route. For C, check whether the existing plan still applies; new concerning symptoms supersede the familiar-flare branch. Do not infer that fear makes the pain false or that reassurance is treatment.

Say or type a short message for B using the six headings. It must communicate the functional loss and request appropriate evaluation without claiming a sprain or fracture. Save the three responses before [the later packet](later-packet.md).

### Action 3 — Let new information change the route

Read the later records and retain what was actually done, what was merely requested, and what remains unknown. For Kit's two alternative branches, choose separately; do not blend their facts. Produce one question about ongoing activity and one about the review trigger. Attempt [the fresh checks](check-prompts.md), then use [the corrective key](check-answers.md).

A complete output contains three observation notes, three proportionate responses, the spoken/typed handoff and the later revision. A polished diagnostic label with no appropriate response is not success. Sending a request does not establish that evaluation occurred.

## Access, harder cases and transfer

Use a body diagram, a communication aid, interpreter or trusted support person. Describe personal changes from baseline rather than comparing pain tolerance with someone else. Keep private health details out of public practice records; case labels and response categories are enough.

Harder case: two reports use the same pain number. One follows familiar mild aching with usual function; the other follows an accident with new functional loss. Explain why the number cannot select the same route. Include the possibility of referred symptoms without speculating about the organ or tissue involved.

For later real transfer, use the note at an appropriate care conversation if a concern actually exists, preserve the received advice, and record the agreed follow-up. Without a concern, rehearse an unseen fictional card rather than manufacture symptoms. This differs from 12.10's load/recovery accounting: the focus here is symptom uncertainty, communication and changed help routes. A case exercise cannot certify triage skill, recovery or safe physical capacity.

**Supportive:** The response follows the observed change and actual guidance, with uncertainty and later review retained.

**Mixed:** The initial response was reasonable, but new information requires escalation or revision.

**Contradictory:** Pain is repeatedly provoked, dismissed as psychological, or a chronic label overrides emergency features.

**Inconclusive:** Categories are memorized, but no communication, appropriate route or follow-up is demonstrated.
