# 10.10 — Teach a small idea and let the learner’s application test the explanation

Adapt language, examples, sequence and practice to another person’s starting knowledge, then revise the explanation based on what they can actually do.

A willing learner’s new application is evidence about this explanation, not a judgment of their intelligence. Self-rehearsal is preparation and does not prove teaching success.

Default lesson: locate a cell on a three-by-three grid. Label columns A, B, C from left to right and rows 1, 2, 3 from bottom to top. The learner should find B2, then an unmodeled cell such as A3. Use paper, tactile squares or accessible row-and-column descriptions. Invite an adult who is willing to try; do not recruit a dependent person under pressure.

Ten minutes to prepare, a fifteen-minute willing learning session and ten minutes to revise.

## 1. Check starting knowledge and choose a usable representation

Ask whether the learner already uses letter–number coordinates and what format is accessible. State the goal as locating a new cell, not repeating a definition. Prepare one worked example, B2, and one contrasting example, B3, so the row difference is visible. Choose ordinary language for column and row and ask permission to continue before launching into a long explanation.

- The learner’s starting knowledge and access needs are asked
- The goal is an observable application
- A worked and contrasting example are prepared
- Participation and pacing remain voluntary

## 2. Explain briefly and give the learner the next move

Demonstrate B2 by finding column B and then row 2. Ask the learner to locate A3 without pointing to the answer or saying yes before they commit. Invite them to describe the step that felt uncertain. Distinguish a language, layout or prior-knowledge barrier from a failure to understand the coordinate rule; do not infer the cause from one mistake.

- The explanation uses a clear ordered demonstration
- The learner attempts an unmodeled example
- Prompts do not give away the answer
- Observed difficulty is explored without labeling the learner

## 3. Revise the explanation and test another new example

Change the part linked to the confusion, such as explicitly naming the bottom row as 1 or using a tactile reference edge. Have the learner locate C1 and explain how they found it. Record whether the revised instruction helped, what support was used and what remains unclear. With no willing learner, rehearse both versions and mark learner application untested rather than inventing a successful student.

- The revision addresses an observed barrier
- A second new example tests the revised explanation
- Support and remaining confusion are recorded
- Rehearsal is not substituted for another person’s application

Use speech, sign, AAC, tactile grids or enlarged text according to the learner’s preference. Remove unnecessary speed demands while keeping the coordinate task. Protect privacy and never record a learner without clear permission.

Teach one grid rule and let a fresh move test the explanation

Learning guide — unscored

Application tests the explanation better than agreement

A bounded teaching cycle asks what the learner already knows and which representation is usable, states an observable goal, demonstrates one case, withholds the answer to a fresh case, and revises the explanation from the learner’s action. Polite agreement, repetition and teacher confidence do not show application. A mistake is evidence about the explanation, task and supports—not the learner’s intelligence or character. Self-rehearsal prepares teaching but cannot become another person’s result.

Supplied three-by-three coordinate grid

Columns A, B and C run left to right. Rows 1, 2 and 3 run bottom to top. A coordinate gives column first, then row. Therefore B2 is the center cell and B3 is the top-middle cell. The first fresh case is A3; the post-revision case is C1. Use nine tactile or paper squares when a visual grid is not accessible. Orientation labels remain available; the learner is not tested on tiny print, speed or remembering an unstated origin.

Example on a different shelf label

A two-column shelf uses X and Y left to right and rows 1 and 2 bottom to top. The teacher checks that the learner understands “bottom,” demonstrates Y2 by choosing the right column then the top row, and asks for X1 without pointing. When the learner chooses bottom-left and says “X first, then row 1 from the bottom,” the new action supports the explanation. Repeating Y2 would test imitation, not the unmodeled rule.

Diagnose the explanation before diagnosing the learner

If A3 is wrong, first check whether Version A stated the row origin, order and labels clearly and whether the representation was accessible. Ask which step felt uncertain. Use a hint ladder only after a committed attempt: repeat the rule; name the relevant edge; then model a different example. Record the hint because a rescued response is not independent application. Revise one supported barrier and test C1, a new coordinate, rather than repeating the demonstrated answer.

Preserve the coordinate rule across accessible routes

Use speech, sign, AAC, tactile squares, large text or the learner’s chosen medium. Remove speed when it is not part of the goal. Participation, pacing and recording require permission and may stop without penalty; never recruit a dependent person under pressure. A helper may operate the medium while the learner chooses the cell. The supplied simulation supports planning and analysis only, not a claim of successful teaching with an actual person.

Revise the smallest evidenced barrier

If a willing learner applies C1 unprompted and explains the rule, retain the revision and test a different grid later. If a prompt was needed, keep that support visible and change only the linked explanation. If access blocked the action, change the medium without lowering the coordinate rule. If no learner participated, the next step is a consented session—not a harder self-rehearsal. One session says nothing about learner intelligence or general teaching expertise.
