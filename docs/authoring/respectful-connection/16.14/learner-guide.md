# 16.14 — Make a first visit considerate

Courtesy makes a shared situation easier to navigate. It includes arriving when expected, choosing practical clothing, acknowledging people, respecting their space and keeping small commitments. It is not a competition in polish. In this practice, prepare for one ordinary first visit, check a consequential uncertainty, then compare your preparation with what actually helped. Allow fifteen minutes to prepare, the visit itself and five minutes afterward.

## Separate respect from a familiar manner

The Emily Post Institute distinguishes its principles of consideration, respect and honesty from particular manners [S01](../SOURCES.md#s01). This is first-party etiquette guidance, not evidence that one social code works everywhere. Here, a manner earns its place through its effect in this setting. A greeting can recognize someone without a handshake. Attention can include looking away to process speech. Appropriate dress can mean clothes that permit safe participation, not expensive clothes or conformity to a gender expectation.

Read an invitation in two columns: stated requirements and your assumptions. “Closed shoes for the craft room” is different from “everyone probably dresses formally.” Requirements can also need clarification or accommodation; an invitation does not make an inaccessible rule reasonable. Ask about the practical purpose and alternatives. Do not buy an outfit to pass this exercise, or treat another person's accent, grooming resources, mobility or appearance as a measure of respect.

Punctuality involves other people's coordination, but delays are not always within your control. Plan from the requested arrival time, allow a reasonable buffer, and know whom to notify if the plan changes. Excessively early arrival can burden a host who is still preparing. Considerate repair means giving useful information and reducing disruption, not demanding that others reassure you about being late.

## Worked example: the rehearsal is not open yet

Jo is invited to a rehearsal starting at 18:00. The organizer says doors open at 17:50 and asks visitors to use the side entrance. Jo's usual bus would arrive at 17:57 with an eight-minute walk. That is too late for the start even though the bus arrives before 18:00. Jo chooses an earlier route, waits outside the preparation area until doors open, and checks whether the side entrance has steps. This is a coordination decision, not proof that Jo has made a good impression.

At the door, Jo says their name and that they are visiting, then asks where to put a bag. A person using a phone to communicate should not have to put it away to satisfy a blanket “devices off” rule. Jo reduces optional distractions while keeping needed access tools available. The useful question is whether attention can be shared, not whether attention looks conventional.

## Your case: first community workshop

This fictional invitation is complete: “Saturday, 14:00–15:30, paper-making workshop in Room B. Doors open 13:50. Free; materials and aprons supplied. Wear clothes you do not mind marking and closed shoes. No food in the work area. Ask Lee, the host, before photographs. Reply to Lee by Thursday if attending or if you need an access arrangement.” The main entrance has three steps; the side entrance's access is not stated. Your bus reaches the stop at 13:42; the walk takes six minutes. A later bus arrives at 13:55. You have suitable clothes already, use your phone for a written communication aid, and would like to photograph your own finished sheet.

1. **Prepare the visit, not a persona.** Record the stated time, place, cost, practical clothing, supplied items and permissions. Mark the side entrance as unknown. Choose the earlier bus: arrival at 13:48 leaves two minutes before doors open and twelve before the start. Ask Lee about an appropriate waiting place and usable access route if these matter to you. Output: a compact arrival plan with one actual question, not a guess about the building.
2. **Prepare a greeting and attention arrangement.** Rehearse “Hello, I'm here for the workshop; where should I put my bag?” Adapt speech or writing to your needs. Keep the communication aid available, silence avoidable alerts and ask how instructions will be shared. Do not promise uninterrupted eye contact. Output: a greeting and an attention choice that could work during the activity.
3. **Participate with permission.** Follow practical directions about materials and food, ask before moving another person's work, and request permission for the photograph without including other people or their details by default. A refusal leaves you free to keep a memory without a picture. Output: what you actually asked or did, with any unanswered permission left pending.
4. **Close and repair.** Return borrowed items, thank the host for something specific and fulfill any commitment you made. If late, briefly acknowledge the disruption and ask how to join without restarting the group. Open the [later packet](later-packet.md) only after saving your first plan. Output: a corrected plan and one assumption checked against the supplied information.

## Evaluate effects without inventing approval

After a real visit, ask a narrow welcome question if appropriate: “Was my arrival arrangement workable?” A volunteered response can tell you more than your own impression of looking composed. It still concerns this interaction, not your universal social competence. If no feedback is available, record the action and uncertainty. Do not translate a smile into proof that everyone experienced you as considerate.

A meaningful attempt may include an awkward greeting, a declined photograph or a delay followed by repair. A polished written plan with no visit remains preparation. Notice whether your behavior reduced confusion, respected an explicit request or created an avoidable burden. Avoid keeping private observations about the host's personality in the application.

For support, use a short written arrival card, travel with a welcome companion, request directions in an accessible format or choose a familiar venue for the first attempt. The task should not require shopping, touch or accent change. For a harder attempt, the host's routine conflicts with a needed access tool: explain the tool's function and seek a workable adjustment without apologizing for needing it. For transfer, try a different ordinary setting and identify one manner you changed because the context changed. Answer the [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) before consulting the key.
