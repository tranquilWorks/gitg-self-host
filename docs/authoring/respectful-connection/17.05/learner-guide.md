# 17.05 — Ask, receive and contribute without keeping an invoice

Healthy dependence includes being able to ask for a little help, receive what was freely offered and contribute when you can. It also includes noticing when requests repeatedly ignore another person's limits. In this practice, make one small direct request, use the agreed help and review both usefulness and burden. Allow ten minutes to prepare, the actual exchange and ten minutes afterward.

## Mutual care need not be equal in each transaction

Clark and Mills distinguish communal relationships, where benefits respond to needs, from exchange relationships organized around comparable repayment [S14](../SOURCES.md#s14). The inspected opening pages present a theory, not a rule that all friendships must follow one model. The distinction helps explain why immediate repayment can sometimes miss the meaning of care. It does not erase explicit agreements, permit exploitation or require anyone to offer unlimited support.

Giving and receiving involve different vulnerabilities. Giving can become controlling if help purchases authority. Receiving can feel exposing if you believe needing anything proves inadequacy. A direct modest request makes both the need and the choice visible. “Could you check this invitation for five minutes?” is clearer than repeated hints followed by resentment that nobody noticed.

A genuine no requires more than the words “no pressure.” Your fallback must remain workable enough that refusal is possible without blame. If the situation is essential care or a serious dependence, it may require a broader support arrangement rather than pretending one friend can freely solve it. This small practice excludes money transfers, unsafe lifting, passwords, legal decisions and other high-stakes favors.

Contribution can take many forms: attention, remembering, practical work, encouragement or presence. Unequal capacity over long periods does not erase dignity or automatically establish exploitation. At the same time, repeatedly demanding help, punishing refusal or denying agreed limits deserves attention. Look at the pattern and its effects without turning disability, illness or caregiving into a debt.

## Worked example: thanks does not need to close the account

Arun asks a friend to review a short invitation for clarity. The friend agrees to five minutes and points out a missing location. Arun uses the correction and says specifically what it helped: “You caught the missing room; people can now find us.” Arun does not immediately insist on providing five minutes of a service the friend did not request.

Later, when the friend mentions needing a second pair of eyes on a simple message, Arun may offer help freely if capacity allows. That offer need not be an invoice settling the first favor. Nor should Arun tell the friend, “Now we're even, so you owe me next time.” Exact accounting belongs where explicitly agreed, such as shared expenses; it is not a measure of each person's moral value.

## Your case: a small invitation review

The supplied fictional invitation says: “Join our free drawing hour on Saturday 28 November, 10:00–11:00. Bring nothing; materials supplied. Reply by Thursday at 18:00 if you would like a place.” You want a friend, Kit, to spend at most five minutes checking whether a newcomer can tell when and where to attend. You have not yet added the room. You need the review by Wednesday 18:00; if Kit cannot help, you can use your own checklist and ask the venue to verify the location. That fallback is less informative than a fresh reader but feasible.

You can later offer a short review of Kit's non-sensitive message if Kit wants one. No reciprocal task has been requested or agreed. This is not a request for Kit to design the event, manage replies or be responsible for attendance.

1. **Write a direct bounded request.** Include the artifact, two questions, five-minute limit and Wednesday cutoff. State that declining is fine and that you have another route. Output: a request that names the help rather than hinting at your stress.
2. **Notice your response to needing help.** Privately identify any urge to apologize repeatedly, pretend the task is trivial or promise immediate repayment. Then ask without making Kit manage that discomfort. Output: a short preparation note about your behavior, not a diagnosis of either person.
3. **Receive the agreed contribution.** If Kit accepts, allow them to perform the bounded review, consider the actual comments and thank them for their use. Do not argue away every suggestion to prove self-sufficiency or expand the job silently. Output: what help was actually received and what changed because of it.
4. **Read the [later packet](later-packet.md).** Separate the useful correction from an offer of extra work. Decide whether you need that work and whether you have agreed to it. Output: a revised invitation, explicit task boundary and remaining uncertainties.
5. **Review the exchange over time.** Ask whether the request was clear, the burden stayed within agreement and the help addressed the need. Consider a later freely chosen contribution only if useful and wanted. Output: one thing to preserve or change, without declaring the whole relationship balanced after one favor.

## Receiving well includes leaving choice intact

A friend may decline, accept less than requested or help differently. If that does not meet the need, acknowledge it and use the fallback. Do not punish the person by withdrawing ordinary warmth or making future invitations conditional on favors. A courteous request that receives a no can still be a meaningful asking attempt; it is not evidence that help was received.

For support, choose an artifact with no private content and a task small enough that both parties can understand the limit. Written requests can give time to consider. If language or communication support is needed, preserve the other person's ability to accept or refuse, not merely your ability to send the request. Solo rehearsal remains preparation.

For a harder attempt, you notice that one person repeatedly asks for more after agreeing to a limit. Address that pattern concretely: what was agreed, what extra work occurred and what you can now offer. Do not respond by creating secret tests or refusing all future dependence. If threats or coercion enter, use an appropriate safety route rather than this ordinary exchange.

For transfer, try a different low-stakes role: accept help if you usually give, or offer a specific useful contribution if you usually receive. Review the actual experience without insisting on symmetry. The [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) distinguish gratitude, boundaries and genuine mutual care from compulsory repayment.
