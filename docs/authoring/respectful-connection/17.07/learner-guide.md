# 17.07 — Make joining easier for one real participant

Hospitality includes the practical work that lets people enter, orient themselves and take part without performing gratitude or familiarity. This practice asks an authorized host or helper to identify one barrier, make a feasible change and ask whether it helped. It can happen in a public room or online; owning a home, buying food or hosting a large event is unnecessary. Allow twenty minutes to prepare, the gathering and ten minutes for feedback.

## Welcome is a sequence of opportunities

An invitation can be warm but incomplete. A person may not know the cost, entrance, transport options or whether arriving late is allowed. At the door, they may not know where belongings go or which activity is open to newcomers. During participation, insider stories, noise, inaccessible instructions or an expectation of rapid speech can keep them outside the group even while seated in it.

Notice outsiders without publicly labeling anyone as lonely, disabled or socially awkward. Look at the setting and ask a private practical question when appropriate. Someone watching quietly may be participating by choice. Inclusion should expand options rather than require everyone to talk, eat, disclose, compete or stay for the whole event.

W3C's event-access guidance covers planning, participant requirements and accessible activities [S09](../SOURCES.md#s09). This companion applies that planning idea to one small gathering. It does not certify a venue, guarantee universal access or replace a person's own report about what works. Accessibility involves more than announcing good intentions.

Map three stages: **invitation**, **arrival** and **participation**. Across them, consider entrance and seating, sensory conditions, communication, time, cost, food and transport. You are not expected to solve every barrier at once, but you should avoid hiding a known barrier behind “everyone welcome.” State what is available, what is unknown and how a person can ask privately.

## Worked example: share the rules, not a test of belonging

At a small game evening, new visitors are told, “Everyone knows how we do this.” A helper instead offers a short explanation of the first round and a visible summary of the rules. Visitors can play, watch or ask a question. The helper checks whether a demonstration would be useful without making one new person perform for the room.

A resource such as a spare rule sheet or a quiet seat becomes hospitable when people can actually use it. Moving someone to an isolated corner without asking may reduce noise while separating them from the group. Ask which option helps, and check the effect. Inclusion cannot be inferred solely from the host's effort.

## Your case: a free Saturday gathering

All facts are fictional. You are an authorized helper for a small game-and-conversation gathering on Saturday 5 December, 14:00–15:30, in Room G. Attendance is free and people may leave early. The east entrance is level; the west entrance has steps. The nearest bus stop is a six-minute walk away. Chairs and a quieter table are available. Simple written game instructions can be supplied. Snacks are optional; ingredient packaging will be available, but you cannot guarantee freedom from allergens or cross-contact. The host, Lin, accepts private access questions and can confirm venue details.

The draft invitation currently says only “Games, Saturday, everyone welcome!” A newcomer, Bo, has privately said that loud background music makes conversation difficult and that they prefer to watch a round before joining. Bo has not agreed to be introduced with a personal story. You control the invitation text and may ask Lin to turn off music; you cannot promise a silent room or alter the building.

1. **Map the joining journey.** Fill invitation, arrival and participation with the actual facts above. Note cost, time, transport, entry, food information, communication options and an access contact. Identify unknowns without diagnosing needs. Output: a usable invitation with truthful limits and a private question route.
2. **Choose one feasible barrier reduction.** Ask Lin to turn off background music and confirm the arrangement before advertising it. Keep the quieter table as an option, not an assigned place for Bo. Output: one authorized change, who will implement it and how you will check that it happened.
3. **Make arrival intelligible.** Offer a greeting, show useful locations and explain join/watch/break options. Ask before introducing Bo, and use only an agreed name or non-sensitive common interest. No one owes a biography, icebreaker performance or food consumption. Output: a brief orientation that leaves choices open.
4. **Support actual participation.** Offer the basic rules and a demonstration if welcome. Share available materials without taking over or assuming the newcomer needs instruction in everything. Respect Bo's preference to watch a round. Output: an actual option used or declined, not a claim that Bo felt included.
5. **Read the [later packet](later-packet.md) and ask about a missed barrier.** Use a narrow question such as “Was there anything that made joining harder?” Act on a feasible correction and keep unresolved issues visible. Output: changed condition, actual reported effect and a next adjustment.

## Inclusion does not require constant sociability

A hospitable group can contain quiet observers, early departures and people who decline an activity. The useful question is whether those choices remain meaningful and respected. Avoid declaring the event successful just because all chairs were occupied or most people laughed. A group may be comfortable for regulars while newcomers still lack the context to join.

For support, choose a short free gathering in a familiar accessible venue, co-host with permission or improve the written welcome for an existing group. A remote option may help some people while introducing connection or platform barriers for others; check rather than assuming it solves access. If no event or willing feedback is available, preparation remains preparation.

For a harder attempt, a change helps one participant but introduces a new burden elsewhere, such as a quieter location that is harder to reach. Reassess with affected people and the authorized host. Do not make competing needs a contest over whose presence matters more. For transfer, repeat the journey check at a different gathering and verify one actual improvement, while acknowledging remaining limits. The [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) focus on choices and practical access rather than the host's self-image.
