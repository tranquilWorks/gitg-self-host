# 17.01 — Open a small, welcome conversation

Connection can begin with an ordinary observation, a question about a shared activity and a little appropriate openness about yourself. The other person must remain free to answer briefly or not engage. This practice is one modest approach in a place you already attend, followed by a review of your intention and response. Allow ten minutes to prepare, a natural brief interaction and five minutes afterward.

## Curiosity has no conversion target

Before approaching, ask what you are trying to obtain. Wanting companionship is understandable. Treating the person as a lead, a confidence test or a source of private contacts changes the interaction. A useful intention is to learn something about the shared activity while leaving the other person room to choose. You are not trying to hit a conversation-length target or collect a new friend.

Epley and Schroeder's experiments compared expected and reported experiences of talking with strangers in particular settings [S11](../SOURCES.md#s11). The inspected abstract supports questioning some pessimistic expectations; it does not guarantee that any particular stranger welcomes contact or that more approaches are always better. Our consent and context boundaries are design choices, not a measured effect size.

A context gives you a starting point, not permission to interrupt indefinitely. With a **stranger**, begin from something plainly shared and avoid assuming familiarity. With an **acquaintance**, refer to a previous ordinary encounter without claiming closeness. With a **coworker**, respect work demands and power differences; service politeness may be part of a role. With a **neighbor**, leave home privacy and freedom of movement intact. With a **peer**, use the shared activity without testing whether they belong. You need one suitable route, not a requirement to approach all five groups.

Appropriate openness means offering a small genuine detail that fits the situation: “I'm still learning this game,” rather than demanding that the other person disclose first. It does not require sensitive family history, income, identity questions or a dramatic confession. Reciprocity can be light. A short answer may be all the person wants to give.

## Worked example: let the activity do some of the work

At a recurring drawing session, Lio sees another participant looking at the available pencils. Lio asks, “Have you tried these softer ones? I'm finding the darker lines easier to see.” The question and small self-detail both concern the activity. If the other person says, “Not yet,” and returns to choosing, Lio can say, “Hope you find one that works,” and continue independently.

If the person asks what Lio is drawing, a short reply and genuine interest in their work can continue the exchange. Neither branch is a verdict on Lio's worth. The quality lies partly in noticing what was actually offered, rather than escalating effort until the interaction resembles a successful performance.

## Your case: a shared puzzle table

All facts are fictional. You regularly attend a free library puzzle session. Before it begins, Sam is sorting edge pieces at an open table. You have seen Sam twice but have not spoken. Sam is not working for the venue. People may join or leave freely. No private contact information is provided. You also notice a staff member carrying boxes toward a closing cupboard and a neighbor on the way out taking a phone call. Those two moments are poor openings for a discretionary chat.

1. **Choose a person, moment and intention.** Explain why the open puzzle table is a more suitable opportunity than interrupting the staff task or phone call. Your intention is to share interest, not request a favor or prove likeability. Output: one context choice and a limit on what you will ask.
2. **Make an optional opening.** Rehearse “Mind if I work on this corner? I keep overlooking the edge pieces.” Leave room for a no and do not move Sam's pieces before agreement. Another natural opening is acceptable if it fits the supplied activity. Output: a brief opening plus a truthful, non-sensitive self-detail.
3. **Listen before adding a second question.** Respond to the actual answer rather than running a prepared interview. Ask a related follow-up only when engagement makes that appropriate, and share enough to avoid making the other person do all the work. Output: the offered response and your next contribution, or a courteous end.
4. **Open the [later packet](later-packet.md).** Follow the response that actually appears, not the more flattering one you imagined. Future contact is optional and should be mutually wanted and intended. Output: a continuation or exit decision with a reason grounded in the supplied words.

## Review your approach without grading the person

After a real attempt, note the opening, what the other person actually said and how you adjusted. Avoid diagnosing disinterest from a single glance or calling a reserved person unfriendly. You may have attempted the practice respectfully even if the conversation stayed brief. You cannot record a conversation as attempted if you only rehearsed it in your head.

If you enjoyed an actual exchange and a future opportunity fits, you may say you expect to attend the next session, provided that is true. Do not use “we should meet sometime” merely to obtain a warm ending, or ask for a phone number by default. Shared public participation can remain the whole relationship for now. Connection does not need immediate escalation.

For support, choose a familiar structured activity, rehearse in writing or use your normal AAC or language support. A greeting followed by a short shared task can be substantive; speed, eye contact and witty delivery are not requirements. An informed willing practice partner can help identify intrusive wording, but role-play is not real initiation.

For a harder attempt, the person is warm yet unavailable. Receive both facts without treating warmth as permission to override the limit. For transfer, try another welcome context and explain what changed because of the role: a neighbor's doorway is not the same as an open hobby table. Never create repeated approaches to someone who declined or use a subordinate's obligated politeness as evidence of mutual interest. Finish with the [fresh cases](check-prompts.md).
