# 24.05 — Business models, pricing, and unit economics

Determine what one sale contributes, how much volume covers fixed cost and whether payment timing leaves enough cash to operate. Allow thirty minutes for the supplied model and ten for the later cost change. You will produce an assumption register and bounded test, with demand and viability left open where the evidence stops.

## An attractive margin can hide a fragile model

A business model connects a recipient's benefit with a way to receive revenue and bear the costs of delivery. The user, buyer and payer can differ. A positive unit contribution is useful but does not establish that enough units can be sold, that acquisition is affordable, that customers return or that delivery capacity scales. A one-time offer and a recurring service have different support and retention burdens.

Separate variable costs, which change with the modeled unit volume, from fixed costs for the stated period and capacity. Contribution per unit is price minus variable cost. Break-even volume divides fixed cost by positive contribution, rounding up when only whole units can be sold. The SBA's [planning page, S10](../SOURCES.md#S10) supplies that basic formula. Its estimate is not proof of viability; all examples here are recomputed from original inputs. No source's incidental monthly conversion example is used.

If price equals variable cost, contribution is zero and no finite volume covers positive fixed cost. If price is below variable cost, each additional unit increases the shortfall under that model. Do not divide by a nonpositive contribution and call a negative or undefined result a viable sales target.

Cash conversion asks when money leaves and when it returns. Inventory, work in progress and invoices can require funding before collection. Retention measures whether customers return over an appropriate period; do not infer it from units sold in a single month. Customer acquisition cost needs a defined spend and a count of newly acquired customers, not an arbitrary division by every unit delivered.

## Supplied offer U8

A fictional label-formatting service sells one standardized label unit for **12 units**. A “pack” below means one such unit, not an extra sale. Per pack, materials cost **2**, delivery labor **4** and transaction or delivery handling **1**, totaling **7 variable cost**. Period fixed cost is **100**, covering the stated capacity of up to 40 packs. These are complete inputs for this simplified model only; tax, financing and other unlisted obligations are omitted. No real product, customer or selling price is being recommended.

At **40 packs**, revenue is **480**, variable cost **280**, contribution **200** and provisional operating profit **100** before acquisition spending and omitted items. Per-pack contribution is **5**, or about **41.67% of the 12-unit price**. Fixed-cost break-even is **100 / 5 = 20 packs**. This is break-even under these assumptions, not evidence that 20 customers exist.

A separate **30-unit campaign** produces **10 new customers** in the supplied period. Acquisition cost is **3 per new customer**. It is not 30/40 unless the claim is explicitly campaign cost per unit, a different measure. Those 10 customers need not account for all 40 packs, and the total number of customers or repeat purchase pattern is not supplied. Including the campaign once reduces modeled period profit to **70**; do not also subtract 3 from every pack as though all 40 were newly acquired customers.

## Compare price and timing, not just revenue

If price falls to **10** while variable cost stays 7, contribution falls to **3**. Fixed-cost-only break-even becomes **34 whole packs**, since 33 packs contribute 99 and do not cover 100. Including the same 30 campaign cost would require **44 packs** to cover 130 at a contribution of 3, which exceeds the stated 40-pack capacity. The lower price may or may not increase demand; the arithmetic does not supply that response.

For the original 12-unit, 40-pack case, assume all modeled costs are paid before day 30 and all customer receipts arrive on day 30. Opening available cash is **200**. Payments before collection are **280 variable + 100 fixed + 30 campaign = 410**. The projected trough is **minus 210**, an unfunded requirement. Positive 70 provisional profit does not pay those earlier costs. Do not assume an overdraft, customer prepayment or financing facility exists.

If all 480 is eventually collected as assumed, arithmetic cash after collection would be 270, but only if the earlier gap had somehow been funded and the costs of doing so were addressed. That conditional ending balance cannot make the unfunded plan feasible. A smaller batch, changed terms or verified funding would each change the model and require legitimate agreement.

## Design a bounded learning test

For a separate prospective pilot, suppose the owner has only the same **200** available and has not incurred the monthly fixed cost yet. A maximum **10-pack** pilot at variable cost 7 plus fixed cost 100 would require **170** before receipts, leaving a 30 buffer. It has **no campaign** and no assumed customers or sales. At most 120 revenue from ten packs would leave a **50-unit modeled loss** against those 170 costs even if all sold. State that possible loss before treating the pilot as acceptable learning.

The owner must decide whether that loss and effort are affordable and worthwhile; this lesson authorizes no spending. A paper redesign or a no-cost usability check may be preferable. The pilot is a candidate test of delivery effort and recipient benefit, not a profitable launch plan. Its fixed costs, scope and cash ceiling are explicit so “small test” does not hide a larger commitment.

## Produce the unit-economics brief

1. Identify the unit, recipient, payer, period and capacity. List every supplied cost and mark omitted obligations, demand and retention unknown.
2. Calculate contribution, margin, 40-pack profit and fixed-cost break-even. Include acquisition separately with the correct new-customer denominator.
3. Recalculate at price 10, both without and with the campaign. Compare required volume with capacity instead of assuming limitless scale.
4. Construct the dated original cash path and label the 210 funding gap. Do not represent projected negative cash as an authorized transaction.
5. Compare the 10-pack pilot, a paper redesign and stopping. State the maximum loss, what the test could teach and its unresolved permissions. Save the brief before opening the [later packet](later-packet.md).

For support, use a single-unit card and twenty counters before moving to the cash dates. For greater difficulty, change labor cost while keeping price and capacity fixed. An optional actual attempt uses only authorized redacted records or a genuinely affordable, consented test after relevant professional questions are resolved. Do not contact prospects, advertise, take payment or incur costs to satisfy the source exercise.

Evidence of progress would explain whether a real offer creates and captures enough value under its actual conditions, using artifacts and later observations. A forecast, a break-even number and a fictional campaign cannot supply repeat demand, retention, scalable delivery or commercial viability.
