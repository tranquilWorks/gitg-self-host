# 24.07 — Competition, monopoly, and market power

Compare three fictional booking services to distinguish useful success, network advantage and potentially exclusionary conduct. Allow thirty minutes for the comparison and ten for a remedy stress test. The product is a mechanism-based account with uncertainties and consumer effects, not a verdict about an actual firm's legality.

## Ask why customers choose and whether they can leave

A service can grow because it is better, because more participants make it useful, because leaving is costly or because rivals are unfairly blocked. These mechanisms can coexist. Concentration describes how activity is distributed across firms in a defined market. It is not meaningful to assign a market-share percentage without specifying the relevant product, geography, time and alternatives. The supplied case gives no such market measurement.

A network effect means that participation can increase value for other users. For a booking platform, more venues may attract bookers, while more bookers may attract venues. Switching costs include relearning, moving permitted data, rebuilding connections and changes to existing commitments. Some arise from genuine coordination or technical needs; others may be imposed to impede exit. An entry barrier matters when a new provider cannot readily overcome it, but identifying a barrier does not alone establish unlawful conduct.

The FTC's [monopolization explanation, S14](../SOURCES.md#S14) distinguishes market power and exclusionary conduct from success through merit. Its [network-economy speech, S15](../SOURCES.md#S15) offers historical conceptual discussion of network value and interoperability. The 2000 speech explicitly presents the speaker's views, not a current Commission policy statement. Neither source supplies findings about the three invented services below, and no legal market-share threshold is imported.

## Supplied booking comparison P8

Three services help community groups reserve meeting spaces. All service names, capabilities and reported behavior are invented. No actual competitor investigation, account access or testing occurs.

| Service | Supplied reason users choose it | Exit, entry and uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Clear availability, accurate confirmations and usable access information; the supplied sample has few correction requests | Customer-owned booking records can be exported; entry still requires building a reliable service. No market-share data or controlled quality comparison exists. |
| B | Many venues and bookers already participate, making matches more likely for both sides | Customer-owned records have a usable export in the supplied case. A new entrant would need to attract both sides; some users keep a manual booking option. Network value does not itself establish abuse. |
| C | Many venues participate, but the service blocks customer-owned record export and asks venues for exclusive listing | Switching may require manual reconstruction; rivals may lose access to venues. Scope, duration, actual effects and any justification for the restrictions are not established. |

Manual booking by email or phone is a possible substitute, but its staff time, coverage and reliability are unknown. A is not automatically best for every user because B may offer more relevant venues. C's reach may create real value while its restrictions raise separate questions. The comparison must keep those propositions together rather than defining every successful service as exploitative or every restriction as efficient.

## Work from conduct to consequences

For A, the plausible mechanism is quality. Ask whether the correction sample is comparable, whether access information is reliable and whether price reflects the benefit. For B, the mechanism includes a two-sided network: losing one side can reduce usefulness to the other. Export helps move records but does not instantly reproduce the venue network, reputation or training. For C, record a concern about lock-in and restricted entry without asserting intent or legal liability.

Who bears the consequence? Bookers may lose choice or spend time rebuilding records. Venues may gain demand while losing the freedom to use another channel. Workers may handle repeated manual requests. A new entrant may be unable to offer enough venues to compete. Consumers also care about price, reliability, privacy, accessibility, innovation and continuity; a nominally cheaper service that fails important bookings can reduce welfare.

Low prices alone do not prove predatory conduct. Nor does a large user base establish monopoly power without a defensible market and competitive evidence. Capture would require evidence that public or institutional rules are bent toward a private interest; none is supplied here. Exploitation is a concern to analyze through actual burdens and constrained choices, not a label to infer from revenue. Keep those missing facts on the page.

## Compare remedies with their own costs

A narrow possible response to C is a permitted, privacy-preserving export of the customer's own booking records. It could reduce reconstruction work while protecting other people's information. A broader interoperability proposal might preserve continuity across services but require common formats, maintenance and safeguards. Transparent cancellation or exclusivity terms could improve informed choice without removing the restriction itself. Independent review may be needed to assess actual conduct and justified limits.

None of these suggestions is a legal order, an instruction to bypass access controls or permission to coordinate competitors' prices, customers or bids. A remedy should address the mechanism, identify who has authority to implement it and test for new burdens. The goal is more useful, contestable choice without discarding legitimate privacy or reliability needs.

## Produce the three-case analysis

1. Map each service to observed facts, likely mechanism and important unknowns. Separate quality, network value, switching cost and restriction. Do not invent a concentration measure from the fact that three examples are listed.
2. Identify users, venues, workers and prospective entrants, and explain a plausible benefit and burden for each relevant mechanism. Include the manual alternative with its unknown costs.
3. For C, write the specific export and exclusivity questions an authorized qualified review would need to answer: scope, duration, affected data, actual switching burden, alternative channels and claimed justification.
4. Propose a proportionate response, its implementing authority and at least one failure mode. Explain why data portability alone may leave B's network advantage intact and why a blanket export can expose others' information.
5. Save the initial comparison and open the [later packet](later-packet.md). Revise the remedy when the supplied facts reveal a real privacy problem; do not use that problem as automatic proof that every restriction is necessary.

For support, classify one row at a time and use “known,” “inferred” and “unknown” explicitly. For a harder pass, imagine exclusivity lasting one event versus five years and identify why duration could alter effects without asserting a legal result. Optional actual transfer can use public terms you are entitled to inspect, with current sources and qualified advice where needed. No scraping, customer contact, internal data access or competitor coordination is required. A reasoned analysis plus a later evidence check could support progress; this fictional comparison establishes neither an actual monopoly nor the effectiveness of a proposed intervention.
