# 23.10 — Material and ecological stewardship

Follow one possession through its lifecycle, then carry out or simulate the smallest safe care action its actual instructions allow. Allow twenty minutes for the map and ten for a later use check. Your product is a decision that records waste, time, energy and effects on other people without inventing an environmental score.

## An owned item has a history and a future

A low purchase price can hide resource use elsewhere. Materials come from land or other inputs; manufacturing uses work and energy; transport adds distance and handling; use requires habits and sometimes additional supplies; care and repair consume resources; and eventual reuse or disposal has consequences beyond the owner's bin. The point is to make relevant consequences visible at the scale of the decision. A learner does not need to calculate a complete lifecycle assessment to stop treating disposal as the disappearance of an object.

Use six stages: obtaining materials, manufacture, transport, use, care or repair, and reuse or disposal. At each stage separate a known fact, an uncertainty and an actionable question. Time and money spent on upkeep are real constraints. So are access to tools, safe storage and the labor of a person who is expected to do the cleaning. A choice that saves money for the owner by imposing an unagreed task on someone else is incomplete stewardship.

The EPA's [materials-management overview, S04](../SOURCES.md#S04) explains why lifecycle measurement matters. Its tools were not run here. The exercise therefore makes no claim about kilograms of carbon saved, a universal number of reuses needed or the environmental superiority of one bag material. A decision to keep using a suitable owned item is a bounded practical choice, not a measured planetary result.

## Supplied item and care record B9

The fictional learner already owns an intact IKEA FRAKTA bag, article **206.111.08**. No purchase is proposed. Its actual public [manufacturer page, S05](../SOURCES.md#S05), read on 24 September 2026, instructs the owner to wipe it with a water-dampened cloth and says not to wash it. It also excludes food contact. The item in this case is used for dry books, with no sharp objects, unknown contamination or visible damage.

This is deliberately a **wipe-clean** example. A generic instruction to wash every reusable bag would conflict with these instructions. If using a different owned item for an actual attempt, inspect its own label and current care instructions first. If the instructions cannot be established, choose another safe item or retain the mapping exercise without attempting care. Do not repair load-bearing handles, treat contaminated material or use chemicals merely to complete the task.

The table makes the environmental scope broad while keeping the practical action narrow. The unknown entries are useful: they prevent the convenient fiction that an unmeasured impact is zero. Conversely, uncertainty about manufacture does not prevent a safe, permitted care action on an already owned item.

## Worked care and burden calculation

The supplied first attempt records **4 active minutes** to inspect and wipe the bag. A damp cloth already in use is used; water quantity and embodied resource use are not measured. No cleaner, new bag or repair material is purchased. After wiping, the bag is left in an unobstructed place until dry before storage. Drying elapsed time is not treated as active labor, and no machine drying or other unverified method is introduced.

The learner had predicted 3 active minutes, so the observed fictional burden is **1 minute higher**. “No new purchase” is a defensible cost statement within the story; “zero environmental cost” is not. The bag is placed near the shelf with the expectation that it will be remembered on the next book trip. That is a habit hypothesis which the later packet will test, not evidence that any disposable item has already been avoided.

## Make a care decision another person could understand

1. Name the item, ownership, current condition and intended use. Check its actual instructions and whether the proposed contact or load is appropriate. Keep a product recommendation separate from permission to care for an existing possession.
2. Create the six-stage map with knowns, unknowns and affected people. Include capital tied up in replacement, storage space, energy or materials that care would use, and who supplies the time.
3. Compare keeping and caring, safe repair if genuinely appropriate, borrowing or sharing with permission, and eventual disposal. Explain why one fits this item's current condition. Do not assume donation makes a damaged item somebody else's useful resource.
4. Record the care action, active minutes, materials and result. If performing it yourself, use your own observed record; if using B9, mark every observation supplied fiction. Stop if the item or task is unsuitable.
5. Specify the next use you will check and what counts as friction: forgotten placement, cleaning burden, damage or another person's inconvenience. Save the first record and open the [later packet](later-packet.md).

Before each use, check that the bag is intact, dry and suitable for the contents. Wipe it when ordinary dirt warrants care under the label. A torn load-bearing handle, unknown contamination or condition that cannot be safely restored triggers retirement from this carrying use and a verified local disposal or other appropriate route; do not keep using it merely to avoid replacement.

## Revisit the habit instead of decorating the claim

For support, make the map with one sentence per stage and select a safe intact item requiring no repair. A helper may read the label, but record that assistance. For a harder pass, compare a proposed repair whose cost and labor are known with replacement whose upstream impacts are unknown. State what information could change the choice rather than manufacturing comparable carbon figures.

In an actual transfer, another household member's task needs their agreement. Do not treat their labor as a free resource or change shared storage without checking access needs. Repeated use, a care log and a later condition or burden observation could support progress. One clean object cannot establish that a household reduced total consumption or that a purchase achieved ecological justice. Material stewardship remains a practice of proportionate, revisable decisions with effects beyond possession.
