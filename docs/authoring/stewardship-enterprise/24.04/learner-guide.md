# 24.04 — Customer discovery and problem validation

Test a problem hypothesis with three contrasting interview records before deciding what to build. Allow thirty minutes for the supplied interviews and ten for a narrower follow-up. The intended product is a changed offer linked to evidence, including evidence that the original idea is unnecessary for some users.

## Ask what happened before asking for endorsement

Discovery investigates a person's situation and current behavior. Selling asks them to accept an offer. Mixing the two can turn polite encouragement into apparent validation. Begin with a recent instance, the workaround, its consequences, frequency and who can decide to buy or change the process. A problem can be annoying but rare, common but cheap to solve, or experienced by a user who has no purchasing authority.

Predeclare what would weaken the idea. In this case, a satisfactory existing method and an infrequent, low-cost problem both count against a broad new tool. A third possibility is that the apparent formatting problem is actually an approval problem. If every answer can be interpreted as a reason to build the original proposal, the interview has no power to change your mind.

The SBA [market-research material, S10](../SOURCES.md#S10) supports direct inquiry alongside alternatives and demand. GOV.UK's [interview guidance, S16](../SOURCES.md#S16) supports neutral questions and concrete experiences with consent. These sources inform the method, not the results. The three complete records below are invented; they cannot be presented as actual customer interviews or real evidence of market demand.

## Define the initial hypothesis and questions

The proposed audience is volunteer coordinators who share event updates. The initial offer is a new app to create and distribute every notice. The hypothesis says coordinators repeatedly lose time because their existing notice tools are inadequate. Before reading the records, write two disconfirmers: a current method already works well, or problems occur too rarely to justify switching. Also identify the assumption that coordinators can approve a purchase; it has not been tested.

Use these five neutral questions, allowing clarification without pitching:

1. Tell me about the last time an event update had to reach your group. What happened?
2. What did you use, and how did you decide it was the current version?
3. What difficulty, if any, occurred, and what did it cost in time or consequences?
4. How often has a similar situation happened recently?
5. Who can approve a change to this process or spending on it, and what would they need to know?

An actual introduction would explain the purpose, voluntary participation, what notes are kept and the right to skip or stop. Do not record names, contact details or sensitive organizational records when a minimal anonymous note will answer the research question. Consent to an interview is not consent to receive marketing.

## Three supplied interview records D6

| Record | Recent event and workaround | Cost and frequency | Decision authority and implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Last Tuesday a weekly coordinator emailed a revised meeting time; three readers used an older attachment. The coordinator manually replied with the new version. | About 20 minutes on that occasion; similar version confusion in three of the last four weeks. | Coordinator can change wording but spending requires a treasurer's approval. A recurring version problem is plausible; purchase authority is absent. |
| B | Last month's coordinator changed a single shared webpage and linked it from the usual message. Readers used the updated page. | No reported correction time; one routine monthly update. | Coordinator can maintain the page without buying a tool. The existing method appears sufficient for this case. |
| C | At a quarterly activity, the coordinator waited for an approver to confirm the room; once approved, the existing template was easy to update. | Ten minutes of chasing on that occasion; no formatting difficulty reported. | Venue lead approves room facts; coordinator cannot bypass that role. The problem concerns approval timing rather than a new authoring tool. |

These summaries supply all five question areas. They are not quotes from real volunteers. The sample is tiny and convenience-selected; no representativeness, response rate or market size is established. The perspective of an actual spending approver is missing, as are organizers outside this small convenience group. Do not report “three of three want the app”: none was asked to purchase it, B supplies contrary evidence and C points to a different mechanism.

## Change the offer because of the evidence

A defensible revision narrows the target to coordinators with recurrent attachment-version confusion, like A. Instead of an app for all notices, propose a dated change note that directs readers to the organizer's existing approved current source. Preserve the approval process and existing channel. The intended benefit is reducing old-version use; the offer must not create a second competing source of truth.

The note's fields are event identifier, change date, what changed, current-source location and facts approver. Do not fill an absent source URL with an invented live link. For this supplied case the source is an approved fictional “current meeting notice” card. A prototype can be tested on that card without buying software or contacting anyone.

B should not be pressured into adopting the offer just to improve the success count. C may need an earlier approval deadline or an authorized backup approver, but those changes belong to the organization and require legitimate agreement. The new offer is therefore a hypothesis for A's situation, not a validated business model. Price, buyer interest, repeated benefit and ongoing upkeep remain unknown.

## Produce the discovery record

1. Save the original problem hypothesis, audience, buyer assumption and disconfirmers before interpreting the interviews. Keep “a new app” identifiable as an initial solution assumption.
2. Map each record to recent behavior, workaround, cost, frequency and authority. Mark estimates as reported figures in the fiction, not objective measurements you collected.
3. Identify which evidence supports, weakens or redirects the hypothesis. Preserve B and C in the record; do not recode them as potential buyers merely because they coordinate events.
4. Write the narrower offer and explain the exact evidence that changed it. Specify the smallest task that could reveal whether it helps, plus the permission and maintenance requirements.
5. Save a limited conclusion and open the [later packet](later-packet.md). Keep later prototype observation separate from the three discovery interviews and from any actual purchasing evidence.

For support, annotate one record at a time using the five question headings. For a harder pass, ask what would happen if A's treasurer refused any new ongoing cost. The appropriate response may be a free workflow change or no offer, not calling the refusal irrational.

Optional actual transfer means three willing conversations with current or likely users and an honest count of those completed. Two interviews cannot become three through reconstruction. Keep notes minimal, avoid pressured participants and do not send outreach from this lesson without authorization. Actual user or buyer evidence that changes an offer could support the canonical progress statement; this authored practice supplies method and contrast while leaving actual discovery pending.
