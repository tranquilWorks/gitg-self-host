# 23.09 — Family and intergenerational stewardship

Compare uses of a family resource that support present care and future agency. Allow twenty-five minutes for the allocation and teaching example, followed by a later revision. You will create a provisional plan with costs, rights and review conditions rather than a decision over another person's property or life.

## Separate benefit, ownership and control

Intergenerational stewardship includes children, elders, education, inheritance, shared assets and the practical teaching of money decisions. Each involves different rights. Being older does not establish incapacity; being younger does not make every preference uninformed. The owner of a resource may set a lawful offer's scope, but a gift can become controlling when affection, disclosure or obedience is demanded in return. The intended benefit should leave room for the recipient's considered choices.

Distinguish an immediate purchase from the capability it might support. A course may build skill only if the learner wants it, can attend and can use the learning. Maintaining a shared tool may preserve future usefulness but also commit someone to storage and upkeep. Cash may meet an urgent need or simply shift consumption across generations. None of these outcomes follows automatically from the word “investment.” Ask who benefits, who does the work, who owns the result and what can be changed later.

The CFPB's [family-help questions, S01](../SOURCES.md#S01) support making expectations explicit. Its [money conversation material, S03](../SOURCES.md#S03) supports developmentally appropriate discussion. Neither source validates the invented family's allocation or settles inheritance, tax, benefits or ownership questions. The linked teaching activities were not inspected or administered.

## Supplied family meeting F8

Mina owns **600 discretionary units**, available after her essential obligations. This is not a shared account or an elder's money. No transfer has occurred. Mina, younger learner Jo and elder Eli have voluntarily joined a discussion, may decline an offer and have not given Mina authority over their other resources.

| Proposed use | Cost now | Intended present and future benefit | Burden, rights and uncertainty |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Jo's chosen introductory course | 200 | A wanted learning opportunity with a small practice project | Jo would attend six sessions; schedule fit still needs confirmation; any offer is a gift, not a career choice |
| Eli's requested transport help | 150 | Access to an already chosen activity this month | Eli controls the activity and trip details; later transport need is unknown |
| Upkeep of a shared workshop tool | 100 | Preserve a useful asset for several family members | Ownership and permission to arrange maintenance have not yet been confirmed; safe work requires an appropriate provider |
| Uncommitted balance | 150 | Keep flexibility for a changed need | Mina retains ownership; nobody has a promised claim on this amount |

The three proposed uses cost **450**, leaving **150** uncommitted. This is a proposal with two unresolved checks, not permission to pay for a course or arrange work on a shared asset. Education, elder support and maintenance are all legitimate candidates; the numbers do not prove that this split is uniquely fair.

## Work through a choice that preserves agency

A controlling offer says, “I will pay for your course only if you choose the career I prefer and tell me all your spending.” Rewrite it: “I can offer up to 200 toward this course if you want it and the schedule works. You choose whether to accept. I do not need your account history or a promise about your career.” The rewritten offer still has a cost ceiling and purpose. Removing coercion does not require an unlimited commitment.

For financial teaching, Jo uses a separate **60-unit fictional practice pot**, not another 60 taken from Mina's 600. Three available choices are to spend 20 on a wanted activity, maintain an owned item for 15 and save any remainder for a self-chosen goal. Jo chooses all three: **20 + 15 + 25 = 60**. Jo's explanation is that enjoyment matters now, maintenance avoids losing use of the item and 25 starts a later goal. Another reasoned split can be defensible; the exercise tests explanation of consequences and tradeoffs, not agreement with Mina.

A useful teaching response asks what would change if repair cost 30 or the activity no longer mattered. It does not reward repeating an adult's favored allocation. If Jo cannot explain where the remaining money went, work the arithmetic together and record that help. A child-friendly discussion can use counters without asking a child to carry responsibility for household distress.

## Build the provisional stewardship plan

1. Identify the resource owner and separate permission to discuss from authority to transfer or contract. Keep inheritance expectations outside current available funds unless an actual qualified determination establishes them.
2. Compare all three uses and the retained balance using cost, near-term benefit, future agency, burden, ownership and reversibility. Do not hide maintenance labor or treat an older relative's preferences as irrelevant.
3. Write a conditional allocation totaling 600. Name the unresolved schedule and tool-permission checks, the person entitled to answer each, and what stays pending if the answer is unavailable.
4. Rewrite the controlling offer and complete Jo's separate 60-unit exercise. Record Jo's supplied explanation distinctly from your interpretation. State one different reasonable choice you would accept.
5. Set a review after those checks or a changed need. Preserve the first proposal, then open the [later packet](later-packet.md) and revise only what the new information supports.

For an optional actual attempt, discuss a small resource you genuinely own with willing participants. A private, redacted comparison or a conversation about a hypothetical amount is enough to practice. Do not seek account access, signatures, wills or estate documents. Real gifts, shared property agreements and inheritance plans may affect tax, benefits and legal rights; use an appropriately qualified local route before relying on a consequential plan.

## Look beyond one agreeable meeting

A polite response is not evidence that a plan strengthens future agency. Look for whether the recipient can use the resource, decline without penalty, explain a choice and revisit a burden. Ownership can remain with the giver before a transfer, while the recipient retains authority over unrelated life decisions. Those two facts are compatible.

For support, use the four table rows and mark each as proposed, checked or committed. For greater difficulty, introduce a rising need without enlarging the 600-unit pot. The first thing to revise may be timing or the uncommitted balance; an urgent request does not silently authorize disposing of someone else's asset. Over time, an actual allocation record plus a later use or teaching observation could support progress. The fictional meeting and teaching response here remain authored materials, not family agreement or longitudinal evidence.
