# 09.03 — Cognitive and motivational bias

## What you are practicing

Notice a specific vulnerability in a decision, install a countercheck that could matter, and actually use it. Naming a bias afterward is not the same as changing the procedure. Keeping your original choice can be a good result when it survives a fair check.

**Canonical scope:** Recognize confirmation, availability, anchoring, sunk cost, overconfidence, tribal reasoning, and self-serving bias.

**Deliverable:** A preserved initial preference, seven reasoned vulnerability dispositions, a performed countercheck, and a decision record showing what changed in the method. Allow about 35 minutes for the fictional packet. A live transfer needs an actual suitable choice; no purchase, attendance, or contact is required for the fictional route.

Use paper, a text editor, dictation, or a willing reader. Everything needed to complete the default rehearsal is supplied. These materials do not diagnose irrationality or establish that bias has been eliminated. They add no scoring rule or claim of mastery.

## Seven routes to inspect, not seven labels to attach to people

**Confirmation:** Preferentially seeking or crediting support for your favored answer. Countercheck: search for a concrete fact that would make that answer fail your stated criterion.

**Availability:** Giving a vivid or easily recalled example more weight than its relevance warrants. Countercheck: compare that example's time, setting, and coverage with the current decision evidence.

**Anchoring:** Allowing an initial number or framing to pull a later estimate without adequate adjustment. Countercheck: calculate from relevant components and examine sensitivity. After seeing the anchor, do not claim your estimate was unexposed or independent of it.

**Sunk cost:** Treating irrecoverable past effort as a reason to incur further costs, apart from future consequences. Countercheck: compare future costs and benefits. Past work can create a genuinely reusable asset; include its future usefulness rather than mechanically discarding all history.

**Overconfidence:** Expressing more certainty or precision than the evidence supports. Countercheck: name a plausible failure condition, missing information, and a response if it occurs. A confidence number alone is not calibration.

**Tribal reasoning:** Applying more favorable standards to a preferred group because it is yours. Countercheck: swap irrelevant group labels while preserving relevant evidence, track record, and constraints. Equal standards do not require equal conclusions when the evidence actually differs.

**Self-serving bias:** Applying an interpretation or standard that favors your own role without an evidence-based justification. Countercheck: consider the same conduct with the roles reversed and check whether relevant responsibilities and constraints are really comparable.

These are working descriptions for this exercise. They overlap and are not seven diagnoses. Mark a route **relevant**, **uncertain**, or **unsupported in this packet**, with a reason. Do not invent evidence merely to fill every row. The background sources and their limits are recorded in SOURCES.md; they do not validate this original checklist.

## A different worked example: continue an illustration or switch?

A person has already spent 90 unrecoverable minutes on a draft. Their remaining task is to produce a readable event sign. Finishing that draft would take 40 more minutes; a simpler alternative would take 15 and meet the same stated requirement. No part of the old draft can be reused, and there is no cancellation obligation or other future consequence in this toy case.

The countercheck is a future-only comparison: 40 versus 15, not 130 versus 15. Selecting the simpler route can fit the stated requirement, while the old 90 minutes remain recorded as past effort. If the old draft contained a reusable illustration that saved future work, that useful asset would matter. The point is not “always abandon work”; it is to identify what can still change.

## Complete practice packet: one community-event choice

All options, advertisements, and statements are fictional. This is a decision rehearsal, not evidence that you attended an event or changed a real-life choice.

### Initial card — record a preference before the detailed comparison

For the fictional decision-maker, the aim is to participate in one event **if** it fits these constraints: at most **70 minutes door-to-door**, confirmed step-free access, and no current personal need to rest instead. The decision-maker reports sufficient energy today. Once these conditions are met, prefer the option with less total future time. Staying home remains the fallback when participation cannot be justified under the constraints.

Event A is organized by a familiar club. An old advertisement calls it a “quick 35-minute activity.” The person spent 60 minutes preparing an A-specific card that cannot be reused or recovered. Event B has an unfamiliar organizer. A friend vividly recalls a noisy B event last year. Option C is to stay home.

Write your initial preference, main reason, and certainty in ordinary words. If you already read the detailed facts, record that exposure; do not claim a blind initial judgment.

### Current detail card — now use the complete supplied facts

| Option | Round-trip travel | Current session duration | Total future time | Step-free access | Other information |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 40 minutes | 45 minutes | Calculate | Not confirmed in this packet | Old advertisement omits travel and does not give the current duration |
| B | 20 minutes | 40 minutes | Calculate | Confirmed for this fictional session | Current notice offers a quiet area; actual attendance and noise are not guaranteed |
| C | 0 minutes | 0 minutes | Calculate | No travel required | Does not meet the participation aim, but is the fallback and rest option |

The 60 minutes of A-specific preparation have already occurred and cannot be recovered. There are no purchases, deposits, contractual duties, or cancellation penalties in this packet. The friend's story concerns a different event last year and does not measure this session's noise. Organizer familiarity is the only group-label difference supplied; no differing safety record, competence evidence, or trust history is provided.

### Action 1 — Preserve the preference and audit the procedure

Fill one row for each of the seven routes:

`Route | relevant / uncertain / unsupported | specific fact or missing fact | how it might distort this decision | suitable countercheck`

Inspect your own reasoning rather than declaring that everyone choosing A is biased. The initial card contains opportunities for several vulnerabilities, but a cue's presence does not prove it caused your preference. For overconfidence and self-serving bias in particular, evidence about your actual reasoning may be absent: an uncertainty or unsupported disposition can be more honest than a confident label.

State the actual decision rule before judging the preferred option. Preserve constraints you would apply to an unfamiliar organizer, and do not invent additional favorable facts for the familiar one.

### Action 2 — Perform one countercheck that could reverse the preference

Choose a vulnerability tied to a concrete reason you recorded. Write a finding that would make the preferred option fail, then perform the matching check using the supplied detail card.

For a time-anchor or confirmation concern, compute travel plus session time for each event and compare the result with 70 minutes. For a sunk-cost concern, keep the 60 past minutes in the history column but exclude them from future time; explain why no reusable asset or obligation is lost in this packet. For an availability concern, compare last year's anecdote with the current notice and keep the remaining noise uncertainty. For a group-label concern, swap only the organizer labels and apply the same constraints again.

Record the actual calculation, source row, or comparison. “I considered the opposite” without showing what you inspected is insufficient. An inaccessible or missing fact stays missing. Do not label unconfirmed access as confirmed in order to keep a preference.

### Action 3 — Decide, preserve disagreement, and distinguish the route

Apply the stated rule to the checked information. Record:

`Initial preference | initial reason | countercheck performed | result | final preference | unchanged or changed, and why | remaining uncertainty | appropriate next step`

A procedure can improve while the answer stays the same. Conversely, switching options does not prove a better procedure if the new choice is unsupported. Keep the actual reason for the decision visible.

For this fictional packet, write a decision rehearsal only. Do not claim real-world participation or bias reduction. For an optional actual low-stakes choice, record your real constraints and preference first, check relevant available information, change the method, and carry out a reversible next step when appropriate. Declining to act or obtaining missing information can be a reasoned outcome. Do not create a purchase, confrontation, or other commitment merely to obtain evidence for this exercise.

## Accessibility and advanced routes

Use a spoken checklist or inspect one route at a time. A willing reader may present anonymized option cards, preserve your first preference, and record your decisions without selecting for you. Record assistance. No time-pressure task is required.

For a more demanding route, identify a case where familiarity or past investment conveys relevant future information. Explain exactly which evidence should remain after a label swap or future-only comparison. Do not turn a useful countercheck into a rigid rule that discards expertise, duty, access needs, or legitimate relationships.

For longitudinal transfer, try a different actual low-stakes choice later. Preserve the initial record and compare procedures, not just whether the final answers changed. A single before-and-after result cannot establish a causal effect of this checklist or reliable judgment across contexts.

## How to interpret your result

**Supportive:** A specific vulnerability leads to a performed calculation or evidence check, both preferences remain visible, and the final action uses the same relevant standard across options.

**Mixed:** All seven labels are accurate but the decisive comparison is never performed, or contrary information is recorded and then ignored without justification.

**Contradictory:** The checklist is applied only to disliked groups, missing information is invented, or the user changes the rule solely to protect their preference.

**Inconclusive:** There is no initial preference, no visible countercheck, or only a simulated choice presented as a real one. The fictional route cannot establish the canonical real-choice progress indicator by itself.

This is not a substitute for qualified advice on high-consequence decisions. Keep the practice ordinary, reversible, and within your own authority.

**Final review:** What changed in your procedure? Which result could have changed your preference? Would the same relevant evidence receive the same treatment after a role or group-label swap?
