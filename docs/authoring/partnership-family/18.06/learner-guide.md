# 18.06 — Relationship stages and changing needs

Compare what a relationship needed before with what its participants need now, then design one small, revisable adjustment. Allow twenty minutes for the comparison, a willing adult conversation, and a review after a short trial. Partnership is optional; single life requires no substitute performance. Use the complete fiction when no suitable relationship or welcome conversation exists. Do not initiate contact with an unsafe or estranged person for this exercise.

## Understand change without imposing a timetable

Formation involves learning whether and how to relate. Commitment makes particular promises. Consolidation develops habits and shared arrangements. Strain exposes pressure or mismatch; repair addresses a rupture; renewal creates a wanted way forward. Ending or restructuring may become appropriate. These are organizing words, not diagnoses or seven compulsory steps. A couple can consolidate a home while repairing a breach and reconsidering future plans. Duration alone tells you little about what they should do next.

Changed needs deserve description before judgment. A new work schedule may reduce available time without reducing affection. Conversely, explaining a missed agreement by stress does not automatically resolve its effect. Separate capacity, conduct and fit: “we have fewer free evenings,” “you changed our plan without telling me,” and “we want incompatible futures” pose different questions. A pleasant new ritual cannot answer all three.

Adaptation asks what still matters, what is no longer feasible, and what both can freely accept. It includes time together, communication, affection, practical responsibilities and future direction. A shorter conversation may preserve contact while giving up spontaneity. Name that loss rather than declaring the new arrangement equally satisfying by definition. An experiment needs a review point so temporary scarcity does not quietly become a permanent entitlement to one person's sacrifice.

Renewal is not an obligation to remain together. Sometimes a trial shows that an arrangement is sustainable; sometimes it reveals an unresolved need or unwillingness to repair. Neither result establishes anyone's moral worth. Threats, coercion and abuse require an appropriate safety route, not a better couples exercise.

## Worked example

A couple previously enjoyed a weekly long walk. One now has a temporary evening class. They freely replace two walks with a twenty-minute tea and review after the class ends. Both report enjoying one tea; the second never happens. “A useful meeting occurred once” is supported. “Our new routine works” is premature. They should examine the missed meeting and the continuing wish for longer time, not add the two planned meetings to a success count.

## Supplied fictional task

Adult partners Neri and Sam have lived together for four years. Neri has an eight-week work placement, ending 29 November, with two late evenings each week. Sam has agreed to help a relative on Saturdays for the same period. Their previous Saturday outing lasted two hours. Both want contact and neither wants a compulsory daily report. They have not decided whether the Saturday responsibility will continue afterward.

On 4 October they willingly discuss a two-week adjustment. Available shared windows are Tuesday 19:30–19:50 and Sunday 10:00–10:20. Neri prefers Tuesday; Sam can agree to two Tuesdays. Sam also wants Neri to acknowledge a separate event: on 1 October Neri changed a shared dinner plan without notifying Sam. Neri has not yet addressed that breach. There is no supplied threat or abuse, no decision to end, and no evidence that either wants less commitment. Do not invent those conclusions.

## Build the comparison and trial

1. Draw five before/now rows: time, communication, affection, practical roles and future plans. Put the lost Saturday outing, new constraints and unknown continuation in the appropriate rows. Mark unprovided details unknown. Output: a comparison grounded in facts rather than a stage label.
2. Sort the issues into temporary capacity, specific conduct and possible longer-term mismatch. The unnotified dinner change belongs in conduct even if work was tiring. Output: two separate questions—what contact is feasible now, and what acknowledgment or repair the dinner incident needs.
3. Draft a negotiable proposal for 6 and 13 October, 19:30–19:50, with a review on 14 October. State that it replaces only the two outings, not all future needs or the unresolved repair. Each person can decline, shorten or suggest another arrangement. Output: a trial card with dates, preserved purpose, known loss and review trigger.
4. Save the card before reading the [later packet](later-packet.md). Classify each meeting as happened, moved by agreement, missed or unknown. Compare each person's feedback with the original purpose. Output: keep, revise or stop, with a reason that includes any unresolved conduct and capacity.

Do not score closeness by minutes alone. Twenty welcome minutes can matter while remaining insufficient for someone's longer-term needs. A completed meeting supplies behavioral evidence; the participants' separate experiences supply different information. Neither is provided by merely writing the plan.

## Support, challenge and transfer

For support, compare only time and one other relevant area, using a dictated note or visual calendar. Supply the dates rather than asking someone under strain to design an elaborate tracker. For a harder fictional attempt, extend the placement by four weeks and decide what must be renegotiated instead of assuming consent extends automatically.

For real transfer, ask whether a modest review is welcome, identify one actual change and agree a short trial. Review after a comparable opportunity with both perspectives if freely offered. Do not turn the review into a demand for reassurance. If no trial is agreed, retain the proposal as preparation and record no relationship outcome. Consequential housing, separation or care decisions may need suitable professional support.
