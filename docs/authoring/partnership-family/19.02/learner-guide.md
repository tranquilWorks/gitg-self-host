# 19.02 — Parenting and child development

Practice adjusting support during one familiar child-care routine. A live route requires an actual authorized parenting or caregiving role and consistency with the child's existing care plan. Without that role, use the complete fiction only; do not recruit a child to demonstrate this competency. Allow fifteen minutes to prepare and two brief opportunities within a week, up to ten minutes each with breaks. Parenthood is not a required life path.

## Security and growing competence belong together

Safety sets limits around hazards and necessary care. Attachment-related needs invite reliable, warm responsiveness; a clean room or fast response to an instruction cannot measure attachment. Development concerns changing capacities over time, including language, movement, planning, emotion and social understanding. Individual access needs, fatigue and familiarity matter now. A general age description cannot diagnose a particular child or determine what they should accomplish today.

Discipline in this task means guiding a manageable routine with clear expectations and support. It excludes physical punishment, humiliation, threats of abandonment and withholding affection. Predictable limits can coexist with choices: “the books stay off the walkway; would you like to put books or soft toys away first?” The child has an accessible decision inside a safety boundary, rather than a false choice between compliance and punishment.

Education and modeling include demonstrating a small action, thinking aloud simply and leaving room to try. Taking over at the first error can remove the practice opportunity; leaving a distressed child without help can turn autonomy into abandonment. Watch what the child can attempt and adjust the assistance. More independent action is not always today's right target: illness, tiredness or an access barrier may call for more support.

Growing competence is specific. A child who sorts three familiar books after one demonstration has practiced that task under those conditions. The result does not prove global maturity, parental quality or secure attachment. A child who declines or becomes frustrated supplies information about the situation; their dignity and entitlement to care do not depend on completing your exercise.

## Worked example

A caregiver says “tidy everything” while a tired child faces a large pile. The child freezes. The caregiver reduces the task to two soft toys, shows one placement and stays nearby. The child places the second with help. The narrower success is an assisted attempt after adjusting demand. Calling the child lazy or claiming independent tidying would both misread the evidence.

## Supplied fictional task

Jules is the authorized caregiver for seven-year-old Remy during an established afternoon routine. The existing care plan permits a brief shared tidy of familiar books and soft toys before story time. No lifting, sharp objects, medicines or unfamiliar child-care action is involved. Remy usually recognizes the book shelf and toy basket and sometimes needs a picture cue when tired. No developmental diagnosis is supplied or requested.

On Monday there are four books and three soft toys on a low table and floor area. Remy says they are tired and would prefer the story. Jules can spend ten minutes, can shorten the tidy, and can keep story time regardless of how much Remy completes. The walkway must remain clear for safety. Remy has not yet been offered a choice or shown the first step. A second comparable opportunity is available on Thursday; its outcome is not yet known.

## Prepare and observe the routine

1. Confirm role, care-plan fit and an ordinary safe setting. Note Remy's current tiredness, familiar cues and the specific safety limit. Output: a short preparation card about today's support, not an age-based rating or comparison with another child.
2. Offer an accessible choice between books and soft toys first, with a small clear next step. If speech is not the useful format, use familiar pictures or another established communication method. Output: the choice and Remy's actual response if provided; do not invent assent from silence.
3. Model one item, then invite a manageable attempt while staying available. Allow time and help. If frustration rises, pause or reduce the demand rather than escalate pressure. Maintain the walkway safety limit through the adult's appropriate care responsibility. Output: what was demonstrated, what Remy attempted and what help was needed.
4. Note specific effort or strategy without turning praise into a demand for performance. Preserve warmth and story time. Plan one adjustment for Thursday—fewer items, a clearer cue or a different prompt—based on the actual first opportunity. Output: a support hypothesis, not a promise of independence.
5. Save your plan and open the [later packet](later-packet.md). Compare only the supplied observations and the changed task conditions. Include Remy's words or mark feedback unknown. Output: what support seems useful and what remains untested.

## Adapt, challenge and transfer

For support, rehearse entirely with the fictional items on paper. A facilitator can model the adult's words without a child present. For a harder fictional review, make the second day more tiring and ask whether adding help is appropriate even if it reduces independence. Difficulty should increase the adult's judgment demand, not the child's distress.

For an authorized real attempt, choose a familiar, safe routine already within your role. Coordinate with relevant caregivers and follow existing disability, medical or educational guidance. Stop the exercise aspect when the child's needs require ordinary care. No public recording or identifiable child evidence is needed. Persistent developmental concerns, distress, suspected harm or uncertainty about care authority require suitable qualified local guidance. Two observations can guide a modest adjustment; they cannot validate parenting ability or diagnose development.
