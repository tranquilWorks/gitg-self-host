# 23.07 — Consumption, status, and enoughness

Decide whether replacing one ordinary possession serves a real need. Spend fifteen minutes comparing options, use a safe existing item on two normal occasions if you choose actual practice, then review the decision for ten minutes. The default is a fully fictional chair case with a separate supplied two-use packet. Fictional observations are never recorded as your actual experience.

## Ask what the object needs to do

A possession can provide function, comfort, beauty, cultural expression and social meaning. These benefits deserve examination without assuming that every preference is wasteful or every purchase worthwhile. Status pressure often appears as a prediction about what others will think; that prediction is different from a discomfort observed during use. A new chair might improve either, both or neither.

Enoughness means identifying a proportionate stopping point for this need under current constraints. It does not require the cheapest possible life or turn scarcity into virtue. Necessary accessibility, safety and comfort can justify spending. Conversely, a discount or attractive image does not answer whether an object fits the available space, solves the problem or creates maintenance work that outweighs its benefit.

The FTC's [comparison-shopping guidance, S14](../SOURCES.md#S14) supports considering total charges, condition and return terms. Only those educational comparison points are used here; no seller has been evaluated and no product price is current. The invented prices and observations below support a decision exercise, not a recommendation to buy a named chair.

## Complete original choice file E3

The existing chair is **safe, stable and serviceable**, with a cosmetic scratch on one side. It is suitable for ordinary seated reading; no structural defect is supplied. Do not use this case to justify sitting on damaged or unstable furniture. If an actual item is unsafe, stop the use test and choose a safe alternative; an essential replacement is not a failure of restraint.

The fictional owner wants comfortable twenty-minute reading, a tidy appearance in a small room and easier cleaning. They dislike seeing the scratch near the desk. A separate status story is, “A new chair might make visitors think the room is more grown-up.” No visitor has said this, and it remains a prediction. Before looking at alternatives, the owner sets a **maximum optional outlay of 80** for this decision and a **48-hour pause** before any optional purchase. These are supplied preferences, not universal spending rules.

| Option | Acquisition | Delivery or transport | Immediate total | Relevant uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Keep | 0 | 0 | 0 | Long-term enjoyment after noticing the scratch |
| Permitted cosmetic repair | 15 | 0 | 15 | Whether the finish improves enough to matter |
| Used replacement | 60 | 20 | 80 | Condition, fit, return terms and remaining life |
| New replacement | 150 | 20 | 170 | Actual comfort, fit and whether appearance merits the cost |

The cosmetic option addresses appearance only; it is not structural repair. The prices are invented. No purchase, repair, disposal or market inspection has occurred. Expected lifespan is unknown for every option; do not invent years of service to produce a precise “cost per use.” The current chair fits the room. Replacement dimensions and packaging/disposal arrangements are not supplied.

## Compare ongoing burdens before choosing

Keeping avoids acquisition and disposal costs but leaves the visible scratch. Cosmetic repair uses money and some effort while preserving the known fit; a safe permitted method would need to suit the material. A used replacement meets the 80 limit exactly before any unprovided costs, so condition and transport terms matter. The new chair exceeds the current limit by **90** and cannot be called within budget because a photograph looks appealing.

Make a qualitative line for durability, cleaning, space, disposal and repair effort. “Unknown; need dimensions” is better than “probably fits.” “No replacement disposal required” is a genuine difference for keeping or repair. Avoid pretending that a used item is automatically poor or a new one automatically durable. If actual shopping is later chosen, include all applicable charges and inspect relevant condition and return information before relying on the total.

## Worked reason versus story

Suppose an owner reports that a safe lamp illuminates a page well but its switch is awkward to reach. The illumination is a functional success; the reach problem is an observed usability issue; imagining that guests prefer a newer brand is a status prediction. The decision might favor a different accessible lamp even when the old one still lights up. Observing use prevents both automatic replacement and automatic refusal to spend.

For E3, the scratch is a real visible feature, while its supposed effect on visitors is untested. It may still be worth paying a small amount for an appearance the owner enjoys. The question is whether that benefit survives normal use and the pause, fits the limit and is worth what the money or space could otherwise do.

## Produce the decision in order

1. State the chair's required job, safety condition, desired comfort and appearance, and the separate status story. Retain the 80 limit and 48-hour pause before comparison. Do not redefine the limit after seeing the expensive option without acknowledging a new budget decision.
2. Calculate all four totals and add the five ongoing-burden lines. Keep unknown lifespan, dimensions and condition explicit. Identify any later information needed before a real purchase.
3. For this fictional case, save your predicted observations before opening the [later packet](later-packet.md). For optional actual practice, use a safe available item twice on ordinary occasions and privately record function, comfort, cleaning and enjoyment as actually experienced. Never substitute the supplied packet for real observations.
4. Choose keep, cosmetic repair, defer replacement or replace within a justified limit. Explain what the two uses changed and which benefit is worth paying for. Name an opportunity cost, such as delaying another chosen purchase or retaining less flexible cash.
5. Wait through the preselected pause before any optional purchase in actual practice. Recheck the reason and total. Record a decision separately from a purchase and a purchase separately from later actual enjoyment. Nothing must be bought to complete the learning.

For support, compare only keep and repair first, then add the replacements. For a harder pass, discover an unlisted transport charge and revise the comparison before commitment. A support person can explain terms without steering the owner's preferences. Actual repair, if freely chosen, must be safe, ordinary and permitted; no hazardous tool or chemical experiment is required. A decision can value comfort and beauty while rejecting a pressure-driven purchase. Neither spending nor restraint establishes a person's worth.
