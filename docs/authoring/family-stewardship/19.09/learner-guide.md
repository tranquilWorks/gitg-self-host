# 19.09 — Developmentally appropriate autonomy and discipline

Audit one ordinary limit, then adjust how you teach it over two opportunities in a week. Use twenty minutes to prepare. Live practice requires an existing appropriate care role and consistency with the person's care arrangements. Otherwise use the supplied books case. The focus is proportional expectations and repair, not a developmental assessment or a test of obedience.

## Ask what the limit protects

Some limits protect immediate safety or other people's legitimate use of a space. Others express an adult's preference for speed, order or a particular appearance. Separate them before enforcing a rule. A child may need to keep a shared walking route clear without arranging every book in the adult's preferred order. A preference can be negotiated; presenting it as a safety emergency makes explanation less trustworthy.

Developmentally appropriate responsibility depends on the particular task and observed ability as well as age. Comprehension, communication, movement, fatigue and familiarity affect what a person can do now. Do not assign a universal age cutoff or interpret one difficult attempt as defiance. A useful expectation is small enough to understand and attempt, with needed support available.

An explanation links the request to its purpose. Modeling shows what the action looks like. A boundary describes what must happen; a choice gives real discretion within it. “Which of these books shall we return first?” can be a real choice when both are acceptable. Offering two choices while punishing the less preferred answer is disguised control.

Consequences should help address the actual situation. If books remain in the shared route, an adult can help return them and reduce the task to a manageable amount. Unrelated deprivation does not teach where books belong. Food, rest, medicine, communication access, affection and other essentials never become rewards that must be earned. Violence, threats, humiliation and abandonment language are excluded.

Autonomy grows when a learner can understand a purpose, choose a workable method, ask for help and gradually take an appropriate share. Less visible resistance alone is not evidence of greater competence. Observe what support was needed and whether the learner could express uncertainty. A successful supported attempt can be more useful than a pressured independent one.

## Worked example

An adult demands that a tired child sort an entire shelf immediately. The shared-space need is simply to return two books left beside the walkway. The adult changes the task to one book at a time with a picture cue, explains the walking-space reason and lets the child choose the first book. The adult helps with the rest. The result is a proportionate repair, not proof that every original demand was developmentally reasonable.

## Supplied fictional case

Caregiver Noor is already authorized to supervise Jules in an ordinary home routine. Two light books need to be returned to an accessible basket. The space is otherwise safe; no dangerous obstruction is created for the exercise. Noor prefers alphabetic order, but that preference is unnecessary for the immediate shared-space purpose. Jules has sometimes returned one book with a picture cue and has not demonstrated sorting a shelf independently.

Noor will offer one-book steps or shared carrying, explain where the books go and observe whether Jules can identify the purpose or ask for help. A second ordinary opportunity will occur later in the week. Essential care, warmth and normal shared activities continue regardless of performance.

## Try a proportionate limit

1. Write the shared-space purpose, the adult preference and the current evidence of ability separately. Output: a justified boundary without turning alphabetic order into a necessity or guessing ability from age.
2. Prepare a brief explanation, a modeled carrying motion and a genuine choice of which book goes first. Choose the picture cue or shared action already appropriate to Jules. Output: a small task and a help route, not a demand for immediate independent sorting.
3. Rehearse or carry out the first ordinary opportunity within the actual role. If it stalls, help restore the space and reduce the demand; do not escalate punishment. Output: what was attempted, what help occurred and whether the purpose or a help request was expressed. Keep identifying observations private.
4. Choose the second demand from the first observation. Retain a useful cue, simplify after difficulty or modestly extend a task that was manageable. Explain the choice. Output: a reasoned adjustment rather than a fixed increase to prove progress.
5. Save your predicted adjustment, then read the [later packet](later-packet.md). Compare conditions and response across opportunities. Output: a narrow conclusion about this task, noting changes that prevent a clean comparison.

## Support and transfer

For support, model a single book and accept an established communication aid, gesture or shared action. Do not make verbal repetition a prerequisite for being heard. For a harder fictional version, another adult wants a much longer task because a same-age peer can do it. Explain why this person's observed needs, the legitimate purpose and available supports matter more than that comparison.

For real transfer, apply the audit to a familiar safe routine in your existing role. Repeated difficulty, distress or uncertainty about needs may require appropriate developmental or care guidance; it is not a reason to intensify control. Caregiver disagreement should be resolved through appropriate adult channels without recruiting the child to adjudicate it. The later opportunity may reasonably need more help, especially under changed conditions.

## Source limits

[The American Academy of Pediatrics policy, S11](../SOURCES.md#S11), supports effective teaching and age-appropriate expectations while opposing physical punishment and shaming. It does not validate this two-book exercise or provide an individual developmental diagnosis. The task distinguishes adult preference, explanation, proportional repair and changed responsibility; the separate parenting and family-teaching guides address broader care and instruction. One tidy space is not a full-domain result.
