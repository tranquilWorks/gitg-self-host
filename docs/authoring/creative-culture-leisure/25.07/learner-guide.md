# 25.07 — Revision, finishing, and publication

Bring one small work to a defined finish, share it appropriately and let actual reception inform closure. Allow twenty minutes to define and revise, one short release and fifteen minutes for feedback review. A private willing reader is a sufficient audience; public acclaim and commercial publication are not required.

## Define done before adding another polish pass

Finishing is a decision against a purpose and a usable set of criteria. It is not a promise that no better sentence exists. The supplied criteria are a coherent beginning and ending, one deliberate expressive choice, corrected distracting errors and a format the intended audience can access. If a criterion remains unmet after two editing passes, repair that specific issue; otherwise stop revising long enough to let someone encounter the work.

The UNC Writing Center's [revision guidance, S02](../SOURCES.md#S02) distinguishes reconsidering meaning from final proofreading. This companion uses that distinction in a small creative case. It does not reproduce the handout or claim that exactly two passes are sufficient for every project. A novel, public exhibition or professional production would have its own scope and standards.

A work can be finished for one purpose and reopened for another. Keep versions so a later change does not erase what the audience actually received. An unsent draft, a sent piece and a reader's response are different events. Self-review can prepare a release but cannot establish that another person encountered the work.

## Supplied piece F9, Version 0

The fictional maker wants to convey quiet relief when a lost mitten is found. The piece contains six original sentences:

> The mitten was gone from the bench.\
> Noor checked under it and behind the door.\
> A cleaner held up a red mitten with a white moon stitched on the palm.\
> She said it had been there all morning.\
> Noor tucked it into her pocket and the room seem warmer.\
> The bus arrived outside.

The opening establishes absence and the last sentence gives a departure point. However, “under it” is less clear than naming the bench, “there” leaves the mitten's earlier location uncertain, and “seem” is a distracting tense error. The intended relief depends on recognizing the object, but the relationship between recognition and the final warmth is thin. These are repairable features, not a reason to call the work worthless.

## Pass one: meaning and sequence

Save Version 0. The meaning pass names the search location, clarifies where the mitten was found and gives Noor an action that connects it to recognition. Do not spend this pass changing typefaces or choosing a title. Version 1 reads:

> The mitten was gone from the bench.\
> Noor checked beneath the bench and behind the door.\
> A cleaner held up a red mitten with a white moon stitched on the palm.\
> It had been caught behind the bench leg.\
> Noor traced the little moon and the room seem warmer.\
> She slipped the mitten into her pocket as the bus arrived.

The location and order are now clearer, and tracing the stitched moon is the deliberate expressive choice. It connects a small object with relief without requiring a speech about its value. The tense error remains visible because proofreading has not yet occurred. Preserving this intermediate state demonstrates the different purposes of the two passes.

## Pass two: presentation and access

Correct “seem” to “seemed.” The fictional intended reader prefers plain text with each sentence on its own line and no decorative background. The maker supplies that format with the title **The Little Moon**. No borrowed text, image or real private story is present in this original supplied specimen.

Finished Version 2:

> The mitten was gone from the bench.\
> Noor checked beneath the bench and behind the door.\
> A cleaner held up a red mitten with a white moon stitched on the palm.\
> It had been caught behind the bench leg.\
> Noor traced the little moon and the room seemed warmer.\
> She slipped the mitten into her pocket as the bus arrived.

Check the four criteria separately. The search and departure provide a beginning and ending. The stitched moon and tracing action are intentional. The known distracting error is corrected. The format matches the fictional reader's stated preference. This is a bounded completion decision, not proof of literary merit or universal accessibility. Another reader could need a different format.

## Prepare an actual release without assuming consent

The supplied invitation asks: “Would you be willing to receive a six-sentence fictional scene in plain text and tell me which moment stayed with you?” The fictional reader agrees to receive this one piece privately. That agreement does not authorize public posting, quoting their response by name or sending endless revisions. Title, brief fictional context and one feedback question accompany Version 2.

In the supplied simulation, Version 2 is delivered through the agreed private channel. No real message is sent by this exercise. If you undertake an actual release, record the actual date, version and confirmation of willingness using only information needed for your private process. Avoid storing contact details or the reader's personal history in the practice record. If consent or a usable format is absent, keep the work prepared and release pending.

At the first fictional check, the reader has not replied. Delivery is established in the case; impact is not. Silence could reflect timing, availability or preference and does not establish rejection. Do not repeatedly prompt for reassurance or invent a favorable response to make the exercise complete. The [later packet](later-packet.md) supplies a later mixed response and a closure decision for analysis.

## Use reception to choose closure or one justified change

Compare the intended quiet relief with the actual response if one arrives. A reader may notice the stitched moon but interpret the warmth literally. That is information about the text in a context, not a universal failure. You can leave the piece finished, make one versioned revision if a criterion or purpose warrants it, or carry the lesson into a different work.

If a revision is made, do not assume the original reader wants it. Ask only when further sharing is appropriate and welcome; a private revision can stay private. A muted response does not undo responsible release, while a strong response does not authorize wider distribution of the work or of the reader's words.

## Produce the finish-and-release record

1. Choose a small original piece or make the default six-sentence lost-object scene. State its purpose and all four completion criteria.
2. Save the earlier version, edit meaning first and presentation second, and name one specific change from each pass. Keep unresolved criteria explicit.
3. Confirm a willing audience and accessible format. Supply a title, needed context and accurate attribution; then distinguish prepared from actually shared.
4. Record the response or nonresponse without making up an effect. Compare what happened with what you intended.
5. Close, reopen once for a reason, or carry the lesson into new work. Label any new version and respect the scope of the audience's consent.

For support, use the four criteria as four short notes and one reader rather than a public platform. For a harder pass, compare whether a single concrete gesture can replace an explanatory sentence without losing clarity. An audio piece may need a transcript; a sketch may need an accessible description. Self-only review remains a valid preparation route, with reaching an audience still unestablished.

Actual progress here needs more than a polished file: a meaningful creation reaches an appropriate audience and reception or its absence is honestly reviewed. This supplied sequence teaches that distinction but is not evidence that you released work or received feedback.
