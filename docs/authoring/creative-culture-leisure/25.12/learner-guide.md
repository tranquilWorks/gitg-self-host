# 25.12 — Communal craft and tradition-bearing

Learn an explicitly shareable beginner form from a willing knowledgeable practitioner or authorized resource. Allow fifteen minutes to arrange permission, one bounded lesson, and twenty minutes to review practice. This supplied case uses an ordinary accordion paper fan and a fictional family teaching line. It claims no actual cultural authority, sacred form or authenticated historical origin.

## A form arrives with relationships

Learning an inherited practice involves more than extracting a technique. A teacher can show what makes an attempt recognizable, why the form matters in that setting, how people learned it and what may be passed on. One person can describe their own practice without representing a whole culture. An invitation to learn privately does not automatically authorize recording, public teaching or sale.

[Local Contexts' TK Labels, S09](../SOURCES.md#S09) illustrate community-specific authority and conditions for access and use in Indigenous contexts. Their labels are not a universal tagging scheme for every hobby, and none is applied here. [WIPO's overview of traditional cultural expressions, S10](../SOURCES.md#S10) explains the relation between expressive forms, community identity and intergenerational transmission. Neither page grants permission for a particular lesson or determines ownership of this ordinary fold.

The fictional example lets you rehearse respectful questions and follow a complete technique. Reading it does not create an actual teacher relationship. For a real attempt, use an offered form whose teaching source and permission can be established. If that is unavailable, preparation or no attempt is legitimate; do not replace a refusal with an unauthorized online copy.

## Supplied lesson agreement T9

Jo learned this family version of an accordion fan from Mae and uses it at neighborhood summer gatherings. Jo offers Tarin a thirty-minute beginner lesson with two ordinary sheets of paper already available. The supplied agreement states that Jo is willing, considers this ordinary family version shareable for this lesson, and asks for no payment on this occasion. That voluntary choice is not a rule that practitioners owe free teaching.

Private learning and practice are allowed. There will be no recording. Public posting, selling and teaching a group have not been authorized. Tarin may ask about a later demonstration, but the answer will be specific to that request. The family connection concerns this teaching line, not a claim that Mae invented accordion folding or that Jo speaks for all paper-folding communities.

Jo explains the purpose: a simple handheld fan made together from familiar material, with attention to repeating folds neatly enough to open and close. The beginner standard has four parts: eight reasonably even panels, seven alternating creases, upper edges kept aligned, and a fan that opens and closes without one crease buckling the wrong way. The practice begins with that standard before inventing a different version.

## Complete beginner sequence

Use an ordinary rectangular sheet in landscape orientation, long edges running left to right. Keep the same top edge throughout. A supported, seated or larger-paper route is legitimate when it makes the folds easier to handle; pause if the movement is uncomfortable. No cutting or special tool is needed.

1. Fold the right short edge to meet the left short edge. Align the upper corners and crease the vertical fold. Fold the resulting stack in half the same way twice more. Unfold completely. There are eight narrow columns separated by seven vertical creases; the edges of the sheet are not counted as creases.
2. Turn the sheet so the original top edge is still at the top. Refold crease one as a valley: the two adjacent panels come toward each other on the side facing you. Refold crease two the opposite way, as a mountain rising toward you. Continue **valley, mountain, valley, mountain, valley, mountain, valley** across all seven creases. Reverse a pre-existing crease gently when necessary.
3. Bring the panels together into an accordion stack, checking that the upper edges align. Hold the bottom of the stack in one hand and spread the upper ends with the other. The paper opens into a fan while the bottom remains held. Close it by bringing the panels back together; no glued handle is claimed.
4. Compare the result with Jo's four-part standard. Identify one component to practice, keeping the first attempt intact for comparison when possible.

This sequence is original instructional prose for the fictional ordinary lesson. It is not copied from an inaccessible tutorial or presented as a named community's protected pattern. An actual teacher's offered variant may use a different count or method; agree on that variant's standard before claiming to have followed it.

## Two attempts and one correction

**Attempt 1:** Tarin makes eight panels and seven creases, but crease four is a valley where the sequence requires a mountain. Opening the fan makes the center buckle. The count alone is therefore insufficient: sequence affects function. Jo points to crease four and asks Tarin to compare its direction with creases three and five.

**Attempt 2:** a second sheet has the correct alternating directions and opens without the earlier buckle. The upper edges are uneven because the corners slipped during the initial folds. Jo's specific correction is to align the upper corners before pressing the fold and to hold them in place while beginning the crease.

Tarin repeats that alignment component with two folds on spare paper. The corners meet more closely in this supplied component practice. There is no third complete fan and no independent assessment of mastery. Both whole attempts, the sequence error and the remaining alignment issue stay in the record.

Technique and responsibility belong together. A technically neater object would not authorize public teaching. Likewise, respectful credit alone would not show that the foundational fold was learned. The review needs an inspectable attempt and a truthful account of the learning relationship.

## Return the learning accurately

Tarin's first wording is “Mae's original fan design, taught by Jo.” Jo corrects it: the family uses this version, but Mae is not known to have invented it. The revised attribution is “I learned this family version from Jo, who learned it from Mae; in their gatherings it is made as a simple handheld fan.” That statement credits the known line without claiming origin or endorsement beyond it.

Before the [later packet](later-packet.md), decide what a limited demonstration request must specify: who will see it, what will be shown, whether recording occurs and whether money or promotion is involved. Keep private practice available even when wider sharing remains unapproved.

For your own attempt, retain the agreement, the form's purpose, the stated beginner standard, two attempts, one practiced correction and the permitted next step. Private notes can omit names when identification is unnecessary. Do not upload a teacher's words, image or restricted material simply to prove participation.

For support, work through the supplied sequence as preparation and use a larger sheet or assistance chosen by the learner. For a harder pass, explain why a count-correct fan can still buckle, then demonstrate the correction only within the actual permission given. One short lesson is an introduction to a practice and its responsibilities; it confers neither membership nor authority to teach unlearned material.
