# 25.08 — Creative collaboration

Give another person real influence over a small work, then examine how the result and control were shared. Allow fifteen minutes to agree, thirty to make and fifteen for a joint review. The default is a six-line invitation to a fictional celebration; a solo rehearsal can prepare the process but cannot establish actual collaboration.

## Shared authorship needs more than two names

A collaborator should be able to change a meaningful choice, explain a concern and decline an unagreed use. Dividing typing duties while one person decides everything is not the same as shared creative influence. Equal dignity does not imply identical time, skills or contribution; credit should describe what actually happened rather than force an artificial fifty-fifty story.

The University of Vermont's [group-writing guide, S03](../SOURCES.md#S03) compares working together, dividing sections and layering contributions, with different coordination and participation costs. Its educational advice does not decide ownership or guarantee a good creative result. Here the supplied makers rotate roles so an idea originator also has a turn shaping clarity and a structure editor also contributes an expressive choice.

Keep the agreement proportionate. Two people making a private miniature need clear purpose, time, decision rights, credit and sharing limits. Paid work or public licensing may need a more formal appropriate agreement. This exercise creates no contract, payment obligation or ownership transfer, and a confident or experienced participant does not gain unilateral control by default.

## Supplied collaboration C9

Fictional adults Rowan and Ellis agree to invite neighbors to a small indoor celebration, warm and lightly playful without putting reluctant guests on the spot. The invented event is Saturday, 4–5 p.m., in Maple Room at Orchard Hall, through an east level entrance. Bringing a snack is optional; quiet attendance and leaving early are welcome. No actual event or venue access is asserted.

They agree to keep the result private unless both later approve a specific use. Rowan begins with images and ideas; Ellis shapes order and clarity. After fifteen minutes they exchange roles. Each has thirty minutes to make, can request a pause and can reject use of their name outside the agreed context. Their disagreement method is to explain the intended effect, try both short versions and choose together against the invitation's purpose.

The initial credit note would say: “Words by Rowan; editing by Ellis.” It is provisional, because a contribution cannot be accurately assigned before the work occurs. If Ellis supplies an image or Rowan reorganizes the notice, the final credit must reflect that change. Neither may publish a discarded draft just because they typed it.

## Start with a joint working draft

Original six-line draft:

> Saturday, 4–5 p.m., a small neighborhood celebration.\
> Maple Room, Orchard Hall; use the east level entrance.\
> Bring a snack if you wish, or come empty-handed.\
> A chair is waiting for your quiet stories.\
> Stay for a little while or leave early.\
> Even the biscuits have practiced their welcome.

Rowan proposes the waiting chair and comic biscuits. Ellis supplies the ordered time/place/access information and the explicit optional snack. After swapping roles, Rowan shortens the event line and Ellis proposes changing “stories” to “company,” because a quiet guest should not feel required to speak. That changes meaning, not just punctuation.

A genuine difference remains at the ending. Rowan wants a playful last beat; Ellis thinks the biscuits sound like a performance expectation when combined with “stories.” Neither dismisses the other as humorless or unserious. Each explains the effect sought: gentle amusement for Rowan, permission to be present without contributing for Ellis.

## Try both endings before choosing

Alternative A is Rowan's line: “Even the biscuits have practiced their welcome.” Alternative B is Ellis's quieter line: “There is room for you without a story to tell.” Read each after the same first five lines. The first adds a comic image, but “practiced” echoes performance. The second makes welcome explicit but repeats the idea of stories and may overexplain.

The jointly selected ending combines playfulness with freedom from performance: “The biscuits will not ask you to make a speech.” This is a new shared choice, not an automatic compromise because it contains pieces from both people. They choose it because it serves the stated audience and purpose. If neither version did that, separate versions or a pause would be legitimate.

Final six-line miniature:

> Saturday, 4–5 p.m., a small neighborhood celebration.\
> Maple Room, Orchard Hall; use the east level entrance.\
> Bring a snack if you wish, or come empty-handed.\
> A chair is waiting for your quiet company.\
> Stay for a little while or leave early.\
> The biscuits will not ask you to make a speech.

The invitation keeps every practical fact while changing the guest's implied obligation. Rowan's image now works with Ellis's welcome concern. That visible interaction is better evidence of collaboration than a claim that teamwork made the piece better in every respect. A different reader could still find the joke distracting; no actual audience response is supplied.

## Review credit and control before any sharing

Rowan says Ellis's change removed an unintended demand to entertain. Ellis says Rowan's image kept the notice from sounding procedural. These fictional statements identify the other's effect on the work. Ellis also says the last choice felt rushed near the time limit. A useful final result can coexist with a process concern; do not delete it to portray mature collaboration as conflict-free.

The revised credit reads: “Created together by Rowan and Ellis: initial images and event phrasing by Rowan; notice structure and quiet-company wording by Ellis; final ending selected and rewritten together.” They both approve that description for the private case. Credit records contribution but does not itself grant publication or reuse permission.

The [later packet](later-packet.md) introduces an unagreed edit and a request for a broader use. Save the approved private version first. Compare the new proposal with the actual agreement instead of assuming that a shared file is available for whoever wishes to post it.

## Produce the collaboration record

1. Agree on purpose, six-line scope, time, chosen contributions, a role swap and a disagreement method. Record credit and specific sharing/reuse limits before making.
2. Make a joint draft and pause at one real difference. Preserve both alternatives and each person's intended effect.
3. Select, combine or keep alternatives by agreement. Identify exactly how each person changed the result, including any substantive contribution beyond the initial role.
4. Review the piece together for strength and constraint. Correct inaccurate credit or an unagreed edit before release.
5. Decide mutually to finish, revise once or keep separate versions. Mark collaboration incomplete if consent or control could not be balanced.

For support, work asynchronously with one visible proposal and one response at a time. AAC, translation or extra decision time may be part of equal influence, not a penalty. For a harder pass, let the preferred ending fail the shared purpose and agree to remove it. Do not manufacture disagreement merely to check a box; an actual difference can concern timing, access, tone or permission.

An actual joint artifact plus each participant's account of influence can support a bounded collaboration claim. The invented case provides no real collaborator, consent or attribution receipt. A solo learner may rehearse every decision while leaving actual shared authorship pending.
