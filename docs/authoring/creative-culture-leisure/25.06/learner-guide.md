# 25.06 — Creative courage and experimentation

Try three different ways of returning a lost umbrella, then learn from one choice that does not work. Allow three ten-minute drafts, fifteen minutes for critique or delayed self-review, and one ten-minute retry. The finished record contains alternatives and a reasoned change; it does not rate your courage or talent.

## Make an uncertainty small enough to examine

A creative experiment changes a constraint so that an effect can be noticed. Telling an event backward might create curiosity, or might obscure who did what. Dialogue can reveal relationships while withholding context. An object's imagined viewpoint can make the familiar strange, but may become an explanation disguised as a voice. State which uncertainty each attempt explores before deciding whether you like it.

The Montana State University Writing Center's [feedback guidance, S01](../SOURCES.md#S01) supports requests directed at a particular writing goal and consideration of suggestions against that goal. It does not prescribe these umbrella stories or validate a courage assessment. The examples, time limits and fictional responses here belong to the curriculum exercise.

Low stakes matter because you need room to keep an unsuccessful draft. Use invented ordinary events, familiar materials and an audience small enough to be freely chosen. Sharing is optional when delayed self-review is the available route. Humiliation, private trauma, dangerous performance and escalating requests for reassurance do not make an experiment more useful.

## Supplied experiment file U9

The common event is simple: Mina leaves an umbrella at a bus stop, a cyclist finds it, and a florist returns it. A blue ribbon helps identify it. The first version tests backward order, the second only dialogue, and the third the umbrella's fictional perspective. Each is six sentences and is retained as drafted. No actual learner wrote these passages in three measured sessions.

**Version A: backward order.** Question: does reverse chronology invite reconstruction while leaving each actor clear?

> At dusk Mina carried the returned umbrella past the florist.\
> Ten minutes earlier, the florist had pointed to the blue ribbon on its handle.\
> Before that, Mina had said the ribbon belonged to her.\
> Earlier, a cyclist had brought the umbrella into the shop.\
> Before that, she had lifted it from the rain beside the bus stop.\
> At noon, Mina had left it leaning against the bus-stop bench.

The order moves from return to loss, but sentence five's “she” leaves the finder unclear. A reader might attach it to Mina rather than the cyclist. Keep that defect in Version A; do not silently replace it before inviting critique. The problem concerns the reference of a word in a sequence, not the writer's identity.

**Version B: dialogue only.** Question: can an exchange supply the necessary event without a narrator explaining it?

> “Does this blue ribbon belong to you?”\
> “It was tied to my umbrella.”\
> “Then you left a small flag at the bus stop.”\
> “I thought the umbrella was gone.”\
> “The cyclist next door brought it in.”\
> “I will leave the ribbon on so it can find me again.”

The last line treats the ribbon as an aid to return and gives the exchange a playful turn. The voices are not named inside the piece; that may keep it light or make the participants hard to distinguish. This remains a separate uncertainty from Version A's chronology. Dialogue-only does not mean the reader must infer every practical fact correctly on the first pass.

**Version C: umbrella viewpoint.** Question: can an imagined object carry surprise without requiring a lesson at the end?

> My blue ribbon spent the afternoon waving for someone who had already left.\
> Rain collected in my folded sleeves.\
> A cyclist lifted me by the handle and carried me into the flower shop.\
> For an hour I stood among stems that were much better at drinking.\
> Then Mina recognized the ribbon and took me outside.\
> I opened over her as if returning a small favor.

The object voice allows an invented comparison between wet fabric and flower stems. It does not report an object's actual feelings or claim novelty beyond this small work. One reader may enjoy the final courtesy; another may find it too neatly sentimental. That disagreement can become a new question instead of a contest over the correct response.

## Ask about a moment, not a verdict

The supplied maker chooses Versions A and C for a private review. The fictional request is: “These are short experiments about the same event. Would you be willing to read them in plain text and say where you became curious or lost track of the action?” A decline ends the request. No explanation, praise or replacement reviewer is owed.

The fictional reader accepts the format and identifies sentence five of A as the point where the finder becomes unclear. For C, the reader notices the flower-stem comparison but says the last sentence explains the kindness more than needed. These are supplied responses to particular features. They do not establish actual outside feedback, consensus or the best version for all audiences.

Separate observation, suggestion and decision. “I lost track at sentence five” is a reception observation; “name the cyclist” is a possible repair; choosing that repair belongs to the maker. A reviewer can help without taking control of the work. You can reject a suggestion with a purpose-based reason while still keeping the observed difficulty in the record.

## Retry one feature and retain the original

Choose A's reference problem for the ten-minute retry. Change only the uncertain actor in sentence five, leaving its position and the six-sentence structure intact. This keeps the comparison interpretable. If you also changed the ending, tense and viewpoint, a clearer result would be harder to attribute to the named repair.

Before opening the [later packet](later-packet.md), write what a useful result would look like: a reader can identify the cyclist as finder without needing a hint. Curiosity is a second question, not automatically resolved by correct reference. An unsuccessful retry may lead to another bounded experiment or a stop. There is no requirement to keep working until the exercise appears successful.

## Produce the experiment record

1. State three uncertainties and make the three constrained versions within their separate ten-minute windows. For the default, retain backward order, dialogue-only and object viewpoint, with six sentences each.
2. Keep all drafts and name one surprise and one failure in the work. Describe choices and effects rather than calling yourself creative or uncreative.
3. Invite a willing small audience to one or two drafts with a specific question, or return after a break. Label a delayed self-review accurately; it is not another person's response.
4. Save the original, select one feature and make a bounded retry. Compare against the original question and preserve incomplete or negative results.
5. Choose a next experiment, a pause or a stop. Keep sharing scope and participation voluntary throughout.

For support, work from the supplied common event so inventing plot does not consume the whole session. For a harder pass, change the unreliable pronoun without adding a sentence, then see whether reverse order still serves the intended effect. Drawing panels, audio or movement notation may carry comparable constraints through a safe accessible form. An actual attempt needs actual made work; reading these specimens is preparation.

The evidence can show repeated exploration and a traceable response to critique under the recorded conditions. It cannot establish stable courage, universal originality or a successful audience relationship. Keeping the awkward version visible is part of making the learning inspectable.
