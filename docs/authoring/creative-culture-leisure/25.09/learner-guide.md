# 25.09 — Art as truth, memory, and moral imagination

Write a scene in which two legitimate concerns remain present. Allow twenty minutes to prepare two perspectives, thirty minutes to make a scene of no more than 300 words, and fifteen minutes for reception or delayed self-review. The task is to let a reader encounter a tension through a work, not to prove that fiction has made anyone more empathetic.

## Give each perspective something to protect

An explanation can state that a community room needs repairs and that its closure disrupts belonging. A scene can make the same tension felt through objects, timing, speech and incomplete action. Neither form replaces evidence about an actual building or community. In invented work you can explore how care for memory and care for access become difficult to reconcile without pretending to report another person's life.

The selected character discussion in OpenLearn's [Start writing fiction, S04](../SOURCES.md#S04) supports moving beyond flat labels and imagining a character's particular circumstances. Only the registered discussion passages were read; the linked book extract and course activities are not supplied here. Our scene, perspective cards and revision are original. Complex characterization is a craft aim, not evidence that the writer understands every group represented.

Choose a low-stakes invented setting. Do not needlessly borrow private trauma, sacred stories or an identifiable person's suffering. You can give characters conflicting priorities without assigning moral superiority to a cultural identity or disability. Let each have an action, a fear and something they do not know about the other. An unresolved ending is acceptable when the unresolved issue is made intelligible.

## Supplied preparation file M9

The fictional neighborhood room will close for repairs. Its entrance threshold is difficult for some users, and a loose board needs attention. No qualified building finding or actual repair decision is being made. The work concerns two people responding to a supplied closure, not deciding whether the room is safe to keep using.

**Amir's perspective.** He helped his late neighbor paint the room's wooden welcome sign. He values the record of ordinary people making a place together. He fears that relocation will disperse the gathering and that the worn sign will be discarded. He does not yet know whether Tess wants any familiar objects kept.

**Tess's perspective.** She uses a walking frame and has repeatedly needed help at the entrance. She values being able to enter without negotiating assistance. She fears that nostalgia will become a reason to postpone work again. She does not know the sign's personal history, and she also wants the group to keep meeting.

Neither card establishes what all longtime residents or all people using mobility aids feel. Each is one invented person's situation. Before writing, identify a possible point of encounter: the sign is movable, while the inaccessible threshold requires material change. Saving an object cannot substitute for that change.

**Plain explanation for comparison:** Amir wants to preserve the room's memories, while Tess needs the entrance repaired. They have not yet found an accessible place for the gathering during the closure. They might preserve the sign and continue looking for a place together.

## Complete original scene, Version 1

> Amir rubbed the thumb-sized hollow in the wooden welcome sign. “Nadia painted this letter twice. She said a welcome should survive the rain.”
>
> Tess waited beside the doorway with her frame. The loose board made a pale line beneath her front wheel. “It should survive being stepped over, too.”
>
> He reached for the nail holding the sign, then stopped. “If they put us somewhere else, people will forget the Thursdays.”
>
> “I remember every Thursday I had to ask someone to lift this.” Tess tapped the frame. “I want to arrive before the conversation starts.”
>
> From inside came the scrape of chairs being stacked. Amir took the sign down and held it against his coat.
>
> “I don't know where we'll meet,” he said.
>
> Tess looked away toward the street. “Neither do I.”
>
> He turned the sign over. On its back Nadia had tested three shades of green. He had never noticed that before.
>
> “We could ask about the library room,” Tess said. “Ask about the entrance first.”
>
> Amir offered her one end of the sign. She took it, and they carried it as far as the gate. Neither turned it outward yet.

The sign carries public welcome, private memory and an unfinished practical question. Its unpainted back reveals trial colors that the explanation cannot make a reader encounter in quite the same way. Tess's account connects access with participation in the conversation, rather than treating access as a decorative kindness. These are readings supported by choices in the text, not mandatory emotions for every reader.

## Receive a response without demanding agreement

Ask a willing reader: “What does each person seem to protect, and where did a detail change your understanding?” They can decline or respond only to the work. Do not ask them to disclose matching experiences. If no reader is available, set the piece aside and read it later, explicitly calling that delayed self-review.

Before opening the [later packet](later-packet.md), mark one line that may flatten either character. Version 1's “looked away” may convey uncertainty, impatience or indifference. An author cannot settle the question merely by announcing a kind intention. A reader's account identifies a possible effect; it does not authorize that reader to speak for an entire community.

Keep the plain explanation and the scene together. Name what the scene adds, what remains ambiguous, and what factual work it cannot do. It does not identify a usable replacement room, assess the building, confirm shared consent or resolve whose needs take priority. Hope can be a concrete next question rather than a false happy ending.

## Make and review your own bounded work

1. Use the supplied cards or invent two perspectives with a value, fear and unknown for each. Spend the preparation window making both intelligible.
2. Draft no more than 300 words. Give each person an action or line that matters to the outcome. Keep a direct explanation beside it for comparison.
3. Invite a freely chosen reader or use delayed self-review. Preserve the particular response and its source without converting it into testimony about real people.
4. Revise one choice that obstructs the intended tension. Retain the original, the reason and any uncertainty after the change.
5. Identify a limit: whose voice is still missing, what practical issue remains, or what another form might express differently.

For support, retain the room, sign and two cards, changing only one action. For a harder attempt, write a different ending that preserves both the unresolved access question and the significance of the sign without explaining either in a final moral. An audio scene, image sequence with descriptions or another accessible form may serve the same comparison. A listener's response is evidence only if someone actually listened and freely responded.

The product can show a traceable use of artistic form to carry memory, suffering, hope and ambiguity. It cannot demonstrate moral improvement, historical accuracy or knowledge of a lived experience the writer has not investigated.
