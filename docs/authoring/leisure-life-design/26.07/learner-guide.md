# 26.07 — Cross-cultural exploration

## Let an unfamiliar practice stay particular

When something feels strange, a quick explanation is tempting: they are more communal, less efficient, more spiritual, or simply doing what we do under another name. Each explanation can erase what participants actually say. Cross-cultural learning needs enough patience to separate an observed action, its stated meaning, your analogy and the part you still do not understand.

The fictional Lantern Lane account supplies two voices from a neighborhood with an unfamiliar way of sharing workshop time. It represents no ethnicity, nation or religion. Allow twenty minutes to read and make an interpretation sheet, ten for later clarification and ten for checks. [UNESCO's dialogue article, S03](../SOURCES.md#S03) identifies listening as part of intercultural understanding. This original exercise is not a Story Circles session or evidence that the article's workshop outcomes apply here.

Worked example: seeing a host leave an empty chair might suggest either hospitality or exclusion. Until the host explains, both are hypotheses. Even a clear explanation of that one chair does not establish what every person in the community believes about guests.

## Two complete accounts and one invitation

**Public invitation from the fictional Lantern Lane workshop committee.** Visitors may read this account and attend an open tool-mending hour as observers. Photographing, recording, taking tools or reproducing the group's closing verse is not included. Questions are welcome after the demonstration if the speaker wishes. The group has not invited visitors to perform or teach the verse. No actual event is available through this exercise.

**Mira, one long-standing participant.** “We leave the first repair slot unassigned until the hour begins. It can go to someone whose bus arrived late or whose work shift changed. I like that someone does not have to explain their whole life to ask. We write the order on a board so people know what is happening. I cannot explain the closing verse on everyone's behalf.”

**Dev, a newer participant.** “The flexible first slot helped me once. On another day I waited longer than I expected and had to leave before my turn. I want clearer end times. I enjoy mending with people, but I do not always stay for the closing.”

**Observation card.** At the described session, the first slot remains blank until 14:05. A late arrival receives it. Two names shift down the board; nobody's reason is written. The committee posts an end time of 15:00. The card gives no private circumstances and no measure of how often the arrangement works.

Your initial hypothesis is supplied: “The blank slot means the organizers forgot to plan.” Treat it as revisable, not shameful. An unfamiliar custom can serve a good and impose a burden at the same time; learning that does not require either praise or condemnation of a whole group.

## Four columns, then a smaller conclusion

1. Make columns headed observed, attributed meaning, my interpretation and unknown. Enter at least three concrete features: the held slot, the visible board and the optional closing. Put Mira's and Dev's explanations under their names. The observation card alone does not establish either person's feelings.
2. Replace the forgotten-plan hypothesis with a narrower account supported by both voices. Name the supported good, the waiting cost and a remaining question about who can change the arrangement. Do not assume the committee speaks for everyone or that Dev's concern cancels Mira's experience.
3. Save the sheet before the [later packet](later-packet.md). Incorporate the clarification while preserving the boundary around the verse. Write one lesson you can use at home without copying a restricted form: for example, ask whether a shared plan needs room for unpredictable arrivals and a clear finish.

## Participation is another permission question

A community-authored account is a useful starting point; reading it is not attendance. At a real invited encounter, confirm visitor conduct, use accurate language support when needed and accept a declined question. No participant is obliged to represent a culture or disclose a difficult personal history.

For support, work only from the supplied text, use a reader, or compare two statements at a time. For a harder exercise, write two different plausible readings of the same board change and name what additional evidence would distinguish them. Do not decide by which reading flatters your worldview.

Later transfer can involve a second authorized account or encounter, followed by a note of what changed and what remains misunderstood. A changed assumption is evidence of that revision; it is not intercultural expertise. If your result says all members feel the same, celebrates every difference uncritically, or dismisses a burden as backwardness, return to the named voices and narrow it.
