# 26.11 — Communal recreation and inclusive play

## Change the opening, not the person

An invitation to play is incomplete if only the fastest speaker can use it. Inclusion concerns the actual opportunity to contribute, watch or pass. It is not equal word counts, compulsory turns or a diagnosis-based guess about what someone can do. A useful adaptation removes a particular barrier and is then checked with the people using it.

This guide supplies a complete no-cost game and two fictional rounds. Allow fifteen minutes to prepare, ten to compare the rounds and ten for checks. Use only text if preferred; no participants are required for the default. [W3C event accessibility guidance, S05](../SOURCES.md#S05) supports accessible information and participant access planning. Applying those ideas to this original game does not establish universal accessibility.

Worked example: a facilitator gives everyone a turn but closes each after two seconds. A person composing a written answer still cannot contribute. Asking what pace would work, leaving an agreed response interval and allowing a pass changes the opening. Praising the person for finally “keeping up” misidentifies the barrier.

## Complete game: impossible object jobs

Choose an ordinary imaginary object and offer a harmless impossible job for it. An umbrella might become a mushroom's theater roof; a spoon might be a moon's rowing oar. There are no correct answers, elimination, speed points or movement requirements. Each willing person may speak, type, draw, sign through an agreed communication route, use a helper they choose, watch or pass. Do not infer humor preferences or communication needs from age or disability.

Four fictional players agree to try: Asha speaks quickly, Bo types, Cora prefers listening and sometimes passing, and Dani draws with a short spoken description. All are adults; if adapting for children, use responsible adult supervision and age-appropriate content. Everyone already has their chosen materials. There is no fee, purchase, public recording or requirement to explain access needs. The room and seating are confirmed suitable in this fictional case.

The first round uses an umbrella. Its starting rule is “one invitation to each person, then an open floor.” The host reads the prompt and makes the same text available. No one has yet checked whether this rule gives the different modes enough time. The initial hypothesis is that a nominal turn for each person will be sufficient. That hypothesis is testable and may be wrong.

Before the second round, participants may agree to a change: collect one response or explicit pass from each willing player before opening discussion; wait without filling every silence; ask whether more time or another mode is needed. There is no fixed response deadline. A pass ends that invitation and does not require later make-up speech. The second object is a shoe, so new ideas remain possible.

## Inspect a barrier without scoring people

1. Write the rules in five short lines: prompt, response modes, invitation structure, pass/watch right and end condition. The round ends when everyone has had the agreed opportunity or chooses to stop, not when everyone has produced an answer.
2. Save the initial hypothesis, then read the [later packet](later-packet.md). Find the specific first-round mechanism that blocked a response. Distinguish the host's interpretation from what Bo actually says. Identify which change the group agrees to and which outcome remains unknown.
3. Compare rounds using access facts: was a typed response received, was a drawing described, was passing respected, and did the open floor dominate? Record optional enjoyment feedback separately. Decide what to retain or change without counting people as inclusion points.

## Meaningful participation can be quiet

For support, analyze the supplied rounds or play alone with imaginary objects, labeled as rehearsal. Simplify vocabulary, describe drawings, use an agreed language/communication aid or give advance prompts when actual participants prefer them. Watching is a valid choice, not evidence the design necessarily failed or succeeded.

For a harder variation, a new player joins using a different language. Ask about workable translation and pacing rather than assigning the fastest player to answer for them. If a suitable route is unavailable, change the activity with agreement or defer; do not call unsupported attendance inclusion.

Later transfer needs another actual agreed game and another check of whether the adaptation helps. Two fictional rounds cannot establish what children, other cultures or all disabled people need. A richer invitation can coexist with neutral enjoyment; leisure must remain chosen.
