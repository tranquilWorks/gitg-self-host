# 26.14 — Ecological relationship and reciprocal stewardship

## Let a place's actual need shape your contribution

Stewardship begins when attention becomes an accountable relationship with a particular place and the people responsible for it. An action that feels caring may be unwanted or harmful: removing a plant without identification, publishing a sensitive location or submitting guesses can create more work than help. Reciprocity includes learning the limits and checking whether the contribution was usable.

Use the fictional Reed Court observation project below. Allow twenty minutes to prepare, ten per supplied session record and fifteen for review and checks. No field visit, cleanup, identification or submission to an actual project is required. [NPS citizen-science guidance, S08](../SOURCES.md#S08) describes both field and virtual opportunities and checking expectations with organizers. It does not validate the invented protocol or supply a live volunteer opening.

Worked example: a volunteer sees a bare patch and wants to plant seeds. The site manager explains that the patch is a monitored drainage area and requests observations from the path instead. Following that request provides a possible contribution; unsolicited planting would alter the very condition being studied.

## Complete project instructions R3

The fictional courtyard manager wants to know whether ordinary water flow is obstructed by fallen leaves. The ecological relationship of interest is between leaf material, water movement and the planted edge; the current pressure is occasional accumulation near a drain. The site is not a wildlife census, and no claim about drainage capacity or habitat health is assumed.

The manager authorizes observation only from the existing accessible window. No entry to the planted area, touching drain covers, litter handling, species removal, feeding, collection or planting is allowed. The observer has read these instructions and the manager confirms the window route is suitable. The requested work is two ten-minute windows at least several days apart. The fictional project sets those durations; they are not a scientific standard.

For each session record: date, actual start/end, whether the drain mouth stayed visible for the full interval, surface state (dry, wet or unknown), leaf obstruction (visible, none visible or unknown), and a short uncertainty note. If the drain view is blocked for part of the interval, obstruction is unknown and the record is incomplete for this project's comparison. Do not fill the missing minutes with an estimate. “None visible” is a completed observation category, not proof that the whole drainage system is clear.

Only the coordinator receives the internal site code R3. No public location, photos of people or sensitive wildlife details are requested. The supplied coordinator reply is the only validation feedback in this exercise. Submission alone is not acceptance, and a virtual analysis of these cards is not a field visit.

**First record, April 3:** 09:00–09:10; the drain mouth stays visible; the surface is dry; no leaf obstruction is visible; no plants or objects are touched. This is a complete null observation under the stated method. Dry conditions do not demonstrate that water would flow well during rain.

## Contribute honestly, including an incomplete record

1. Write the requested need, relationship, pressure and action limits. Copy a blank record with the allowed categories. Identify which questions require qualified staff rather than a volunteer's intervention. Preserve the difference between learning about restoration and being authorized to restore.
2. Save the first record and read the [later packet](later-packet.md). Apply the exact visibility rule to the second session before reading the coordinator's feedback. Keep observed wetness separate from unknown obstruction. Do not replace the first null with a more exciting finding.
3. Compare what was submitted with what was accepted. Name the burden of an incomplete record on the coordinator. Choose a realistic return or responsible end; a proposed date is not another observation. The useful output is an honest contribution record and response to feedback, not a claim of ecosystem recovery.

## Return within permission and capacity

For support, use the complete supplied text or an actual authorized remote-data task with its own instructions. A helper may read or transcribe without inventing missing values. A field route requires actual permission, access, conditions and training; hazardous cleanup and ecological intervention are outside this exercise.

For a harder case, the observer notices an injured animal or a sharp object. Stop within a safe location and notify the appropriate actual responsible service; do not handle it or extend the project's authority. Record only the permitted minimal information.

Later transfer is two suitable contributions under an actual project's current method, followed by real usability feedback. Keep missed opportunities explicit and agree to a cadence you can sustain. Ending responsibly can be more reciprocal than promising weekly help and disappearing. Longer-term ecological conclusions require suitable evidence beyond two windows.
