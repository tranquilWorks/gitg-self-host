# 24.12 — Ownership, governance, and alternative enterprise forms

Compare who supplies resources, who decides, who bears loss and who can challenge a decision. Allow thirty minutes to map alternatives and twenty to test the two governance conflicts. Your output is a conditional institutional choice with unresolved constraints, not incorporation paperwork.

## A name does not settle control

Ownership can provide economic rights, voting rights or both, depending on the arrangement. Governance specifies who makes which decisions, under what duties and procedures. Management carries out operations within that authority. A person may manage work without owning the enterprise, and an employee may hold a financial stake without having equal votes over every decision.

The SBA's [business-structure overview, S07](../SOURCES.md#S07) supplies introductory distinctions among several forms. Its context is the United States; legal, tax and liability details do not transfer automatically to other jurisdictions. The Department of Labor's [employee-ownership landing page, S08](../SOURCES.md#S08) identifies multiple employee-ownership arrangements. It does not establish that every arrangement provides direct democratic control.

The OECD's [selected state-owned-enterprise chapter passages, S09](../SOURCES.md#S09) discuss public ownership or control, economic activity and public-policy objectives. A public service is not necessarily an enterprise, and a government-owned enterprise is not identical to a ministry. Compare actual governing documents rather than inferring accountability from a label.

## Compare all seven forms

| Form | Capital and control | Surplus, loss and accountability questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sole ownership | One owner supplies or arranges capital and normally holds decision authority | Who bears obligations, keeps residual returns and hears affected users? Exact liability depends on lawful structure |
| Partnership | Partners contribute under an agreement allocating authority | How are profit, loss, work and disputes shared; can one partner bind others? |
| Corporation with investor ownership | Investors supply equity; voting and board arrangements govern control | How do investor returns, board duties and stakeholder effects interact? Shares need not carry identical rights |
| Cooperative | Members participate under a defined membership and voting system | Which workers or users qualify, how are returns allocated, and whose interests remain outside membership? |
| Employee ownership | Workers hold an economic stake through a specified arrangement | Inspect plan, trust and governing rules for actual voting, exit, concentration and benefit rights |
| Nonprofit | Funding may include earned revenue, donations or grants; a governing body directs the mission | Revenue and paid work are possible; owner-profit distribution is constrained by the form, with exact duties and tax status requiring advice |
| Public enterprise | A public body owns or controls an economic enterprise under a defined mandate | Public reporting, oversight, service obligations and commercial objectives may conflict; identify the actual accountable authority |

For capital and loss, the map needs a second pass. A sole owner or partners arrange funding and residual returns under their lawful arrangement; investor shareholders elect or otherwise appoint directors under the share rules, with company resources and any specific guarantees determining loss exposure. Cooperative members may contribute capital, elect the governing body and allocate surplus or absorb losses according to their rules. Employee owners may supply value through a plan or trust; the plan defines economic downside and whether employees appoint decision makers. Nonprofit funders do not automatically appoint directors; the charter and lawful rules govern appointment, mission use of any surplus and treatment of deficits. A public owner appoints or oversees decision makers through its authorized structure; surplus use and public exposure to losses depend on that mandate, with no automatic unlimited bailout. Borrowing and outside support can exist in several forms and require their own conditions. These are questions to resolve, not universal liability promises.

Public provision is the broader alternative: an authority might provide the service directly, contract for it or own an enterprise. The public-enterprise row describes the ownership option within that broader category. None of the seven labels inherently violates the learning-service mission; a specific concentration of control, funding condition or inability to provide access can conflict with it.

This is a comparison tool, not a ranking from selfish to good. Each form can be well or poorly governed. A cooperative can exclude nonmember users; a nonprofit can have an unresponsive board; a public owner can demand conflicting goals. Specific rights, accessible participation, clear duties and usable challenge routes matter.

## Supplied service G8

A fictional learning-resource service wants reliable access, fair worker treatment and enough maintenance capacity. Launch requires **300 units**; confirmed resources are **180**, leaving a **120 gap**. A prospective funder offers 120 only if it receives sole veto power to reduce the service from four open hours to two whenever it chooses. The service's proposed mission commitment requires at least **four open hours**.

The recurring budget forecasts **200 revenue**, **140 worker cost** and **40 maintenance**, leaving **20 for reserve**. Users propose a fee reduction that would lower revenue by **40**, to **160**. With costs unchanged at 180, the service would have a **20 operating deficit**, not a zero balance. The forecasted reserve contribution disappears; it is not an existing reserve that can be spent indefinitely. No other subsidy or spare worker capacity is supplied.

Two fictional governance cards let you test the conflicts. These rules are exercise assumptions, not legal models. In the cooperative card, five members comprise two workers, two users and one community member. Routine decisions require three affirmative votes with at least three members present. Changing the four-hour mission commitment requires four of five members after notice to affected users. No funder receives votes automatically. Members can request a recorded reconsideration through a designated secretary.

In the nonprofit card, three independent directors govern; routine decisions require two votes. Changing the mission commitment requires all three after an announced user-and-worker hearing. A funding agreement cannot silently override that rule. An accessible written concern goes to a named board secretary and must enter the next decision agenda. The hearing supplies input, not a promise that participants can compel their preferred outcome.

## Test two conflicts in both forms

For the funding conflict, the cooperative members and nonprofit directors must each identify who can consider the offer, the requested change in control and the conflict with four-hour access. Both can decline the conditions and retain the mission. Neither can assume that money will appear afterward: **120 remains unfunded**. If either proposes a lawful mission change, the relevant heightened procedure, stakeholder effects and qualified review must be explicit. An informal manager acceptance is insufficient under either supplied card.

For the fee conflict, both forms must show the revised 160 revenue and 180 costs. In the cooperative, worker and user members can voice the conflicting interests, while nonmember users need a route to be heard. In the nonprofit, the board hears both groups before its vote. Neither form gives an automatic right to cut worker pay or neglect maintenance. A smaller fee change, external support or a reduced service commitment might be considered, but each needs facts and appropriate authority. Do not turn an uncosted wish into an approved solution.

The worked provisional choice is the cooperative because the service wants users and workers to share formal participation. This reason concerns the fictional mission, not superiority of the form. The decision remains contingent on legal feasibility, participants' willingness, accessible meeting arrangements and financing. If members cannot sustain governance work, a responsive nonprofit with concrete participation rights could become more suitable. Name the fact that would change the recommendation.

## Make the decision inspectable

1. Complete the seven-form comparison using capital, control, mission, accountability and stakeholder effects. Mark which conclusions require governing documents or qualified advice.
2. Use both cards to record each conflict: decision owner, required procedure, affected people, numerical consequence, challenge route and unresolved result.
3. Write the conditional choice and the changed fact that would reverse it. Keep the launch gap and operating deficit visible.
4. Prepare three questions for a qualified adviser: which forms and membership arrangements are available; how voting, duties, liability and funder conditions can lawfully interact; and what tax, reporting and paid-worker obligations apply. These are questions, not drafted legal terms.
5. Save your recommendation, then open the [later packet](later-packet.md) to test whether the preferred form can actually make its next decision.

For support, compare just the two supplied cards after reading the seven-form map. For a harder pass, add an affected nonmember who cannot attend meetings and design a feasible input route without falsely granting a vote. An actual institutional recommendation would need verified law, governing documents, consent and financial evidence. This exercise requires none of the learner's private employment or ownership history.

Progress is an analysis that connects an institutional form to actual rights and consequences. Selecting the most appealing label does not demonstrate capital adequacy, fair governance or mission achievement. The later failure matters precisely because a good intention cannot substitute for a decision procedure that works.
