# 24.14 — Global trade, development, and supply chains

Explain why a trade could help under a simple model, then test how a real chain of obligations could change that judgment. Allow thirty minutes for production and twenty for disruption. Your output is a conditional trade explanation with named risk bearers, not an import plan.

## Start with the forgone alternative

Comparative advantage concerns opportunity cost: what a producer gives up to make an additional unit of something else. Absolute productivity concerns how much it can produce with the stated resources. These questions differ. The IMF's [trade explanation, S11](../SOURCES.md#S11) supports the opportunity-cost distinction and the need to consider distribution. Its historical trade figures are not used as current facts here.

Fictional producer A can use one hour to make **six bread units or three cloth units**. B can make **four bread or four cloth**. Assume linear production, divisible units and no transport, transaction or adjustment costs only for this calculation. It is not a claim about actual workers, countries, wages or technology.

A gives up six bread to make three cloth: **one cloth costs two bread**. B gives up four bread for four cloth: **one cloth costs one bread**. Thus B has the lower opportunity cost in cloth. For bread, A gives up half a cloth per bread, while B gives up one cloth; A has the comparative advantage in bread. A's six versus four bread per hour and B's four versus three cloth are separate absolute-productivity comparisons.

The proposed exchange is **one cloth for 1.5 bread**. That cloth price lies between the producers' opportunity costs of one and two bread. A can obtain cloth more cheaply than making it, and B receives more bread than it forgoes. This conclusion depends on the model's assumptions and terms of exchange, not on a slogan that every trade benefits everyone.

## Show the gain rather than merely naming it

If A makes six bread and exchanges 1.5 for one cloth, A retains **4.5 bread and one cloth**. Producing one cloth itself would use one-third of its hour, leaving **four bread and one cloth**. The exchange adds half a bread relative to that comparison.

If B makes four cloth and exchanges one for 1.5 bread, B retains **three cloth and 1.5 bread**. Producing 1.5 bread itself would use three-eighths of its hour, leaving **2.5 cloth and 1.5 bread**. The exchange adds half a cloth. Compare the same desired bundle component before declaring a gain; do not add bread and cloth into an unexplained total.

These examples do not allocate gains among owners, employees or consumers. Even when total possibilities expand, a worker or place may lose employment, income or bargaining power, and adjustment may take time. Nor does the model describe development as a single income number: capabilities, infrastructure, institutions and distribution can affect who can use a new opportunity. Those questions require evidence beyond the two production ratios.

## Trace a separate generic cloth chain

The chain is raw material, worker, producer, shipper, customs process, buyer and end user. It is generic and fictional; it does not imply that any real country, product or party is approved for trade. Trace the movement of goods, information, payment and risk as separate relationships.

| Stage | What needs checking | Supplied risk allocation or unknown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw material and worker | Inputs, working conditions, compensation, safety and resource use | Actual labor and environmental evidence is absent; a low input price proves neither fair nor unfair conditions |
| Producer | Capacity, quality specification and payment timing | The toy buyer pays a deposit; producer funds work before the balance, creating cash-flow exposure |
| Shipper and storage | Transit time, damage, energy, holding cost and responsibility | The toy buyer bears agreed delay and storage cost; actual carriage terms are not supplied |
| Customs and authorities | Classification, tariffs, product rules, sanctions and export controls | Current jurisdiction-specific authority and qualified review are required; no clearance is assumed |
| Buyer | Currency, inventory, rejection decisions and supplier leverage | A foreign-currency invoice assigns the modeled exchange movement to the buyer |
| End user | Availability, suitability, access and cost passed onward | A stockout postpones delivery; the buyer must disclose the limit rather than promise supply |

For a **100 foreign-unit invoice**, an exchange rate of **1.2 domestic units per foreign unit** costs **120 domestic units**. A move to **1.3** raises that to **130**, a ten-unit increase for this buyer under the stated invoice. Different invoice or hedging terms could assign exposure differently; none is supplied. Do not reverse the units or treat a currency move as equally beneficial to every party.

For the toy quality agreement, a rejected batch leaves the producer responsible for replacement production and the buyer responsible for already-agreed storage. This does not establish lawful real contract terms. A powerful buyer delaying the final payment could transfer financing pressure to the producer and workers. That is a risk mechanism, not an allegation about an actual factory. Record who can reject, whose evidence determines quality and how disputes could be reviewed.

The OFAC [introductory sanctions FAQs, S12](../SOURCES.md#S12) and BIS [export-compliance overview, S13](../SOURCES.md#S13) identify why current authoritative checks matter. Neither page constitutes screening of this chain. No parties, goods, classifications, licenses, tariffs or product requirements have been checked for a transaction. Permission cannot be inferred from the comparative-advantage calculation, a cheap offer or an educational source link.

## Compare responses to two disruptions

The buyer needs **ten cloth units per week**. A **20-unit buffer** covers two weeks if demand is unchanged. A supplied shipping delay lasts **three weeks**, leaving **one week, or ten units**, uncovered. The buffer is useful but insufficient. A separate single-source failure has no confirmed recovery date; stock cannot solve an indefinite interruption.

| Option | Benefit | Cost and evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Twenty-unit buffer | Covers two ordinary weeks | Two storage units per week, tied-up resources and possible damage; not a complete answer to a three-week delay |
| Second lawful source | Could reduce one-source dependence | Four extra units per cloth and 15 qualification units; capacity, labor, quality and legal checks remain open |
| Local production | Could shorten one transport path | Six extra units per cloth in the toy quote; local does not automatically mean cleaner, fairer or sufficiently capable |
| Longer lead time | Avoids a promise the stock cannot meet | Users wait and may need alternatives; lost use and downstream work are costs even without an extra invoice |

For the short delay, use the existing buffer only with honest availability information and an agreed longer lead time for the uncovered week. For supplier failure, investigate a qualified second source while keeping demand commitments provisional. This is a conditional analysis, not a purchase or contact instruction. Do not assume the second source is ready just because its price is listed.

## Produce the connected explanation

1. Show both cloth opportunity costs, both bread opportunity costs and the two worked bundle comparisons. State every simplifying assumption.
2. Draw or describe the seven-stage chain and assign delay, rejection, currency and bargaining-power exposure. Mark unknown labor and environmental conditions.
3. Calculate the delay gap and currency change. Compare all four resilience responses with costs and limits.
4. Save the conditional response, then open the [later packet](later-packet.md). Explain the original potential gain alongside the new constraints.
5. Name the evidence and lawful authority needed before any actual transaction. Keep aggregate benefit distinct from worker, place and user outcomes.

Use tokens and a verbal chain if tables are difficult. A harder pass changes demand or the invoice currency without changing the production ratios. Progress requires an analysis that can explain both gains and limits. The existence of friction does not erase the simple model's result; the model's result does not settle a real supply decision.
