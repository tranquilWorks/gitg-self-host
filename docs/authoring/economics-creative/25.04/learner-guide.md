# 25.04 — Medium and technique

Keep a small idea stable while changing how the medium carries it. Use two fifteen-minute technique sessions and a fifteen-minute comparison. You will practice a controlled variation, make a four-line exchange in two forms and choose a form for a named audience and effect.

## A medium gives possibilities and constraints

Written language can use punctuation, line breaks and a reader's ability to return to earlier words. Spoken language can use timing and emphasis, but a listener may not be able to revisit a phrase immediately. A marked performance script can make intended timing visible without requiring speech. An assistive communication device also has expressive possibilities and limits; it is part of the medium, not a lesser substitute.

The UNC Writing Center's [reading-aloud guidance, S16](../SOURCES.md#S16) describes how changing the encounter with a text can reveal tone, rhythm and omissions. It does not require a particular voice or equipment here. This companion's examples and notation are original. No audio was generated or performance recorded; a written rendering should not be described as a heard result.

The foundational technique is a controlled change in pause or emphasis while preserving words. If everything changes at once, you cannot tell whether a new effect came from vocabulary, timing or context. Comfort sets the limit: use silent notation, familiar text or a preferred communication method. There is no need for loudness, breath strain, painful movement or equipment purchases.

## Isolate three variations of one line

The exact starting line is **“I thought the door was closed.”** Preserve its words while exploring these variants:

| Variant | Written or marked line | Possible effect to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statement | I thought the door was closed. | Reports a previous belief; context may imply it changed |
| Question | I thought the door was closed? | In dialogue, rising uncertainty may ask for confirmation; the grammar alone does not fix delivery |
| Pause between clauses | I thought—the door was closed. | Separates “I thought” and “the door was closed,” potentially making the second clause sound like a new assertion |

The dash variant exposes an ambiguity: the pause could communicate hesitation, or it could make the second clause sound more certain. Record what your actual rendering does rather than assuming punctuation has one universal emotional meaning. The six words stay fixed. Typographic marks, rhythm and emphasis are the variables.

Choose one controlled variation and practice it three times. The supplied choice uses a short pause after “thought” and emphasizes that word, aiming to suggest a belief now in doubt. For a silent route, actually write or mark each pass and compare the notation; reading the table three times without making or executing the chosen variation is not the same attempt.

| Supplied pass | Marking made | Process observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | I THOUGHT [long pause] the door was closed. | The separation may sound or read like two disconnected claims |
| 2 | I THOUGHT [short pause] the door was closed. | The shorter pause keeps the idea more connected |
| 3 | I THOUGHT [short pause] the door was closed. | The chosen mark is repeated consistently; no actual listener response is supplied |

The bracketed instructions and capitals are notation, not added spoken words. The first pass tests an overly long pause; the next two stabilize a shorter one. In your record, note what you actually marked, performed or played through your chosen device. Repetition of supplied text does not establish an unobserved spoken skill.

## Make the same exchange in two forms

The supplied written piece aims at quiet uncertainty in a fictional rehearsal room. It uses four lines and concrete details: a ribbon, a window, a note and a latch.

> A: I thought the door was closed.\
> B: Then why is the ribbon moving?\
> A: The window is shut.\
> B: Read the note under the latch.

Line breaks give each response a separate turn. The ribbon makes uncertainty visible without explicitly naming it. The note leaves a small question unresolved; this is a fictional scene, not an instruction for checking a real building or an emergency. A reader can return to the earlier line to consider why the window matters.

The second form is an original marked performance script of the **same spoken words**:

> A: I THOUGHT [short pause] the door was closed.\
> B: Then why is the RIBBON moving? [one beat]\
> A: The WINDOW is shut.\
> B: [brief pause] Read the note under the latch.

This is an accessible rendering attempt on the page, not a claim that someone performed or heard it. It places timing instructions where the written version relied on turn structure and rereading. If you choose speech or a device in an actual attempt, use a comfortable delivery and record what happened. A device's pause controls may differ; do not pretend the notation guarantees the same result in every medium.

Written form affords precise spacing and rereading, but cannot guarantee which word a silent reader stresses. A performance affords timing and emphasis, but can pass too quickly and may be inaccessible without an agreed alternative. A marked script exposes timing choices visually, but cannot itself establish acoustic tone or a listener's experience. Compare what each form actually supplies, rather than declaring one more artistic.

## Choose a form for a reason

The supplied context is a private reader who wants to revisit lines at their own pace. The maker chooses written text with a small amount of visible emphasis because the reader controls pace and the materials are already available. The intended effect remains uncertainty about the speaker's earlier belief, not accusation toward the other person.

The revised written piece is:

> A: I thought the door was closed—thought, not checked.\
> B: Then why is the ribbon moving?\
> A: The window is shut.\
> B: There is a note under the latch.

Now vocabulary changes deliberately after the controlled technique exercise. The first line states the distinction between belief and checking, while the final declarative sentence softens the command. This revision may be clearer but less subtle. Explain that tradeoff; do not claim it preserves the exact original words, which was a constraint on the earlier practice and same-exchange rendering.

## Produce the technique-and-medium record

1. Write or render the three starting variants with the six words stable. Note how statement, question and pause alter possible meaning.
2. Practice the chosen variation three times in an accessible form. Save a notation or actual process record without fabricating sound, timing precision or audience evidence.
3. Make a four-line exchange with one concrete detail and deliberate turns. Render the same exchange in a second accessible form and identify one affordance and limitation of each.
4. Name the audience and intended effect, choose a medium and revise from the comparison. Keep the original alongside the revision so the reason for change is visible.
5. Use a willing reader or delayed self-review, then open the [later packet](later-packet.md) for the supplied reception case. Decide what to keep and what remains uncertain.

For support, mark only one emphasis and one pause per line. For a harder pass, hold all words stable in the final revision too and test whether spacing alone helps. A tactile arrangement, gesture or sound notation may replace the default when it preserves a comparable controlled technique and two-form comparison. Familiar safe materials are sufficient.

Actual progress combines making, a traceable technique choice and a reception check at its proper scale. Choosing a medium by prestige, owning equipment or imagining a performance is insufficient. A quiet marked script can demonstrate deliberate technique without proving any unperformed vocal skill.
