# 25.02 — Observation and imagination

Use specific details to generate possibilities that a broad first impression would miss. Allow ten minutes for observation, twenty for alternatives and ten for comparison. You will make one miniature and trace its invented relationships to details you actually had available.

## Describe before assigning a story

“An ordinary cup” names a category. “A loop-shaped handle shadow reaches the edge of a sheet of paper” describes a relationship that can become material for a scene. Close attention does not require declaring an object beautiful or finding a hidden symbolic meaning. The distinction between observation and interpretation lets imagination remain free without being mistaken for evidence.

Project Zero at Harvard Graduate School of Education discusses observation and interpretation in [See, Think, Wonder, S15](../SOURCES.md#S15). That resource is a conceptual reference only. The eight-detail exercise, transformations and texts here are original teaching material drawn from this curriculum's task; they do not reproduce or adapt its branded routine or worksheet. No effect study or validated assessment is claimed.

Choose a familiar safe object, an accessible image description, a sound or a verbal scene. You do not need every sense. Mark unavailable information rather than filling it with an imagined sensation and calling it observed. Do not watch private behavior, record strangers or infer a person's motives from a gesture. An object and supplied description are enough for this attempt.

## Supplied verbal scene O8

A white cup stands on a table with a window to its left. Its rim appears oval from the described viewpoint. A blue band circles the lower body. The handle points right. A narrow bright reflection runs along the upper-left rim. The handle casts a loop-shaped shadow to the right, ending at the near edge of a sheet of paper. The paper's corner curls upward. No sound, movement, temperature, smell or tactile texture is described.

Spend five minutes attending to the description or an equivalent actual object, without inventing missing observations. The supplied first broad impression is “a plain cup beside paper.” The detail record below extracts what the description actually supplies. Tag these entries **S**, meaning supplied description. In an actual observation, tag what you directly notice **O** and keep your interpretations in a separate column.

| Detail | Available description | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D1 | Rim appears oval from this viewpoint | S; apparent shape, not a claim that the rim is physically oval |
| D2 | Body is white | S; no material or temperature specified |
| D3 | Blue band circles the lower body | S |
| D4 | Handle points right | S |
| D5 | Narrow bright reflection at upper-left rim | S |
| D6 | Handle shadow has a loop shape to the right | S |
| D7 | Shadow ends at the near edge of the paper | S |
| D8 | One paper corner curls upward | S |

Sound, smell, touch and actual movement remain unavailable. “Someone abandoned the cup” is an inference with no supporting history. “The cup is lonely” may be an imaginative premise, but is not an observation. A precise record can include absence of information without becoming incomplete or inferior.

## Transform relationships in three ways

**Alternative 1: cup as a place.** The oval rim becomes a harbor boundary, and the blue band becomes a shoreline visible below it. A traveler on that shoreline can see the bright rim reflection as a narrow opening in the weather. D1, D3 and D5 remain traceable while a household object becomes a navigable place. The change is in scale and possible movement, not simply renaming the cup a castle.

**Alternative 2: shadow as the main character.** The loop-shaped shadow wants to cross onto the paper, but its leading edge stops at the paper boundary. The curled corner becomes a raised threshold that the shadow cannot lift. D6, D7 and D8 now determine an obstacle for an invented agent. The unexpected connection is that a flat boundary can function as a doorway without the cup itself moving.

**Alternative 3: one change in light.** Imagine moving the light source to the cup's right while keeping the cup, right-pointing handle and curled paper corner fixed. The shadow would extend in the other direction under the simplified imagined setup, so the paper would no longer be its destination. D4 and D8 remain, but their relationship to the imagined light and shadow changes. The exact physical shape is not observed or calculated; label the result as an imagined variant, not a new actual observation.

An unsuccessful early possibility was “the cup is a magical castle.” It used a new noun but did not make the oval rim or blue band affect anything. Keep that failed attempt in the record. Alternative 1 revises it by making the rim a boundary, the band a route and the reflection a condition the traveler can respond to. The detail now does work in the imagined situation.

## Make a complete miniature

The supplied choice is Alternative 2. Here is its original six-sentence passage:

> The handle's shadow waited at the edge of the paper.\
> Its loop made a doorway, but the doorway could not step through itself.\
> Ahead, one corner of the sheet lifted like a small roof.\
> The shadow tried to shelter beneath it without leaving the cup behind.\
> Nothing on the table moved.\
> Still, the distance between shade and shelter had become a journey.

The loop, paper edge and raised corner come from D6–D8. Waiting, trying and experiencing a journey are invented. “Nothing on the table moved” is part of this imagined scene; the source description did not supply an actual five-minute movement observation. Keeping that distinction visible prevents a creative sentence from rewriting the observation log.

Compared with “a plain cup beside paper,” the miniature uses an overlooked alignment between shadow and paper. Its specificity does not prove universal originality or that a reader will share its associations. A reader might find the personification too abstract. That response can guide another version while leaving the original observation record intact.

## Produce your observation-to-making record

1. Record the first broad impression, the information source and at least eight concrete details. Use available senses or a descriptive source; mark unavailable channels honestly.
2. Separate interpretations from observations. Preserve source tags so supplied wording does not become a claim that you directly saw or heard something.
3. Make all three alternatives: object as place, shadow as focus, and one changed light or position. Map at least two details into each and describe the changed relationship.
4. Keep one unsuccessful possibility and revise it beyond a noun swap. Choose an alternative and make a six-sentence passage, brief sound sketch or accessible arrangement.
5. Save the miniature and compare it with the first impression. Name one newly noticed connection and identify the invented elements. Then open the [later packet](later-packet.md) to test what happens when a supporting detail changes.

For support, number details and draw simple arrows from two of them to each alternative. For a harder pass, keep the same facts while removing personification, so a spatial or rhythmic relationship must carry the piece. Work privately unless a willing audience would help your purpose. You may stop if the setting becomes uncomfortable; no sensory intensity, touching or performance is required.

An actual record paired with a made miniature can show that particular details affected the work. A list of vivid adjectives alone cannot show transformation, and a polished fictional passage alone cannot establish observation. The claim stays small: richer attention supplied options that were absent from the first broad impression.
