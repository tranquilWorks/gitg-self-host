# 18.04 — Attachment and relational security

Replace one reactive pattern with a direct, bounded request and a realistic agreement. Allow fifteen minutes to prepare, one naturally occurring nonurgent opportunity and ten minutes to review. This role-conditional practice is optional and for suitable voluntary adult relationships. Single life is legitimate; use the complete fiction if you have no appropriate partner or prefer not to attempt it live. It is not attachment diagnosis, therapy or a promise to change a relationship style.

## Notice behavior without assigning a type

A bond can matter deeply and make uncertainty uncomfortable. Begin with the observable cue, then distinguish the story you attach to it, the feeling and the action impulse. A delayed reply is observable. “They are leaving me” is an interpretation unless other evidence supports it. Keeping that distinction does not mean every concern is imagined: broken agreements, deception or threatening conduct are real information.

Pursuing can become repeated contact intended to force reassurance. Distancing can become withdrawal designed to avoid a needed conversation or make the other person chase. Testing creates a hidden task whose correct answer the partner does not know. Controlling restricts another's choices or demands access to prove trust. Idealizing treats a partner as flawless or as the only possible source of security. Fear of abandonment may intensify these impulses, but fear does not authorize them.

The alternative is not suppressing every need. A direct request identifies what would help and permits negotiation: “Could we agree when to check in after your shift?” differs from “If you cared, you would already know.” Consistency means keeping a feasible agreement or communicating a necessary change. Reassurance should be freely meant and bounded, not extracted through escalating demands for passwords, location access or constant availability.

Self-regulation creates room to choose conduct. For an ordinary nonurgent cue, pause briefly in a way that is safe for you, orient to known facts and decide whether a conversation is needed. This is not punishment through silence or an instruction to ignore urgent responsibilities. State a boundary over your own behavior and capacity; do not disguise control of the other person as a boundary.

## Worked example

Lena has not heard from a partner during a known work period. Lena notices the story “I no longer matter” and an urge to send many messages. After a brief pause, Lena waits for a suitable time and asks whether a short evening check-in could work on late-shift days. The partner can agree, propose another arrangement or decline. The request addresses a real wish while allowing the partner's actual capacity to matter. If an agreed check-in is repeatedly missed, Lena can discuss reliability rather than explaining away all concern as personal insecurity.

## Complete fictional nonurgent situation

Adult partners Kai and Morgan have agreed that Morgan will be unavailable during a workshop from 15:00 to 18:00 on 6 October. They have not agreed to a reply before the workshop ends or to a check-in immediately at 18:00. At 16:00 Kai sends an ordinary nonurgent message. By 17:00 there is no reply. No emergency, threat or unexplained disappearance is supplied.

Kai feels anxious, thinks “Morgan is pulling away,” and considers sending five more messages or deliberately ignoring Morgan later. Kai would like a predictable brief connection on workshop days. Kai is available from 19:30 to 20:00; Morgan's evening availability is unknown. A possible request is a ten-minute check-in somewhere within that window, with a different arrangement allowed. No request or agreement exists yet.

## From cue to an explicit agreement

1. Write four separate lines: cue, interpretation, feeling and impulse. Identify which of the six patterns the proposed extra messages or retaliatory silence might express, without diagnosing Kai or Morgan. Output: a behavior account that preserves the agreed unavailability and unknown motive.
2. Choose a brief safe pause and a nonurgent time to communicate. Draft a direct request for a feasible check-in, including room for a counteroffer. State what Kai can do if no arrangement fits, such as making a separate evening plan and revisiting needs later. Output: a request and self-directed boundary, not a hidden test.
3. Save that plan before opening the [later packet](later-packet.md). Record the exact agreement that follows, if any. In real practice, use the actual response rather than the answer you hoped to receive. Output: proposed, counteroffered, agreed or declined, with the timing made explicit.
4. Compare later behavior with the agreement that actually existed. Notice both your own conduct and any kept, changed or missed commitment. Output: a limited review of request, consistency, reassurance and boundaries. One successful conversation does not establish cured anxiety or permanent security; one missed moment does not automatically establish abandonment.

The exercise fails its purpose if an apparently calm request still demands proof through surveillance. Likewise, the learner should not be trained to tolerate every broken promise by regulating harder. A pattern of unmet needs may call for an honest discussion of fit, different support or a relationship decision.

## Support, challenge and real transfer

For support, rehearse the four lines using only the fiction, write instead of speaking, or prepare a short request with a trusted appropriate helper. Do not disclose a partner's private messages to obtain a label. If the pause itself increases distress, choose another safe grounding option or appropriate professional help rather than forcing a technique.

For a harder fictional case, let the first proposed time be impossible. Practice accepting a genuine counteroffer while checking your own availability. Do not deliberately delay messages to provoke a reaction. For real transfer, wait for an ordinary opportunity, make one suitable request and review conduct across subsequent agreed occasions. Evidence of security concerns repeated reliability and felt experience, not a speed score or a permanent identity category.

## Sources and professional boundary

[S11](../SOURCES.md#S11) reports evidence favoring dimensional over categorical descriptions of adult attachment in its samples. It does not validate this exercise or diagnose either case person. The six behavior distinctions are canonical teaching, not six clinical types. [S13](../SOURCES.md#S13) supports individualized safety help. Threats, coercion or abuse require suitable support rather than a better reassurance ritual or compelled mutual discussion.

Consent, health, abuse, and legal concerns require appropriate medical, legal, or safeguarding resources. Preserve private content outside the application. See the [scope map](SCOPE-MAP.md), then attempt the [fresh cases](check-prompts.md); a person's dignity is never determined by attachment language.
