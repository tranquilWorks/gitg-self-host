# 15.07 — Water competence and rescue awareness

## Establish your actual water boundary

Begin on dry land with the fictional lesson record below. Allow twenty-five minutes to distinguish observed skills from missing evidence and choose a safe next learning step. You need only the supplied record and somewhere to write or dictate. This exercise never requires entering water, holding your breath, cold immersion, swimming against a current or rescuing a person. Qualified aquatic instruction is the route to physical skill.

## Competence is a sequence in a setting

Swimming a distance is one part of water competence. Safe entry and resurfacing, floating or treading, changing orientation, reaching a safe exit and getting out also matter. Assistance, equipment, fatigue and the environment affect what a demonstration establishes. The Red Cross describes these components and layers of protection; its skill benchmarks belong in appropriate instruction, not an unsupervised self-test. See S06 in [SOURCES](../SOURCES.md).

Floating supports remaining at the surface; treading is another way to maintain position using movement. Neither label tells you whether a particular learner can do it without assistance in a particular setting. A record should name which was observed, for how long if measured, and with what support. “Comfortable in water” leaves all of that unclear. An instructor may adapt or omit a task and record why; omission is not failure, and assisted performance should remain accurately described.

The environment changes the task. Pool depth and a ladder differ from a bank with no stable exit. Cold water can abruptly disrupt breathing and movement. Moving water adds forces a pool lesson did not assess. RNLI guidance explains cold-water shock and the value of appropriate flotation and learning how to float; this guide does not turn that knowledge into permission to try cold water. See S07. Check conditions, supervision, entry, exit, visibility and activity-appropriate flotation before an outing. A buoyancy toy is not a substitute for a suitable approved lifejacket.

Rescue awareness includes protecting the would-be helper. Someone struggling may not be able to wave or call clearly. Alert a lifeguard or appropriate emergency help promptly. The Red Cross's reach-or-throw principle avoids entering the water, but it still requires a stable safe position, appropriate equipment and relevant instruction. Do not lean over an unstable edge, improvise a pull on a person, or enter because help seems slow. This exercise practices recognizing the boundary and communicating location, not a rescue maneuver.

## Worked example

A record says, “Ari swam eight metres with a flotation aid, then an instructor helped them use the steps.” A friend summarizes that as “Ari swims and can get out.” That removes two material conditions. A faithful summary is “eight metres with the named aid; assisted exit by steps; unsupported exit unassessed.” The next step is an instructor-guided assessment or lesson suited to Ari's needs, not a deeper-water challenge to settle the argument.

## Fictional record and environment cards

No real learner or instructor is represented. There is no hidden expectation that you reproduce these tasks physically.

| Skill | Instructor's fictional observation |
| --- | --- |
| Entry and resurfacing | Controlled entry with support; resurfacing without support not assessed |
| Float or tread | Back float for 20 seconds with an aid; treading not assessed |
| Turn/orient | Turn toward steps after a verbal cue |
| Swim toward safety | Eight metres with the same aid; no other distance assessed |
| Exit | Steps used with instructor assistance; ladder exit not assessed |

Environment A is a staffed lesson pool with an instructor-selected depth, approved assistance, clear steps and an agreed stop signal. Environment B is a cold river with visible current, poor visibility, an unstable bank, no confirmed exit and no lifeguard. Environment C is a leisure pool described by a friend as “easy”; depth, access and supervision are not yet known. An invitation is not an assessment of conditions.

A separate rescue vignette occurs entirely on paper: at a waterfront you observe someone making no forward progress beyond safe reach. You are on stable public ground well away from the edge. A marked help point is behind you. A trained staff member is present there. You have no rescue training or suitable equipment in hand.

## Make the boundary usable

1. Copy the five skill rows and separate “observed with stated support,” “observed without support,” and “not assessed.” Keep partial evidence: a turn after a cue is neither a total absence of skill nor an independent demonstration.
2. For A, B and C, choose an appropriate next action using the information supplied. A supports further instructor-led learning if the instructor confirms suitability; B does not support entry by this learner; C needs clarification before participation. List the missing information for C.
3. Draft two questions for the instructor. One should ask how a task can be safely adapted; the other should ask what evidence would establish the next skill in that environment. Do not request a certificate that silently covers unassessed conditions.
4. Rehearse the waterfront report aloud, with a communication aid or in writing: the location, observed difficulty, where the person was last seen and the need for trained help. In this paper case, delegate to the marked staffed help point; in a real emergency contact local emergency services as appropriate. Do not dial or send anything for rehearsal.
5. Explain why entering, reaching from the unstable bank or trying to pull a partner would exceed this exercise. Say what the reach-or-throw principle does and does not authorize. There is no throwing or pulling drill here.
6. Save a one-paragraph water boundary that names demonstrated support, unassessed skills and environments, and the next qualified learning route. Then open the [later packet](later-packet.md).

## Check the claim, not your courage

Your paper classification and report are actual tasks only if you attempted them. The supplied swim observations remain fictional. Confidence, swimming years ago, booking a lesson and understanding cold-water risk do not establish present physical competence. A lesson can show meaningful partial progress without earning a broad “water safe” label.

For a supported version, use one skill row at a time and ask a helper to read the cards. Keep aids and assistance visible in the record. A harder version changes the pool's usual steps to a ladder; reconsider exit suitability rather than assuming all exits are equivalent.

For real transfer, locate qualified aquatic instruction appropriate to your age, access needs and health circumstances. Discuss the setting, assistance, consent and stop signal before entering. The instructor selects safe tasks and documents entry/resurfacing, float or tread, orientation, swimming and exit as observed, assisted, omitted or still untested. They determine whether any further environment-specific course is suitable. Dryland work remains a useful alternative when water practice is unavailable, but cannot establish demonstrated aquatic skill. Use the [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) and [key](check-answers.md) separately.
