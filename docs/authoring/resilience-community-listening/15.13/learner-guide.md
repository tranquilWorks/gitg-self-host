# 15.13 — Bystander intervention and violence prevention

## Support a targeted person without taking over

Use the invented event scene below. In about twenty-five minutes you will choose among five intervention strategies, rehearse an indirect request for help and respond when the person declines your offer. Nobody needs to act out harassment. Do not confront strangers, stage a threat, film people or disclose an actual incident for this exercise. A useful response considers safety, the targeted person's choices and your own role.

## Five options are a menu, not an escalation ladder

Right To Be describes direct, distract, delegate, document and delay as ways to support someone facing harassment. Direct intervention can increase risk; documentation requires particular attention to whether help is already available and how the targeted person wants material used. See S16 in [SOURCES](../SOURCES.md). The original scene and decisions below are practice materials, not a validated guarantee of violence prevention.

Direct means a brief response to the harmful behavior when circumstances support it; it need not become a debate. Distract shifts attention to create an opening, without requiring the targeted person to perform gratitude or explain themselves. Delegate asks an appropriate willing person to help with a specific task. Document can preserve facts when safe and useful, but recording is not automatically the most helpful action. Delay offers support after the incident when intervention during it was not feasible. More visible action is not inherently better action.

Observe conduct: repeated unwanted comments, ignoring a request to stop, following, blocking movement or threats. Identity, unfamiliarity and a guessed diagnosis are not substitutes for observed behavior. Harassment, abuse and conflict are not interchangeable categories. An ongoing abusive relationship can require specialist support and safety planning, and direct confrontation may increase danger. You do not need to determine someone's entire history to offer bounded support.

Ask rather than assume what the person wants when it is safe to do so. An offer can include a concrete option—company while leaving, help locating staff, or quiet space—but must allow refusal. Consent to one action is not consent to recording, public reporting, police involvement or future contact. In immediate danger, obtain appropriate emergency help as circumstances require. Local safeguarding duties may apply to particular roles or situations; do not promise secrecy you cannot keep or claim a universal reporting rule.

## Worked example

At a public talk, an attendee is repeatedly mocked and moves away. Jules plans to loudly announce, “I’ll protect you; everyone look here.” The targeted attendee would become the center of attention and has not asked Jules to take over. Jules instead identifies the event steward and gives a factual report with a specific request for discreet assistance. When safe, Jules offers the attendee a choice about whether they want company reaching reception. The change is attention to the other person's agency, not merely lowering the volume.

## Complete fictional scene

At a community exhibition, one attendee repeatedly makes unwanted comments about another attendee's appearance. The targeted person says “Please stop,” then moves toward the reception area. You are an ordinary visitor. No weapon, pursuit beyond the described movement or physical assault is supplied. Do not add one to make a dramatic intervention seem necessary.

The level route to reception is clear. A marked event steward stands near the information table, away from the exchange. A quiet staffed waiting area is beside reception. You have no security role. No one has asked you to record. Your willing rehearsal partner may read only neutral role cards: “Steward available,” “Steward unavailable,” “Yes, company would help,” or “No, thank you.” Do not perform insults, aggression, blocking or physical contact.

## Choose before you act

1. Describe the observed behavior and the immediate support opportunity without guessing motives. Note the exits, staff, your role and the targeted person's movement away. Name what would change the situation into an emergency requiring a different response.
2. Give each of the five options a disposition for this scene. Explain why direct confrontation may add risk; how a neutral distraction could create an opening; whom you could delegate to; why recording is not the default; and what delayed support could offer. You do not need to use all five.
3. Choose a primary strategy and one fallback. For delegation, draft a factual request such as “Repeated comments continued after a request to stop, near the display. Could you discreetly check whether the attendee wants help getting to reception?” Avoid demanding punishment based on unverified details.
4. Rehearse that request in a normal voice or accessible communication mode. Then practice a separate offer to the targeted person: “Would you like company to reception, or would you prefer space?” Use the supplied “No, thank you” reply once. Show how you would accept it without following, demanding reasons or seeking praise.
5. Introduce “Steward unavailable.” Choose a remaining proportionate option based on the same facts. Unavailable staff do not compel direct confrontation. You might offer support later from a safe context or locate another legitimate assistance point, leaving uncertainty explicit.
6. Write the documentation boundary: help and safety first; factual minimal notes only when appropriate and lawful; no public sharing or use without the person's permission, subject to applicable duties. No real recording is performed here. Preserve your plan and open the [later packet](later-packet.md).

Your output is a five-option comparison, one rehearsed request, a refusal-respecting response and a fallback. It can show decision quality in a simulation; it cannot establish that you prevented real violence or understood an actual targeted person's wishes.

## Interpret the response honestly

A person accepting company does not prove all harm was resolved. A person declining does not make your offer a failure or grant permission to keep trying. The steward's acknowledgment shows the request was received; it is not proof that the attendee received support. If the person has left, their wishes and outcome may remain unknown. Do not fill that gap with a comforting success story.

For a smaller version, rehearse delegation and accepting “no” before comparing the other options. Use fictional text cards, pauses and communication aids; anyone may stop. A harder paper case removes the quiet area and adds a crowded route. Reevaluate available choices without practicing physical intervention.

For real transfer, learn the event or workplace's legitimate assistance and safeguarding routes during a calm moment. Relevant bystander training can expand your options within its scope. Do not seek an incident to test yourself. In a real event, prioritize safety, avoid exceeding competence and preserve the targeted person's choices where circumstances allow.
