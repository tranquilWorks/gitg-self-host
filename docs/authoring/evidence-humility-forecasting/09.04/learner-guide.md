# 09.04 — Source and evidence evaluation

## What you will produce

An evidence audit of one exact claim, a confidence judgment with reasons, and a decision about what you may responsibly repeat. Allow 35–45 minutes. Use paper, a text editor, dictation, or a willing reader. The fictional source packet below supplies everything required; no subscription, account, or outside search is necessary.

**Canonical scope:** Assess expertise, incentives, methodology, sample quality, replication, uncertainty, and competing explanations.

This practice asks what the evidence warrants, not whether an author is a good person. The background on contextual authority and study limitations is identified as S1 and S2 in SOURCES.md. All packets, calculations, and checks here are original instructional examples. They are not actual studies.

## A usable reference

Start with the claim, not a trust score for the website. Specify the population, comparison, outcome, and time period. An author can have relevant practical expertise yet provide weak evidence for a causal conclusion. A financial interest is a reason to inspect methods and reporting, not proof that the conclusion is false.

**Methodology** concerns how the comparison and measurements were produced. **Sample quality** concerns who and what was observed, who was missing, and whether those observations support the intended generalization. More respondents do not repair a mismatch between a question and the behavior claimed. People in one shared session also do not supply independent repetitions of the whole intervention.

**Replication** requires a new investigation or dataset appropriate to the claim. A second article reporting the first study is not a second study. An independent reader's reanalysis can identify errors, but it does not add new participants. Keep these contributions distinct.

A limitation should change a particular conclusion. Missing an allocation procedure weakens a causal comparison; missing a follow-up limits a durability claim. Neither automatically proves no effect. Good evaluation allows a narrow supported statement inside a broader unsupported headline.

## Different worked example — a reading lamp

A seller says a new lamp improves reading speed. Eight volunteers choose it, read different passages than the comparison group, and say they like it. A newsletter repeats the seller's account. The supplied information supports a report about those volunteers' stated preference, not a measured speed gain. Different passages and self-selection are competing explanations. The newsletter does not add replication. The seller's interest warrants scrutiny but cannot, by itself, establish that the lamp is ineffective.

A useful request is for timed reading on comparable passages with a documented allocation method. Asking for a more impressive biography would not repair the missing measurement.

## Complete packet — the meeting-format headline

All organizations, people, and records below are fictional. Treat supplied counts as accurate for this exercise, but do not fill unreported facts with guesses.

**Card A — advertisement, Day 6.** The facilitator writes: “Our new format doubles actual participation for all members.” A paid training course is offered below the claim. The advertisement links to Report R1.

**Card B — original Report R1, Day 5.** The facilitator has five years of meeting-facilitation experience. The report does not establish training in study design. Fifty-two club members were invited to choose between two sessions on Day 4. Twelve chose the new format; forty chose the old format. There was one session per format, both run by the facilitator. No allocation by chance, baseline participation measure, or later follow-up is reported. Both sessions used this exit question: “During this session, did you feel able to contribute?” It is a yes/no self-report, not a count of actual contributions.

| Format | Selected members | Returned responses | Yes responses | Missing responses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New | 12 | 10 | 10 | 2 |
| Old | 40 | 40 | 20 | 0 |

The two missing new-format responses have no known values or reasons. The report defines its comparison as the proportion answering yes **among returned responses**. Recruitment covers this club only. No other dataset or independent replication is supplied.

**Card C — newsletter, Day 7.** A writer repeats the advertisement and cites R1. The writer collected no observations and states no independent method. This is another publication of the same evidence.

**Card D — volunteer audit, Day 8.** A reader recomputes R1's fractions and points out the two missing forms. The reader has no connection to the paid course but uses exactly R1's data. This provides an independent arithmetic check, not a new intervention study.

### Action 1 — Trace the claim and its support

Preserve the headline. Write two distinct claims beneath it: the descriptive statement R1 can directly address and the causal/general claim the advertisement makes. Fill this record:

`Exact claim | population and time | outcome actually measured | comparison denominator | primary evidence | relevant expertise | incentive | unknowns`

Draw or write the source chain: advertisement, newsletter, and audit back to R1. Count underlying datasets separately from publications. Explain what the facilitator's experience helps establish and what it does not. Do not infer dishonesty, incompetence, or independence of data from a person's role alone.

### Action 2 — Inspect the comparison, not just the headline

Calculate each returned-response percentage and their ratio. Then examine the missing forms: calculate the new group's possible yes fraction among **all selected members** if both missing answers were no, and if both were yes. Label these as missing-response bounds, not confidence intervals or estimates of a causal effect. The actual two answers are still unknown.

For each of the seven canonical dimensions, write a finding and its consequence for the claim:

`Expertise | incentives | methodology | sample quality | replication | uncertainty | competing explanations`

Consider self-selection, the single session per format, and the difference between feeling able to contribute and actually contributing. Give at least two plausible alternatives to the advertised explanation. A plausible alternative is not an established cause either.

Finally separate two kinds of repair. Better reporting could reveal a missing form or clarify recruitment. A new design or new observations would be needed to measure actual contributions or test whether the format caused a change. An exact count of participants cannot substitute for these missing steps.

### Action 3 — Give a bounded judgment and act on it

Use this response form:

`What I may repeat | what I should not repeat | confidence and reasons for this particular claim | important remaining uncertainty | evidence that would change the judgment | information-use decision`

Choose a suitable judgment such as “supported for this narrow descriptive claim,” “uncertain,” “unsupported as stated,” or “contradicted by the supplied record.” Explain it; do not combine unrelated dimensions into a made-up precision score. You may have high confidence that the report lacks a behavior count while remaining uncertain whether the format actually helps.

State the smallest relevant next evidence, not just “more research.” For example, a documented count of contributions would address measurement; a comparable allocation process and repeated sessions would address different design weaknesses. No single repair necessarily resolves all limitations.

Decide whether to share a narrowed statement, seek clarification, or decline to repeat the headline. An optional live route uses an accessible original report on an ordinary topic and preserves the same record. An inaccessible original remains an access limitation; a summary alone must not be called a completed primary-report audit.

## Accessibility and progression

A reader may transcribe the cards and calculations at your direction. Use a calculator, spoken source chain, or one audit dimension at a time. Preserve the denominators and source dependence even when simplifying the layout. Record help without treating access support as an error.

For a harder route, use a paper with several outcomes. Identify which outcome was highlighted, whether its analysis was specified beforehand, and whether multiple reports reuse the same participants. Ask for suitable specialist help when the method exceeds your understanding; mark that limitation instead of manufacturing confidence.

Later, evaluate another low-stakes claim from a different context. Preserve the audit before discussing it with someone. A live audit demonstrates this particular evaluation; it does not establish general resistance to misinformation or scientific expertise.

## Interpreting the result

**Supportive:** The source chain and denominators are correct, measurement and selection limits change the stated conclusion, and a narrower information-use decision follows.

**Mixed:** Incentives are noticed and the percentages are right, but the newsletter is counted as replication or the exit question is treated as a behavior measure.

**Contradictory:** The author is believed solely for prestige, rejected solely for financial interest, or failure to show a causal benefit is declared proof of no benefit.

**Inconclusive:** There is a trust label without the exact claim, original method, denominator, or evidence-based judgment.

Do not use this educational audit as the sole basis for medical, legal, financial, or safety-critical decisions. Choose an ordinary claim within your competence.

**Final review:** Which limitation most changed what you would repeat, and which tempting conclusion is still beyond the evidence?
