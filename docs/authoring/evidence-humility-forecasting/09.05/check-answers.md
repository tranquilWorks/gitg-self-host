# 09.05 — Answer guidance and repairs

Open after saving the update and fresh-check responses. There is no single mandatory confidence word; the claim, evidence, context, and explanation must fit one another.

## Main practice

L1 and L2 show difficulty in the Main room during the observed periods. L3 and L4 show usable attempts by the same reader on the same kind of task in Quiet Q. Those successes contradict the original claim that no room is usable or every trip is wasted. They do not establish that Quiet Q will always be available or manageable, that most possible visits succeed, or that every reader will find it useful.

L5 is a staff report about a usual workshop and possible reservations. It does not establish the cause of L1's interruptions or reserve a future visit. L6 challenges the overgeneralization without denying the difficult attempts. An accurate restatement keeps that distinction.

A reasonable change log is: “I generalized from one Main-room visit to the whole library. Two later Quiet-Q attempts met this reader's criterion. I now consider that room a plausible option for this task, subject to availability, rather than rejecting every afternoon visit. I still lack evidence about other rooms, future days, and durable suitability.” State whether that is a narrower proposition rather than presenting it as a numeric change in one unchanged belief.

A suitable next step is to check a particular future room opening or make another ordinary trial while retaining an alternative. Frustration about the original visit need not disappear. The update corrects an inference, not a person's dignity. The fictional record does not establish that the learner revised a personally meaningful belief.

**Repair:** If you wrote “the library is always fine,” replace that opposite universal claim with the observed room/task scope. If you erased V0, restore it and add the revision as a later entry. If you inferred a confirmed workshop cause, attribute the staff report and preserve the missing historical record.

## Check A

One genuine successful afternoon attempt contradicts “every attempt fails,” even though the later Main-room failure remains real. The evidence supports acknowledging that at least one context worked. It does not support “always suitable.” Retain the room distinction and choose a further Side-room check or another bounded action rather than merging all rooms into one assured property.

The weaker claim “more than half of attempts are unsuitable” is not logically refuted by one success. Evaluating that frequency claim requires a specified collection of attempts, adequate sampling, and awareness of context. The observed attempts in the prompt are not a representative census of future possibilities. Do not change “every” into “usually” after the fact and say nothing changed.

A correction could read: “I said every afternoon attempt would fail. The Side-room attempt is a counterexample. I was too broad; future reliability remains uncertain.” No self-insult or forced apology for the earlier discomfort is needed.

## Check B

After the unsupported request to believe the room will open, Ellis can retain the expectation supported by the current notice. The friend supplies no relevant new evidence. A fair response invites a newer record without attacking the friend.

After the later official correction, the current operational expectation should be updated to scheduled-open, retaining ordinary uncertainty about actual operation. The earlier statement that the first notice scheduled a closure remains historically accurate. What changes is which schedule is current, not what the old document said. Preserve both with their order and provenance.

A suitable acknowledgment is: “I was relying on the earlier closure notice. The facility has since changed it, so I have updated the plan.” This is not a confession that reading the old notice was irrational. A decision to use the room can change accordingly, with a proportionate current access check if needed. Neither notice establishes a guarantee.

**Repair:** If both messages caused the same reversal, identify the source and relevance of each. If you refused the official correction to remain consistent, distinguish consistency in evidence standards from keeping an outdated conclusion. If you rewrote the original record, restore its historically correct content and append the new one.
