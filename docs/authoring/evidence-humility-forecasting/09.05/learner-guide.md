# 09.05 — Epistemic humility

## What you will produce

A versioned belief record: your initial claim and confidence, its assumptions and unknowns, a fair evidence check, and an appended update explaining what changed or why it did not. Use about 15 minutes before inspecting new evidence and 20 minutes afterward. Paper, a voice memo, or a text editor is enough.

**Canonical scope:** State confidence levels, assumptions, unknowns, limitations, and what would change a belief.

The fictional route supplies all evidence. The optional live route uses a meaningful but low-stakes belief of your own. Fictional updating demonstrates the procedure, not that a personally held belief changed. Research background S3 in SOURCES.md is limited support for the concept of recognizing fallibility, not validation of this exercise.

## The distinction that makes humility useful

Humility is not saying “anything could be true,” apologizing for knowing something, or agreeing with the last speaker. It means making your confidence answerable to relevant evidence while acknowledging what you do not know. Strong evidence can justify strong confidence. A demand to concede is not new evidence.

Keep three things separate. The **claim** is what you believe. **Confidence** is how strongly the available evidence supports that claim in your judgment. An **action** also depends on practical consequences, duties, and alternatives. A cautious action does not necessarily mean a low estimated probability; you might avoid even an unlikely large cost.

An **assumption** is a premise your inference relies on, such as treating one visit as representative. An **unknown** is a question the record does not settle, such as whether another room is available. A **limitation** identifies the boundary of your observation or ability to assess it. State them specifically instead of attaching “I could be wrong” to an otherwise unchangeable verdict.

Confidence words are sufficient: low, moderate, or high, each with a reason. These are not calibrated numerical probabilities or measured traits. Do not invent percentages to make an uncertain judgment look scientific.

## Different worked example — the missing blue folder

A person says, “The blue folder is not in either of the two drawers I just checked.” A colleague replies, “It must be somewhere; be open-minded.” That reply provides no observation contradicting the bounded claim. The person can retain it while admitting the folder's location is unknown.

Later, the colleague opens a previously overlooked compartment in the first drawer and shows the folder. Now the inspection's completeness is undermined. An accountable update is: “I thought I had checked both drawers completely. I missed that compartment. The folder is there.” Neither self-insult nor pretending the first statement was never made improves the correction.

Notice that “I did not see it” and “it was not there” are different propositions. Preserve the wording rather than silently retreating to the weaker one after challenge.

## Complete initial packet — an afternoon reading place

All observations concern a fictional reader, Rowan, and fictional North Library. No real library visit or private disclosure is required.

**L1, Tuesday of Week 1, 13:15–13:35:** Rowan tries to read in the Main room. Six audible interruptions occur; Rowan does not finish the planned passage. Rowan reports frustration and concludes: “No room at North Library is usable for my afternoon reading. It is always a waste of a trip.” No other room was inspected. No other afternoon visit is recorded. The task was ordinary quiet reading, not an emergency or essential appointment.

For this practice, a **usable attempt** means Rowan completes the planned 20-minute reading task and reports the setting manageable for that task. It does not mean perfect silence, usefulness to every reader, or access on every future day. A different actual reader may choose a different reasonable criterion before testing it.

Do not open `evidence-update.md` yet. It contains the later observations. Reading them now would turn the initial record into a retrospective reconstruction; record any such exposure honestly.

### Action 1 — Preserve the original and set a fair revision condition

Copy Rowan's original wording as V0. Identify its universal claims and the observation that motivated them. Write the emotion in a separate line without treating it as proof about every room.

Create V1, a bounded working claim worth investigating. You might focus on which room could support the same reading task during weekday afternoons. Do not report that V1 was Rowan's original position: record the change of scope explicitly.

Fill this form before seeing later evidence:

`Version and time/order | exact claim | confidence word and reason | evidence IDs | assumptions | unknowns | limitations | evidence that would increase confidence | evidence that would decrease it | evidence that would leave it unchanged | planned action`

For the original universal claim, ask what a genuine counterexample would look like. For a weaker frequency claim such as “usually unsuitable,” ask what repeated observations would be needed; one successful visit has a different logical role there. Avoid a revision condition that only your preferred answer could satisfy.

### Action 2 — Inspect a fair challenge without turning it into a contest

Save your first record. Now open `evidence-update.md`. List each new observation with room, date, task, and result. Separate the staff member's report from Rowan's own attempts. Do not average unlike settings and call the result a universal property of the whole building.

Restate the strongest relevant challenge to the initial claim in a sentence its proponent could recognize. Also preserve the strongest remaining reason for caution. You are not required to agree with a person, deny frustration, or prove a motive to use their relevant evidence.

For each item, mark whether it addresses the same proposition, a narrower one, or a different question. Explain which assumption it changes. A personal judgment of usability remains about this reader and task; it should not be generalized to everyone with a similar preference or identity.

### Action 3 — Append a change log and a proportionate next step

Keep V0 and V1 readable. Append V2 rather than editing either in place:

`Old claim and confidence | new evidence | revised claim | revised confidence and reason | scope changed or same proposition? | what remains unresolved | next action | next review condition`

Use plain language such as: “I previously thought __ because __. The new observation __ changes __. I now think __ within __. I still do not know __.” Fill the blanks with this packet's evidence rather than a generic admission of fallibility.

A correct update may reject a universal claim, narrow the context, or retain a supported statement. It need not jump to the opposite universal claim. If you change the proposition, do not report the two confidence words as though they were measurements of the same claim.

Choose an action within your authority, such as another appropriate trial or asking about room availability. Describe why it fits the remaining uncertainty. A precaution can remain useful even after your belief changes. If you communicated an overstatement, draft a brief correction to the same relevant audience without unnecessary embarrassment or public disclosure.

## Fresh checks and feedback

Save responses to the two cases in `check-prompts.md` before opening `check-answers.md`. The checks include an appropriate revision and an appropriate refusal to revise under unsupported pressure. Both are part of the skill. There is no reward for changing your mind merely to satisfy the exercise.

## A complete live-transfer route

Choose one ordinary belief whose accuracy matters to a reversible choice you actually face: a place is unsuitable for a particular task, a routine never works at a particular time, or a service is unavailable under stated conditions. Avoid claims about another person's private motives, identity, diagnosis, or experience of harm.

Record V0 before seeking new information. Set a safe discriminating check, an observation window, and a return point. Inspect an accessible current record or make an ordinary authorized observation. Preserve contradictory as well as supportive evidence. At the return point, append your update even when no new evidence arrived; in that case mark the test unresolved rather than inventing an event.

This route can support the canonical indicator of updating a meaningful belief when relevant evidence actually warrants change. A fictional exercise or the words “I am open-minded” alone cannot establish that indicator. Do not provoke conflict or make an unnecessary commitment to obtain evidence.

## Accessibility and progression

Use a voice recording for each version, short claim cards, or a reader who preserves your exact wording. Dates may be replaced by an unambiguous sequence when precise timestamps are unnecessary. Keep sufficient context to distinguish the visits. A helper can transcribe without deciding your confidence.

For a harder route, inspect evidence that changes one assumption but leaves the main judgment reasonable. Explain both parts without claiming that every new fact must produce a reversal. Later compare two actual belief records for consistency in evidence standards, not just how often you conceded.

## Interpreting the result

**Supportive:** The original remains visible, relevant evidence changes a stated assumption, the revision is proportionate, and the new action fits the remaining uncertainty.

**Mixed:** The new conclusion is reasonable but the previous wording is overwritten, or a change of scope is hidden inside a claimed confidence update.

**Contradictory:** Social pressure substitutes for evidence, a person is humiliated into concession, or humility is used to demand equal weight for unsupported and well-supported claims.

**Inconclusive:** The record says “I could be wrong” without an assumption, revision condition, evidence check, or accountable update.

**Final review:** What changed your judgment, what did not, and how could another reader distinguish your update from politeness or hindsight?
