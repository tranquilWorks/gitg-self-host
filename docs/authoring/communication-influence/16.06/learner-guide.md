# 16.06 — Change the message for the task, keep the facts

Audience adaptation changes what you emphasize and how people receive it. It must not change material facts, conceal a burden or invent a person's needs from their age, role, language or culture. In this practice you prepare two messages about one fictional room change and test whether their recipients can take different next actions. Allow twenty minutes to write, two short willing reader checks and ten minutes to repair a mismatch.

## Start with the decision each reader faces

A visitor needs to find the event. A setup volunteer needs to move the right items at the right time. Giving both the same long operational note may hide the visitor's route; giving both the same short invitation may leave the volunteer without responsibilities. GSA's audience guidance recommends learning what users need and separating material for distinct audiences [S06](../SOURCES.md#s06). That supports task-based adaptation, not an assumption that one group is less intelligent.

Use a stable fact list before drafting. Then ask what the audience already knows, what they need to do and what could make the channel unusable. Language, detail, medium and timing work together: an urgent text may fit a last-minute change, while a long image-only poster may not be readable or available on the journey. Essential information should remain usable with relevant access supports; W3C guidance discusses advance materials and accessible presentation methods [S07](../SOURCES.md#s07). It is not proof that a particular message worked for a particular person.

Emotion and urgency also need calibration. A routine room change does not justify “Emergency!” A person who has already traveled to the old location may need acknowledgment of the inconvenience and a clear route. Culture and role can affect greeting, formality and consultation expectations, but ask or use known local guidance instead of performing a stereotype. Keep the practical decision visible even when a respectful introduction is appropriate.

## Worked example: the same delayed collection

A hobby organizer knows that finished clay pieces will be ready Monday, not Friday; collection remains at the same desk. Visitors need the new day and reassurance they need not make a wasted trip. Desk volunteers need to replace the Friday label and use Monday in replies. A visitor version can begin, “Please collect your finished piece from the usual desk on Monday; it won't be ready Friday.” A volunteer version adds who changes the sign and when.

Neither version should invent opening hours or guarantee that every visitor received the update. A polite thank-you from a volunteer does not prove the label was replaced. A visitor who says “I'll come Friday afternoon instead” reveals a comprehension problem despite a friendly response. Repair the date prominence and check the affected action again.

## Your fact card: Saturday workshop

The fictional workshop moves from Room A to Room C. Saturday's start remains 10:00. The east entrance is step-free and leads to Room C along the signed ground-floor corridor; the west entrance has stairs. Organizer Neri will be at the east entrance from 09:40 to 10:10 to help. The setup team may enter Room C at 09:15 and must finish by 09:40. Team member Sol has agreed to move the sign from Room A; team member Kit has agreed to bring two boxes of paper materials. No other equipment or transport is promised. The organizer has approved sending a room-change notice; this fictional approval authorizes no real-world message.

Visitor profile: a first-time attendee is reading on a phone and wants to know where to arrive. Setup profile: two returning volunteers know the building but need timing and responsibilities. These profiles describe task knowledge, not demographic traits. Translation or another format can be requested without a diagnosis or explanation.

1. **Freeze the facts.** List room, day, unchanged start, usable entrance, help route and setup responsibilities separately. Mark what is unknown, such as parking or additional equipment. Output: a fact card that can be compared with both drafts.
2. **Write for each next action.** Produce a visitor message and a setup message. Keep the visitor's arrival route prominent and the team's access window and named tasks explicit. Choose a medium and send time for each; explain why these fit the task. Do not bury the step-free route or remove it because volunteers know the building. Output: two complete messages with the same material facts where relevant.
3. **Audit adaptation dimensions.** For each version, inspect language, amount of detail, medium, timing, emotional tone, local formality, role and urgency. Name one thing changed for a reason and one assumption you still need to check. Replace unsupported shorthand such as “the usual accessible way.” Output: a brief adaptation rationale, not a personality profile of the readers.
4. **Test an action, then use the [later packet](later-packet.md).** Ask two willing readers to act as the respective audiences. Before coaching them, ask the visitor where and when they would arrive and how they would seek help; ask the team who does what and by when. A role-play checks the message in rehearsal, not real event participation. Output: actual reader answers if available, each mismatch, corrected copy and retest status.

## Keep the limits of a successful version

You may find both messages understandable to your two readers and still have unknowns about translation, device access or delivery to other recipients. Do not infer universal accessibility. If a channel cannot carry essential information, change or supplement it rather than asking the recipient to work harder. A message being sent, opened, understood and acted upon are separate states.

For support, use a short heading plus three action lines, an accessible text version and more processing time. Have a willing reader identify unexplained abbreviations. For a harder attempt, the room changes again after some visitors have departed. Decide who needs a timely update, where an authorized sign is needed and what cannot be assumed about receipt. Do not solve the problem by merely rewriting a document no one will see.

For real transfer, choose one authorized low-stakes notice with two materially different audiences. Confirm the facts with its owner, obtain any needed permission to distribute, and test the actual recipient tasks. Record the version used and remaining reach/access gaps. This source exercise itself sends nothing. Finish the [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) by asking what a reader could actually do, rather than which version sounds most polished.
