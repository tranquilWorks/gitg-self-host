# 16.07 — Give useful feedback and do something with what you receive

Feedback connects an observed feature to its practical effect and a possible improvement. A developmental conversation also gives the recipient room to explain, correct the observation, choose a response and review a later attempt. This practice has two directions: you give feedback on a fictional invitation and receive feedback on your own revision. Allow fifteen minutes to prepare, a short willing exchange and ten minutes to revise. Use a harmless artifact, not a surprise appraisal of someone's personality or a disciplinary matter.

## Observation, effect and proposal are different claims

“The invitation says ‘usual place’” is an observable feature. “A first-time visitor may not know where to go” explains a plausible effect; if a reader actually went to the wrong place, report that narrower observed event. “Use a large map” is one possible solution, not a fact proved by the problem. A text address or clear room name may solve the same issue more accessibly.

A useful comment names what should continue as specifically as what should change. “The reply deadline is easy to find; keep it beside the response route” identifies a working feature. Generic praise followed by a hidden criticism can leave the person unsure which part matters. Timeliness means the feedback arrives while the recipient can use it and is welcome; it does not justify public interruption or constant monitoring.

MIT's teaching activity distinguishes description from evaluation and asks the recipient to identify the behavior, evidence, effect and request [S08](../SOURCES.md#s08). This companion uses an original invitation case and permits accepting, adapting or declining a proposed fix. It does not require scripted feelings, public disclosure or role-playing a distressing real conflict.

## Worked example: a useful correction to your correction

A reviewer says, “The activity sheet is confusing; make it more colorful.” The author asks which task was difficult. The reviewer explains they could not tell whether to submit one answer or both. The author restates the actual problem, then changes the instruction to “Choose one of the two questions and submit one answer.” They decline the color suggestion because it does not resolve the ambiguity and would not help a monochrome printout.

The reviewer tries again and correctly identifies one answer. That supports the specific repair in that attempt; it does not validate every instruction on the sheet. Receiving feedback well did not require agreeing with the first solution or displaying gratitude on command. It required understanding the observation, responding with reasons and checking the relevant result.

## Your artifact: the invitation

Here is the complete fictional first draft: “Making afternoon. Saturday 7 November, 14:00–15:30. Come to the usual room. Beginners welcome; bring the necessary things. Reply to Neri by Thursday 5 November at 18:00 if you want a place.” The approved facts are: Room C in the community building; east step-free entrance; paper and pencils supplied; no purchase required; participants may bring an optional example of their own work; Neri accepts replies through the group's existing message thread. This is a local fictional exercise, not an actual event notice.

A willing partner can act as the author or reviewer. Agree to focus only on whether a first-time participant can find the session and know what to bring. Do not evaluate graphic style, the author's diligence or unrelated writing ability. If working alone, preserve the distinction between self-review and an interpersonal feedback exchange.

1. **Give one bounded piece of feedback.** Identify a strength worth keeping and a specific ambiguous phrase within the agreed scope. Explain the task consequence, ask what the author intended and propose one change. Keep your proposed solution separate from the observed defect. Output: a comment someone could act on without inferring a character judgment.
2. **Produce your own revised invitation.** Use the approved facts and no invented requirements. Preserve date, start/end, reply deadline and response route while clarifying location and supplies. Give it to a willing reader and ask what they would do to attend. Output: the actual revision, not merely a plan to “be clearer.”
3. **Receive criticism before defending.** Ask for one specific difficulty. Restate the observation and effect; if the criticism is vague, request an example. Decide whether to accept, adapt or decline the proposed fix and explain the decision against the task. Do not demand positive feedback to offset criticism or agree to an inaccurate claim simply to appear receptive. Output: feedback as received, your understanding and your reasoned disposition.
4. **Use the [later packet](later-packet.md) and review.** Apply a justified change, then ask the same reader to retry the original attendance task where possible. Record what should continue, what changed and what will be reviewed when. Output: before/after artifacts and a retest result or explicit pending status.

## Receptivity includes limits

You may pause if flooded or ask to continue in writing. You may refuse abuse, public humiliation or an out-of-scope critique. A proportionate boundary can preserve a useful review: “I can discuss the invitation wording; I won't continue with personal insults.” This exercise is for voluntary peers and cannot replace formal responsibilities where employment, safeguarding or rights are at issue.

For support, ask the reviewer to mark one exact phrase and describe one attempted action. Use a written comment, accessible format or additional processing time. A person who uses direct language need not add a performance of warmth, and a person who prefers a gentle opening can keep it while making the observation specific.

For a harder attempt, two reviewers propose incompatible solutions. Compare the tasks they were trying to perform, test a version that addresses the actual problems and retain any tradeoff. Do not average suggestions blindly or choose the more senior person's preference as evidence. Explain why a proposed solution was declined without dismissing the experience that prompted it.

For real transfer, obtain permission to review one low-stakes artifact while it can still be revised. Agree the feature and review point, make the actual change and invite a task-based retry. Record the artifact and the other person's actual response separately from self-report. Fictional model feedback cannot supply a second independent evidence type or show that an interpersonal conversation occurred. The [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) focus on criticism that contains useful information even when its proposed solution is poor.
