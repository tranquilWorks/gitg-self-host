# 16.03 — Notice a cue, then check what it means

Nonverbal communication offers information about an interaction, but a movement is not a transparent label for an inner state. This companion helps you describe cues cautiously, adjust your own delivery and accept direct clarification. It accompanies the frozen legacy PracticeEmotionalCueDetection practice. Its existing actions, evidence fields, ten-day practice, seven-day follow-up, completion and replay stay unchanged. Use only the app's original fields if undertaking that practice; this document adds no score, completion rule or surveillance task.

## Read the situation without claiming a hidden answer

Posture can change with fatigue or furniture; a facial movement may have several meanings; looking away may help someone think. Distance has practical and interpersonal functions. Gesture, pace and tone can make a message easier or harder to receive. None of these observations alone proves anger, lying, attraction, competence or consent. A review by Barrett and colleagues describes substantial variation in how facial movements relate to emotion [S02](../SOURCES.md#s02). Its focus is facial inference, not a universal theory covering every gesture, voice or culture.

Use three levels. First describe what was available to you: “The answer became shorter after the schedule question.” Then form tentative explanations: the question was unwelcome, the person was distracted, or they had little time. Finally identify a welcome clarification that can change what you do: “Would it help to pause this planning, or is now still okay?” This is narrower than asking someone to explain their whole emotional life. A refusal to answer leaves the interpretation unknown; it does not confirm the most worrying hypothesis.

Context includes what the person has chosen to tell you, the setting, access needs, timing and the relationship. It does not license replacing one stereotype with another: “People from that culture avoid eye contact” is still an unsupported inference about this person. You need no eye-contact quota, face recording, emotion-recognition software or covert test. Report only cues you actually had access to, and mark the rest unobserved.

## Worked example: the doorway

During an ordinary planning exchange, Ren moves farther from a noisy doorway and turns one shoulder toward the hall. Jo first thinks Ren wants to leave. Jo notices their own voice has become louder over the noise and asks, “Would a quieter spot or a pause work better?” Ren says, “A quieter place, please; I still want to finish the plan.” The words contradict the leaving interpretation and identify a practical adjustment. They do not prove exactly which feeling Ren had or why their shoulder turned.

Jo moves only after agreement, lowers volume enough for the quieter setting and checks audibility. This is using one's own distance, orientation and tone to support access. It is not mirroring movements to manufacture trust. A smooth interaction cannot retroactively turn the initial guess into a correct reading.

## Your case: a weekend plan

Allow fifteen minutes for the paper attempt and, only when welcome, a brief ordinary interaction. The fictional observation is this: while discussing a weekend hobby session, Mika usually gives full sentences. When you ask about starting earlier, Mika answers “Maybe,” pauses, glances toward a wall clock and shifts a chair backward. You notice you have leaned forward and sped up while listing reasons. The room is warm and another activity starts soon. You do not know Mika's preference, schedule or feelings. No recording is available or needed.

1. **Inventory the available cues.** Describe posture, face, optional gaze, distance, gesture, pace and tone in separate short rows. Only some are supplied here. Mark facial movement and tone unobserved rather than inventing a frown or sharp voice. Include the clock glance and your own lean and faster pace. Output: observation rows that another reader can distinguish from interpretation.
2. **Keep alternatives alive.** Write at least two explanations, including one unrelated to you. For each, name an answer that would count against it. “Mika is upset, even if they deny it” cannot be corrected and is unsuitable. Note what you actually need to decide: continue, slow down, defer or use another medium. Output: tentative hypotheses with disconfirming information.
3. **Change your contribution and ask once.** Rehearse settling at an agreed comfortable distance, allowing time and offering a neutral question. Do not touch, block an exit, insist on eye contact or imitate a mannerism. Accept “I don't want to discuss it.” Output: a short clarification and controlled adjustment; a solo rehearsal stays a rehearsal.
4. **Compare with the [later packet](later-packet.md).** Preserve your original interpretation and write what the fictional reply supports, contradicts and leaves unknown. Do not score success by the number of emotions guessed. Output: an update with at least one retained unknown.

## Follow-up and transfer

If a real welcome exchange occurs within the existing practice, record only a minimal observation and actual clarification permitted by its original evidence fields. Within the existing seven-day follow-up, compare later information or a welcome correction with the initial interpretation. Lack of later information means inconclusive, not silently correct. Preserve the original ten-day practice and seven-day follow-up rather than treating this short paper exercise as a replacement.

For support, use text to ask about timing or audibility and omit visual cues entirely when unavailable. A blind, low-vision, nonspeaking or remote participant need not simulate access they do not have. A person may prefer more distance, a quieter place, slower pacing or no adjustment; ask rather than assigning needs from a diagnosis. Do not make them justify an accommodation with private history.

For a harder attempt, words and cues appear inconsistent: someone says the time is fine while repeatedly checking the door. Offer a practical exit or later option and accept the expressed choice unless a separate concrete safety concern requires another response. You cannot settle the mismatch through more confident mind-reading. For real transfer, use one low-stakes already welcome conversation and look for whether your adjustment helped the actual task. Do not practice on disciplinary interviews, suspected deception, abuse assessment or a stranger unaware of observation.

The [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) ask you to retain uncertainty under pressure. The desired progress is responsible use of cues and clarification, including correction of your own first guess.
