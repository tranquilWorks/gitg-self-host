# 16.05 — Imagine a perspective, then let the person correct it

Perspective taking can help you generate possibilities beyond your own preferred explanation. Empathic accuracy requires checking those possibilities against information from the person. This practice asks you to represent another view fairly, revise it when contradicted and keep disagreement visible. Use an ordinary outing or scheduling choice, with fifteen minutes to prepare and one welcome clarification. It is not a method for diagnosing motives, extracting private history or deciding whether harm was deserved.

## Fair representation is revisable

Suppose you prefer a fixed plan and another person wants flexibility. “They don't value my time” reduces a practical difference to a moral story. “They may not know their schedule yet” is a hypothesis that opens a question. It is still a guess. A warm tone, a detailed imagined biography or confidence that you are compassionate does not turn it into knowledge.

Eyal, Steffel and Epley's experiments distinguish imagining a perspective from obtaining new information through conversation [S05](../SOURCES.md#s05). The author-hosted abstract reports that imagined perspective taking did not consistently improve accuracy across their tasks, while direct conversation helped in a final experiment. This is a reason to check assumptions, not a claim that every report is complete or that one conversation makes you accurate in all relationships.

Use three headings: observed, inferred and only the person can report. Then separate what they report from your interpretation of that report. If a person says they need a later start, you know the stated constraint. You may still not know the reason, whether it will change or what feeling accompanies it. People can be uncertain, change their minds or decline to explain. A useful representation can preserve those limits while identifying the practical choice.

## Worked example: a quieter café

Dee suggests a quieter café after Arlo proposes a busy market. Arlo imagines Dee dislikes markets and might be anxious about crowds. Instead of presenting either as truth, Arlo asks, “What matters most about the quieter option for this visit?” Dee says, “I want to hear the conversation, and I have only forty minutes. I actually enjoy markets when we have longer.”

Arlo's revised account is: “For this meeting, Dee wants an easy conversation within forty minutes. A market isn't ruled out generally, but it doesn't serve this visit as well.” Arlo can still prefer the market and propose another day. Accuracy here involves keeping the time and conversation priorities, correcting the broad dislike inference and not adding an unreported diagnosis. The account remains an illustrative fictional one.

## Your case: decide now or later?

You and Morgan are choosing a Saturday outing. You want to reserve the 10:00 museum entry by Thursday because there are limited places. Morgan writes, “Can we decide on Saturday morning?” The museum also offers untimed entry later in the day, but availability is not guaranteed. A free park walk requires no reservation. You can attend either option but need to finish by 14:00. Morgan's schedule, preferences and reasons are unknown. You have not been authorized to buy a ticket for them.

1. **Describe the difference without moralizing.** Put the exact request and known ticket conditions under observed. Put your guesses about spontaneity, cost, energy or other commitments under inferred. Under only-the-person-can-report, list which preference or constraint would help choose. Output: three short columns, with your own end time included as your constraint rather than Morgan's motive.
2. **Generate competing reasonable accounts.** Write at least two explanations under which Morgan is trying to meet a legitimate need, including one that differs from your own preference. For each, name information that would weaken it. Do not make “Morgan just doesn't care” the default that all refusals supposedly confirm. Output: two tentative accounts and a genuinely open question.
3. **Ask for the practical perspective with permission.** Rehearse a question about what matters in waiting and which option would be workable. Let Morgan decline or answer only at the level of logistics. Avoid “Isn't this just your fear of commitment?” or questions designed to secure agreement with your preferred plan. Output: a clarification that could change your choice, including what you will do if no answer comes.
4. **Open the [later packet](later-packet.md).** Compare Morgan's supplied report with each hypothesis. Write a two-sentence representation that Morgan could correct, then name your remaining disagreement or need. Output: supported, contradicted and unknown claims plus a practical next step that does not purchase or commit on their behalf.

## Respect is compatible with a boundary

A fair representation does not require giving up your own end time, accepting every proposal or explaining away harmful conduct. It requires stating the other position in terms consistent with their report before deciding what you can do. If the options cannot meet both people's constraints, separate plans may be the honest outcome. You can acknowledge a reason without endorsing a demand.

For support, use a short written exchange, an interpreter or a prepared “I thought / you said / I now understand” table. Ask only about the practical issue, not intimate background. If no partner is available, use this case and describe the result as hypothesis revision from supplied information. Delayed self-reflection cannot substitute for the other person's report.

For a harder attempt, Morgan gives a new constraint after your summary. Preserve the earlier and later reports with their timing; ask what governs the current decision rather than accusing them of inconsistency. A real contradiction can remain unresolved. Do not claim certainty about whether the change reflects forgetfulness, new information or concealment.

For real transfer, choose one safe, welcome difference of view within a week. Offer your short representation and invite correction without demanding praise for your empathy. Keep the actual correction, the revised account and the decision separate. A person saying “close, but…” may provide stronger learning material than a vague agreeable response. Do not retain sensitive reasons when the practical constraint is sufficient.

Use the [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) to test whether you can leave an unknown unknown. Your aim is an account that another person can recognize and revise, not the feeling that you have entered their mind.
