# 16.09 — Deliver a talk someone can use

A presentation succeeds at its purpose when an audience can follow the intended idea or action, including the part that was initially unclear. Slides, rehearsal time and reduced nerves are useful inputs, not evidence of comprehension. Here you prepare a three-minute explanation of a fictional borrowing shelf, rehearse twice, deliver to a willing audience and revise one observed weakness. Allow twenty minutes to prepare, two actual three-minute rehearsals, delivery and ten minutes to repair a section.

## Structure around the audience's next action

Choose one purpose: a first-time user should know how to borrow and return an item and where to ask when something is missing. Begin by giving a reason to care, then explain the sequence with a concrete example, and close by restating the action and help route. Transitions tell the audience why the next part follows. A list of details without those connections can be accurate but hard to use.

MIT's presentation guidance treats rehearsal as a way to revise content and transitions, not simply memorize a script [S11](../SOURCES.md#s11). W3C's event guidance supports accessible advance material and conveying essential information beyond a single sensory channel [S07](../SOURCES.md#s07). This companion does not require standing, eye contact, a particular accent, a loud expressive style or unaided speech. Composure includes pausing, using notes and saying when an answer must be checked.

Audience adaptation during delivery can mean slowing at a confusing step, describing a visual, accepting a written question or shortening an aside. Do not infer understanding from nods. You need an actual response to a task, such as the listener explaining where a returned item goes. A question can reveal a missing assumption rather than a weakness in the questioner.

## Worked example: an unclear return step

A speaker says, “Put it back where you found it and update the card.” In rehearsal they realize the return area is different from the borrowing shelf. They revise: “Place returned items in the tray labeled RETURNS, then mark the return column on your borrowing row. A steward will put the item back on the shelf.” The transition now explains why the tray exists: it gives the steward a chance to check the item.

During delivery, a listener asks whether damaged items also go in that tray. If the rules do not specify a damage process, a composed answer is, “That isn't covered on this card; please check with the steward before returning a damaged item.” Inventing a fine or repair policy to sound decisive would undermine the explanation. The example is a rehearsal model, not an actual audience result.

## Your complete fictional shelf rules

Only items with a green BORROW label may be borrowed. Read the item's own use instructions; this practice teaches the shelf process, not safe operation of equipment. Before taking an item, write the item code, your chosen borrower label and planned return day on the paper log. Do not add private contact details. Return it to the RETURNS tray and mark the return column. Steward Neri checks the tray and puts items back. If an item is missing, ask Neri through the existing group message thread. If you cannot return it on the planned day, contact Neri to discuss a new plan; no extension is automatic. The rules specify no fine, damage procedure or ability to reserve an item.

Use one page or at most three simple slides. No slide file or public upload is necessary. Keep essential instructions available as text or spoken/signed description as appropriate. The audience may be one willing person. If none is available, complete preparation and mark audience delivery and response unattempted.

1. **Build the three-minute route.** Write the intended audience action, an opening, the borrowing/return sequence, a missing-item route and a close. Include a concrete green-labeled item example. Remove details that do not serve the action. Output: a complete outline or script and any accessible support page.
2. **Rehearse twice in the actual delivery form.** On pass one, record the actual duration and where sequence or timing breaks. On pass two, edit excess material rather than accelerating everything, describe essential visuals and pause after a key instruction. Insert “What if I cannot return it on time?” and answer from the supplied rules. Output: two actual rehearsal durations, a change between them and an uncertainty response. Imagining delivery is not a timed rehearsal.
3. **Deliver and hear a question.** With consent, present to the audience and invite a question through their usable channel. Let the question finish, clarify if necessary, then answer within the known rules. Do not record anyone without agreement. Output: delivery status and an actual question/response, or explicit pending status.
4. **Test and repair comprehension.** Ask the listener to explain borrowing, return location and missing-item help in their own words before you supply the answer. Identify one observed weakness, revise that section and redeliver it. Then open the [later packet](later-packet.md) for the separate fictional outcome. Output: audience response, targeted repair and retest result or remaining gap.

## Transfer the method, not a performance ideal

For support, use seated delivery, notes, AAC, captions, interpretation, recorded/asynchronous delivery or a longer response window. An asynchronous audience must actually access and respond before it supplies audience evidence. The three-minute target is a practice constraint, not a universal pace requirement; agree a reasonable accommodation and record it. Do not penalize the access support as evidence of poor speaking.

For a harder attempt, the visual aid fails. Explain the route from your outline and offer the essential text afterward in a usable way. Alternatively, handle a question whose answer is genuinely absent from the rules. State the limit, name who can verify it and keep the unresolved answer visible instead of filling the silence with invented policy.

For real transfer, explain one harmless process you understand and are authorized to describe. Verify its current rules, rehearse, deliver and ask for a task-based account. Compare the same section before and after revision. Your improved confidence may matter to you, but the separate question is what the audience could understand and use. The [fresh checks](check-prompts.md) help keep those outcomes separate.
